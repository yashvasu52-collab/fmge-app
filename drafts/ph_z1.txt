@ Pharmacology ~ ADRs, enzyme inducers/inhibitors ~ PH_AEI ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Adverse Drug Effects
E ~ Which type of adverse drug reaction is dose-dependent, predictable from the drug's pharmacology and accounts for most reactions in practice? ~ Type A, augmented ~ Type B, bizarre ~ Type C, chronic ~ Type D, delayed ~ A ~ Type A reactions are managed by dose reduction whereas type B idiosyncratic reactions require withdrawal, and this distinction determines whether the drug can ever be used again in that patient.
E ~ Which reporting system in India collects spontaneous reports of suspected adverse drug reactions? ~ The Pharmacovigilance Programme of India, coordinated by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission ~ The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation trials registry ~ The Clinical Trials Registry India ~ The Drug Controller General of India licensing database ~ A ~ Spontaneous reporting detects rare and delayed reactions that trials are too small and too short to find, which is why post-marketing surveillance is a distinct phase of drug evaluation.
M ~ A patient stable on warfarin is started on rifampicin and the international normalised ratio falls. The mechanism and the time course are: ~ Induction of CYP2C9 with the effect developing over days to weeks as new enzyme is synthesised ~ Inhibition of CYP2C9 with an immediate effect ~ Displacement from plasma protein with an immediate effect ~ Reduced absorption of warfarin ~ A ~ Induction requires new protein synthesis so both the onset and the offset are gradual, which is why the dose must be adjusted again when the inducer is stopped.
M ~ A patient on a statin develops myopathy after starting clarithromycin. The mechanism is: ~ Inhibition of CYP3A4 raising statin concentrations ~ Induction of CYP3A4 ~ Displacement from albumin ~ Reduced renal clearance ~ A ~ Enzyme inhibition is immediate because it is competitive, and the risk is greatest with simvastatin and atorvastatin while pravastatin and rosuvastatin are less CYP3A4 dependent.
H ~ Why does a drug with a narrow therapeutic index and a high hepatic extraction ratio show the greatest change in effect when an enzyme inhibitor is added? ~ First-pass metabolism is a large fraction of the dose, so inhibiting it markedly raises bioavailability ~ Renal clearance dominates such drugs ~ Protein binding changes ~ Volume of distribution falls ~ A ~ This is why oral drugs such as verapamil, midazolam and simvastatin are so susceptible to interactions with grapefruit juice and azole antifungals.
H ~ Why does a drug that causes a type B idiosyncratic reaction usually escape detection in phase III trials? ~ Reactions occurring in fewer than 1 in 1000 patients require far more exposures than a trial enrols ~ Trials exclude susceptible patients ~ The reaction only occurs after 10 years ~ Trials do not record adverse events ~ A ~ The rule of three states that to be reasonably confident of seeing at least one event of a given frequency about three times its reciprocal must be exposed, which is why post-marketing surveillance is indispensable.
@ Pharmacology ~ Adrenergic agonists/antagonists ~ PH_AAA ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Adrenergic System
E ~ Which receptor mediates bronchodilatation, and which drug is selective for it? ~ Beta-2 receptor, targeted by salbutamol ~ Beta-1 receptor, targeted by dobutamine ~ Alpha-1 receptor, targeted by phenylephrine ~ Alpha-2 receptor, targeted by clonidine ~ A ~ Beta-2 selectivity is relative and is lost at high dose, which is why tremor, tachycardia and hypokalaemia appear when nebulised doses are escalated in acute asthma.
E ~ Which drug is a selective alpha-1 antagonist used in benign prostatic hyperplasia with relative uroselectivity? ~ Tamsulosin ~ Prazosin ~ Phenoxybenzamine ~ Propranolol ~ A ~ Its preference for the alpha-1A subtype in prostatic smooth muscle causes less postural hypotension than prazosin, and the first-dose phenomenon is the classic hazard of the non-selective agents.
E ~ Which effect explains why adrenaline is preferred to noradrenaline in anaphylaxis? ~ It combines alpha-1 vasoconstriction with beta-2 bronchodilatation and mast cell stabilisation ~ It is a pure alpha agonist ~ It has a longer half-life ~ It is given orally ~ A ~ Intramuscular injection into the anterolateral thigh gives faster and more reliable absorption than subcutaneous, and the dose is 0.5 mg of the 1 in 1000 solution in an adult.
M ~ Which effect of a non-selective beta blocker explains its danger in a diabetic on insulin? ~ It masks the adrenergic warning symptoms of hypoglycaemia and impairs glycogenolytic recovery ~ It causes hyperglycaemia only ~ It increases insulin secretion ~ It accelerates insulin clearance ~ A ~ Sweating remains because it is cholinergically mediated, so it becomes the only warning symptom, and cardioselective agents are preferred where a beta blocker is needed.
M ~ Which paradoxical effect follows the use of adrenaline in a patient fully treated with a non-selective beta blocker? ~ A rise in blood pressure with reflex bradycardia from unopposed alpha stimulation ~ Profound hypotension ~ Tachycardia with hypotension ~ No change ~ A ~ The same principle explains why alpha blockade must precede beta blockade in phaeochromocytoma, and why beta blockers are avoided in cocaine toxicity.
M ~ Which drug acts as an indirectly acting sympathomimetic and shows tachyphylaxis with repeated dosing? ~ Ephedrine, through displacement of stored noradrenaline ~ Phenylephrine, through direct alpha stimulation ~ Isoprenaline, through direct beta stimulation ~ Dobutamine, through beta-1 stimulation ~ A ~ Depletion of releasable stores together with receptor desensitisation explains the rapid loss of effect, in contrast to the slow tolerance seen with directly acting agents.
H ~ Why does a patient on a monoamine oxidase inhibitor develop a hypertensive crisis after eating mature cheese? ~ Tyramine escapes gut and hepatic metabolism, enters sympathetic nerves and displaces a large amount of stored noradrenaline ~ Cheese contains adrenaline ~ Tyramine directly stimulates alpha receptors ~ It causes serotonin syndrome ~ A ~ The reversible selective inhibitor moclobemide and the transdermal selegiline patch largely avoid the reaction, which is why the dietary restriction is specific to the older irreversible non-selective agents.
@ Pharmacology ~ Anthelmintics, antileprotics & antiamoebics ~ PH_ANT ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Anthelmintic and Antiamoebic Drugs
E ~ Which anthelmintic acts by inhibiting microtubule polymerisation through binding beta-tubulin? ~ Albendazole ~ Ivermectin ~ Praziquantel ~ Diethylcarbamazine ~ A ~ Because it depletes the worm's energy stores over days it is given as a short course, and the same mechanism explains its teratogenic potential and the need to avoid it in the first trimester.
E ~ Which drug is the treatment of choice for neurocysticercosis, and what must accompany it? ~ Albendazole with corticosteroids and antiepileptics to control the inflammatory response to dying cysts ~ Praziquantel alone ~ Ivermectin with steroids ~ Diethylcarbamazine with antihistamine ~ A ~ Killing the cysts provokes oedema and seizures, so steroid cover is essential, and ophthalmic cysticercosis must be excluded first because intraocular inflammation can destroy vision.
E ~ Which antiamoebic acts only in the intestinal lumen and is used to eradicate cysts after treating invasive disease? ~ Diloxanide furoate or paromomycin ~ Metronidazole ~ Chloroquine ~ Emetine ~ A ~ Tissue amoebicides such as metronidazole do not reliably clear luminal cysts, so a luminal agent must follow to prevent relapse and interrupt transmission.
M ~ Which drug is used for filariasis and causes a Mazzotti-type reaction from dying microfilariae, requiring caution in onchocerciasis? ~ Diethylcarbamazine ~ Albendazole ~ Praziquantel ~ Niclosamide ~ A ~ In onchocerciasis the reaction can threaten sight, so ivermectin is used instead, and combining diethylcarbamazine with albendazole is the mass treatment strategy for lymphatic filariasis.
M ~ Which antileprotic drug causes reddish-brown skin discolouration and has an anti-inflammatory action useful in erythema nodosum leprosum? ~ Clofazimine ~ Dapsone ~ Rifampicin ~ Ofloxacin ~ A ~ It is a riminophenazine dye that binds mycobacterial DNA, and its pigmentation is reversible but is a common cause of poor adherence, particularly in lighter-skinned patients.
H ~ Why is metronidazole combined with an alcohol warning, and what is the mechanism? ~ It inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase, producing a disulfiram-like reaction with flushing, vomiting and hypotension ~ It increases alcohol absorption ~ It induces alcohol metabolism ~ It causes hepatotoxicity with alcohol only ~ A ~ Its reduction to a cytotoxic nitro radical by anaerobic organisms is its antimicrobial mechanism, which also explains why it is inactive against aerobes.
@ Pharmacology ~ Antibiotics: classes, MOA, resistance, DoC ~ PH_ACM ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Antimicrobial Drugs
E ~ Which antibiotic class inhibits the 50S ribosomal subunit and is the drug of choice for atypical pneumonia? ~ Macrolides ~ Aminoglycosides ~ Fluoroquinolones ~ Beta-lactams ~ A ~ Because Mycoplasma has no cell wall, beta-lactams are useless, and macrolides also cover Legionella and Chlamydophila, which is why they are combined with a beta-lactam for severe community-acquired pneumonia.
E ~ Which pharmacodynamic property makes once-daily aminoglycoside dosing both effective and safer? ~ Concentration-dependent killing with a prolonged post-antibiotic effect, while toxicity relates to trough exposure ~ Time-dependent killing ~ A short post-antibiotic effect ~ Toxicity relating to peak level only ~ A ~ Beta-lactams by contrast show time-dependent killing, so their efficacy depends on the time above the minimum inhibitory concentration, favouring frequent dosing or extended infusion.
E ~ Which antibiotic is the drug of choice for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia? ~ Vancomycin, with daptomycin as an alternative ~ Cloxacillin ~ Ceftriaxone ~ Azithromycin ~ A ~ Trough monitoring or area under the curve targeting guides dosing because the therapeutic window is narrow, and treatment failure with a rising minimum inhibitory concentration prompts a switch.
M ~ Which mechanism explains resistance to fluoroquinolones? ~ Mutations in DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV with efflux pump upregulation ~ Beta-lactamase production ~ Ribosomal methylation ~ Altered penicillin-binding protein ~ A ~ Because resistance emerges stepwise under selection pressure, restricting fluoroquinolone use is a stewardship priority, and their association with tendinopathy and aortic events has further narrowed indications.
M ~ Which antibiotic combination is synergistic against enterococcal endocarditis, and why? ~ Ampicillin with gentamicin, because cell wall disruption enhances aminoglycoside uptake ~ Ampicillin with a macrolide ~ Vancomycin with tetracycline ~ Ceftriaxone alone ~ A ~ Enterococci are intrinsically tolerant to cell wall agents alone, and high-level aminoglycoside resistance abolishes the synergy, which is why it must be tested for before relying on the combination.
M ~ Which adverse effect limits the use of chloramphenicol, and which is dose-independent? ~ Idiosyncratic aplastic anaemia, which is dose-independent, in addition to dose-related marrow suppression ~ Nephrotoxicity ~ Ototoxicity ~ Tendon rupture ~ A ~ Grey baby syndrome in neonates results from immature glucuronidation, and these hazards confine its use largely to topical preparations and to specific situations such as typhoid or rickettsial disease where alternatives are unavailable.
H ~ Why does the presence of an inducible AmpC beta-lactamase make a third-generation cephalosporin unreliable even when the isolate tests susceptible? ~ Therapy can select derepressed mutants, so susceptibility is lost during treatment ~ The organism has no cell wall ~ The drug is not absorbed ~ The enzyme is inhibited by clavulanate ~ A ~ Organisms such as Serratia, Pseudomonas, indole-positive Proteus, Citrobacter and Enterobacter carry this risk, so carbapenems or cefepime are preferred for serious infection.
@ Pharmacology ~ Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, thrombolytics & hypolipidaemics ~ PH_ACG ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Drugs Affecting Coagulation
E ~ Which laboratory test monitors unfractionated heparin, and which antidote reverses it? ~ Activated partial thromboplastin time, reversed by protamine sulphate ~ Prothrombin time, reversed by vitamin K ~ Anti-Xa activity, reversed by idarucizumab ~ Thrombin time, reversed by fresh frozen plasma ~ A ~ Low molecular weight heparin requires no routine monitoring and is only partially reversed by protamine, which is one reason unfractionated heparin is preferred where reversibility matters.
E ~ Which antiplatelet drug irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase-1 for the life of the platelet? ~ Aspirin ~ Clopidogrel ~ Dipyridamole ~ Tirofiban ~ A ~ Because platelets cannot synthesise new enzyme, the effect lasts 7 to 10 days, which determines the interval needed before surgery, whereas endothelial cells regenerate cyclooxygenase within hours.
E ~ Which agent reverses the anticoagulant effect of dabigatran? ~ Idarucizumab ~ Andexanet alfa ~ Protamine ~ Vitamin K ~ A ~ Andexanet alfa reverses the factor Xa inhibitors rivaroxaban and apixaban, so matching the reversal agent to the drug class is essential in major bleeding.
M ~ Which mechanism explains the delayed onset of warfarin's anticoagulant effect? ~ Existing carboxylated clotting factors must be cleared, and factor II has a half-life of about 60 hours ~ Absorption is slow ~ It is highly protein bound ~ It requires hepatic activation ~ A ~ The transient early fall in protein C, which has a short half-life, explains warfarin-induced skin necrosis and the need for heparin overlap in acute thrombosis.
M ~ Which antiplatelet mechanism does clopidogrel use, and what pharmacogenetic factor limits its effect? ~ Irreversible P2Y12 receptor blockade, requiring CYP2C19 activation which is impaired in poor metabolisers ~ Cyclooxygenase inhibition ~ GpIIb-IIIa blockade ~ Phosphodiesterase inhibition ~ A ~ Prasugrel and ticagrelor are less dependent on CYP2C19, and proton pump inhibitors that inhibit the enzyme may reduce clopidogrel effect, which is why pantoprazole is often preferred.
H ~ Why does a statin lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol more when given at night for the shorter-acting agents? ~ Cholesterol synthesis peaks overnight, so inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase then matches the diurnal rhythm ~ Absorption is greater at night ~ Excretion is reduced at night ~ The receptor is only expressed at night ~ A ~ Longer-acting agents such as atorvastatin and rosuvastatin can be given at any time because their half-lives exceed the diurnal cycle, which improves adherence.
@ Pharmacology ~ Antihypertensives ~ PH_A ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Antihypertensive Drugs
E ~ Which antihypertensive class is preferred as first line in a young patient with proteinuric chronic kidney disease? ~ An ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker ~ A thiazide diuretic ~ A calcium channel blocker ~ A beta blocker ~ A ~ Efferent arteriolar dilatation lowers intraglomerular pressure and reduces proteinuria beyond the effect of blood pressure reduction, which is why an expected small early rise in creatinine is accepted.
E ~ Which adverse effect is characteristic of dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers? ~ Ankle oedema from precapillary vasodilatation without venodilatation ~ Dry cough ~ Hyperkalaemia ~ Bradycardia ~ A ~ Because the oedema is not from fluid retention it responds poorly to diuretics and better to dose reduction or to combining with a renin-angiotensin blocker, which dilates the venous side.
E ~ Which antihypertensive is safest in pregnancy and has the longest record of use? ~ Methyldopa ~ Enalapril ~ Losartan ~ Atenolol ~ A ~ Labetalol and nifedipine are also used, whereas renin-angiotensin blockers are fetotoxic in the second and third trimesters and atenolol has been associated with growth restriction.
M ~ Which combination should be avoided because it increases the risk of hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury without added benefit? ~ An ACE inhibitor with an angiotensin receptor blocker ~ An ACE inhibitor with a thiazide ~ A calcium channel blocker with a thiazide ~ A beta blocker with a calcium channel blocker of the dihydropyridine class ~ A ~ Dual renin-angiotensin blockade was shown in ONTARGET and ALTITUDE to increase harm, so the axis is blocked at only one point.
M ~ Which mechanism explains the antihypertensive action of spironolactone in resistant hypertension? ~ Mineralocorticoid receptor blockade, which addresses the frequent contribution of aldosterone excess ~ Direct arteriolar dilatation ~ Central sympatholysis ~ Beta-1 blockade ~ A ~ The PATHWAY-2 trial showed spironolactone to be the most effective fourth-line agent, reflecting how often unrecognised primary aldosteronism underlies resistant hypertension.
M ~ Which drug causes reflex tachycardia and fluid retention, so it is given with a beta blocker and a diuretic? ~ Hydralazine ~ Amlodipine ~ Ramipril ~ Clonidine ~ A ~ It also causes a drug-induced lupus syndrome with anti-histone antibodies, more often in slow acetylators, which limits long-term use outside pregnancy and heart failure regimens.
H ~ Why does abrupt withdrawal of clonidine cause rebound hypertension? ~ Upregulated peripheral adrenergic responsiveness is unmasked when central alpha-2 inhibition is removed, releasing a catecholamine surge ~ It has a very long half-life ~ It induces renin ~ It causes sodium retention ~ A ~ The drug must therefore be tapered, and if a crisis occurs it is treated by reinstating clonidine or by combined alpha and beta blockade rather than beta blockade alone.
@ Pharmacology ~ Antipsychotics, antidepressants ~ PH_AA ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Drugs for Mental Illness
E ~ Which antidepressant class is first line because of a favourable safety profile in overdose? ~ Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors ~ Tricyclic antidepressants ~ Monoamine oxidase inhibitors ~ Tetracyclic antidepressants ~ A ~ Tricyclic overdose causes seizures, coma and cardiac arrhythmia from sodium channel blockade, which is why they are avoided where suicidal risk is significant.
M ~ Which antipsychotic requires mandatory monitoring of the neutrophil count, and for which adverse effect? ~ Clozapine, for agranulocytosis ~ Haloperidol, for dystonia ~ Risperidone, for hyperprolactinaemia ~ Olanzapine, for weight gain ~ A ~ Clozapine is uniquely effective in treatment-resistant schizophrenia and reduces suicidality, so the monitoring requirement is accepted, and myocarditis, seizures and severe constipation are its other serious risks.
M ~ Which mechanism explains the delay of two to four weeks before an antidepressant produces clinical benefit? ~ Downregulation of presynaptic autoreceptors and downstream neuroplastic changes take weeks despite immediate reuptake blockade ~ Slow absorption ~ Slow accumulation to steady state alone ~ Delayed hepatic activation ~ A ~ Because the biochemical effect is immediate but the clinical effect is not, patients must be warned and monitored, since early activation may precede mood improvement.
H ~ Why must a washout period be observed when switching from fluoxetine to a monoamine oxidase inhibitor? ~ Norfluoxetine has a half-life of one to two weeks, so serotonin syndrome can occur unless five weeks elapse ~ Fluoxetine induces enzymes ~ The inhibitor requires loading ~ Absorption is delayed ~ A ~ Serotonin syndrome presents with altered mental state, autonomic instability and neuromuscular hyperactivity with clonus and hyperreflexia, which distinguishes it from neuroleptic malignant syndrome with lead-pipe rigidity.
H ~ Why does lithium toxicity occur when a thiazide or an NSAID is added? ~ Both reduce renal lithium clearance, thiazides by increasing proximal reabsorption and NSAIDs by reducing renal perfusion ~ Both displace lithium from protein ~ Both inhibit hepatic metabolism ~ Both increase absorption ~ A ~ Lithium is not protein bound and is entirely renally excreted, so anything that alters sodium handling or glomerular filtration changes its level, which is why levels are checked after any such change.
