@ Biochemistry ~ Oxidative phosphorylation, ETC & free radicals ~ BC_OXP ~ Harper's Illustrated Biochemistry 32e, Ch. The Respiratory Chain and Oxidative Phosphorylation
E ~ At which point does cyanide block the electron transport chain, and what is the metabolic consequence? ~ At complex I, so NADH cannot be oxidised ~ At cytochrome c oxidase in complex IV, so oxygen cannot be reduced and cells switch to anaerobic glycolysis ~ At complex II, so succinate cannot be oxidised ~ At ATP synthase, so the proton gradient is not used ~ B ~ Tissue oxygen extraction falls so venous blood stays bright red with a high oxygen saturation and a rising lactate, and treatment with hydroxocobalamin or nitrite plus thiosulphate provides an alternative ferric sink for the ion.
M ~ Why is the ATP yield from FADH2 lower than that from NADH? ~ FADH2 carries fewer electrons ~ FADH2 enters at complex II, bypassing the proton pumping of complex I, so fewer protons are translocated ~ FADH2 is oxidised in the cytosol ~ FADH2 cannot reach coenzyme Q ~ B ~ The number of protons pumped rather than the number of electrons determines the yield, which is why the accepted figures are about 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per FADH2.
M ~ Which inhibitor blocks ATP synthase itself rather than electron transport? ~ Rotenone ~ Antimycin A ~ Oligomycin ~ Malonate ~ C ~ Because the gradient cannot be discharged, electron transport also slows, which distinguishes an ATP synthase inhibitor from an uncoupler where oxygen consumption instead rises.
M ~ What happens metabolically when 2,4-dinitrophenol is given as a weight-loss agent? ~ It uncouples oxidation from phosphorylation, so substrate is oxidised with heat production instead of ATP synthesis ~ It inhibits complex I so metabolism slows ~ It inhibits fatty acid synthesis ~ It blocks the ADP-ATP translocase ~ A ~ Oxygen consumption and heat rise while ATP falls, so hyperthermia, sweating and death from hyperpyrexia are the reason the agent was abandoned, and aspirin overdose and thyrotoxicosis produce a milder version of the same picture.
H ~ Which shuttle transfers cytosolic reducing equivalents into mitochondria with the higher ATP yield? ~ The glycerol phosphate shuttle, because it delivers electrons to complex I ~ The malate-aspartate shuttle, because it regenerates NADH inside the matrix ~ The carnitine shuttle ~ The citrate shuttle ~ B ~ The glycerol phosphate shuttle delivers electrons at the flavoprotein level so it yields less, which is the reason ATP totals per glucose are quoted differently for liver and heart than for skeletal muscle.
M ~ Which superoxide dismutase isoform is found in mitochondria, and which metal does it use? ~ The cytosolic isoform using copper and zinc ~ The mitochondrial isoform using manganese ~ The extracellular isoform using iron ~ The nuclear isoform using selenium ~ B ~ Superoxide generated by electron leak is dismutated to hydrogen peroxide, which catalase and the selenium-dependent glutathione peroxidase then remove, so the defence operates as a sequence rather than a single enzyme.
M ~ Which product of membrane lipid peroxidation is measured as an index of oxidative damage? ~ Malondialdehyde ~ Glutathione disulphide ~ Uric acid ~ Bilirubin ~ A ~ Peroxidation propagates as a chain reaction through polyunsaturated fatty acids until a chain-breaking antioxidant such as vitamin E intervenes, which is why the vitamin is concentrated in membranes.
H ~ Why is a specific translocase needed to move ATP out of the mitochondrion? ~ ATP is too large to diffuse ~ The inner membrane is impermeable to charged metabolites, so the adenine nucleotide translocase exchanges matrix ATP for cytosolic ADP ~ ATP would be hydrolysed in the intermembrane space ~ The outer membrane lacks pores ~ B ~ This impermeability is precisely what allows a proton gradient to exist at all, and it means every metabolite crossing the inner membrane needs its own carrier, which is why inherited carrier defects mimic enzyme deficiencies.
@ Biochemistry ~ Protein structure, collagen & haemoglobin variants ~ BC_PRO ~ Vasudevan Textbook of Biochemistry for Medical Students 9e, Ch. Proteins and Amino Acids
M ~ A child has recurrent fractures with blue sclerae and progressive deafness. Which protein is abnormal and why is the defect dominant? ~ Type III collagen, and the defect is recessive ~ Type I collagen, and a single abnormal chain disrupts the whole triple helix by a dominant negative effect ~ Fibrillin, and the defect is dominant ~ Elastin, and the defect is dominant ~ B ~ Because three chains must assemble, one mutant product can spoil the majority of trimers, which is why a heterozygous mutation produces disease and why null mutations giving less but normal collagen are milder.
M ~ Which enzyme forms the covalent cross-links that give mature collagen its tensile strength, and which metal does it need? ~ Prolyl hydroxylase, needing iron ~ Lysyl oxidase, needing copper ~ Lysyl hydroxylase, needing zinc ~ Transglutaminase, needing calcium ~ B ~ This explains the fragile vessels and skeletal changes of copper deficiency and of Menkes disease, and it is a separate step from the vitamin C-dependent hydroxylation that stabilises the helix itself.
H ~ Why does deficiency of alpha-1 antitrypsin cause emphysema? ~ It fails to neutralise neutrophil elastase, so alveolar elastin is progressively degraded ~ It fails to transport oxygen in the lung ~ It cannot cross-link collagen ~ It prevents surfactant synthesis ~ A ~ Smoking both increases the elastase load and oxidises the inhibitor at its active methionine, which is why the panacinar basal emphysema appears decades earlier in a smoker with the deficiency.
E ~ Which chains make up fetal haemoglobin, and when is the switch to adult haemoglobin complete? ~ Two alpha and two gamma chains, with the switch largely complete by about 6 months of age ~ Two alpha and two beta chains, switching at birth ~ Two alpha and two delta chains, switching at 2 years ~ Two gamma and two beta chains, switching at 1 year ~ A ~ Fetal haemoglobin has higher oxygen affinity because it binds 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate poorly, which favours placental transfer, and its delayed decline is why beta-thalassaemia major presents only after the first few months.
M ~ Which haemoglobin fraction is characteristically raised on electrophoresis in beta-thalassaemia trait? ~ Haemoglobin H ~ Haemoglobin A2 ~ Haemoglobin S ~ Haemoglobin Barts ~ B ~ Reduced beta chain output allows more delta chains to pair with alpha, so a raised fraction with microcytosis and a normal or high red cell count distinguishes the trait from iron deficiency, in which the fraction is normal or low.
E ~ What is the Bohr effect? ~ A rise in carbon dioxide and fall in pH reduces haemoglobin oxygen affinity, favouring unloading in tissues ~ A rise in pH reduces oxygen affinity ~ Oxygen binding increases carbon dioxide carriage ~ Temperature has no effect on affinity ~ A ~ Protons and carbon dioxide stabilise the deoxygenated form, so exercising muscle that is acidotic, warm and rich in 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate extracts oxygen more readily than resting tissue.
M ~ Why is carbon monoxide poisoning more dangerous than an equivalent reduction in haemoglobin from anaemia? ~ Carboxyhaemoglobin also shifts the dissociation curve to the left, so the remaining haemoglobin releases oxygen less readily ~ Carbon monoxide destroys red cells ~ Carboxyhaemoglobin cannot be measured ~ Carbon monoxide lowers plasma oxygen tension markedly ~ A ~ Pulse oximetry reads falsely normal because it cannot distinguish the two pigments, so co-oximetry is needed, and treatment is high-flow or hyperbaric oxygen to displace the gas.
M ~ What is the function of a molecular chaperone such as a heat shock protein? ~ It cleaves misfolded proteins ~ It assists correct folding and prevents inappropriate aggregation of nascent polypeptides ~ It transports proteins across the plasma membrane ~ It determines the primary sequence ~ B ~ The primary sequence still dictates the final conformation, so the chaperone lowers the risk of the wrong pathway rather than adding information, and failure of this quality control underlies aggregation diseases.
@ Biochemistry ~ Transcription, translation & post-translational modification ~ BC_TRN ~ Harper's Illustrated Biochemistry 32e, Ch. Protein Synthesis and the Genetic Code
M ~ What does the wobble hypothesis explain about the genetic code? ~ Why one transfer RNA can read several codons differing at the third base ~ Why the code has no punctuation ~ Why the code is read in triplets ~ Why some codons are stop signals ~ A ~ Fewer transfer RNA species are therefore needed than there are codons, and it is why a silent mutation at the third position of a codon so often leaves the protein unchanged.
E ~ Which amino acid is carried by the initiator transfer RNA in eukaryotic translation? ~ Formylmethionine ~ Methionine ~ Valine ~ Alanine ~ B ~ Prokaryotes use the formylated derivative instead, and this difference in the initiation machinery, along with ribosomal subunit differences, is what allows antibiotics to inhibit bacterial protein synthesis selectively.
M ~ Aminoglycosides inhibit bacterial protein synthesis at which ribosomal subunit, and why does this spare human cells? ~ The 30S subunit, which has no exact counterpart in the human cytosolic ribosome ~ The 50S subunit, which humans lack ~ The 60S subunit, which bacteria lack ~ The 40S subunit, which is bacterial ~ A ~ Human mitochondrial ribosomes resemble bacterial ones, which is the accepted explanation for the ototoxicity of these drugs and for the increased susceptibility conferred by certain mitochondrial DNA variants.
H ~ Which inhibitor of protein synthesis acts on eukaryotic but not prokaryotic ribosomes? ~ Chloramphenicol ~ Cycloheximide ~ Tetracycline ~ Streptomycin ~ B ~ Puromycin differs again by resembling an aminoacyl transfer RNA so it causes premature chain release in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes, which is why it is a laboratory tool rather than an antibiotic.
M ~ What determines that a newly synthesised protein is translocated into the endoplasmic reticulum? ~ An amino-terminal signal peptide recognised by the signal recognition particle ~ A carboxy-terminal glycosylation site ~ Phosphorylation of a serine residue ~ Addition of ubiquitin ~ A ~ Cleavage of the signal after entry commits the protein to the secretory pathway, and proteins lacking such a sequence are completed on free ribosomes and remain cytosolic.
H ~ Where does N-linked glycosylation begin, and which residue is the acceptor? ~ In the Golgi, on serine residues ~ In the endoplasmic reticulum, on asparagine residues ~ In the cytosol, on threonine residues ~ In the nucleus, on lysine residues ~ B ~ A preassembled oligosaccharide is transferred from dolichol phosphate and then trimmed in the Golgi, whereas O-linked chains are added residue by residue on serine or threonine, and defective mannose-6-phosphate tagging of lysosomal enzymes causes I-cell disease.
M ~ How are damaged or short-lived cytosolic proteins selectively degraded? ~ By lysosomal autophagy alone ~ By tagging with ubiquitin for recognition and proteolysis by the 26S proteasome ~ By secretion into bile ~ By spontaneous hydrolysis ~ B ~ The tag is added by a cascade of activating, conjugating and ligase enzymes, so the specificity lies with the ligase, and this pathway is the target of bortezomib in myeloma.
E ~ How does a silent mutation differ from a missense mutation? ~ A silent mutation changes the codon without changing the amino acid, whereas a missense mutation substitutes a different amino acid ~ A silent mutation deletes a base ~ A silent mutation creates a stop codon ~ A silent mutation always affects splicing ~ A ~ Degeneracy of the code is what permits a silent change, and its clinical significance is usually nil except when it disturbs a splice site or alters translation efficiency.
@ Biochemistry ~ Inborn errors of metabolism ~ BC_IEM ~ Vasudevan Textbook of Biochemistry for Medical Students 9e, Ch. Inborn Errors of Metabolism
M ~ A neonate feeds poorly, becomes encephalopathic and has urine smelling of burnt sugar with raised leucine, isoleucine and valine. Which enzyme is deficient? ~ Phenylalanine hydroxylase ~ Branched-chain alpha-ketoacid dehydrogenase ~ Homogentisate oxidase ~ Cystathionine beta-synthase ~ B ~ Leucine and its ketoacid are the neurotoxic species, so treatment restricts branched-chain amino acids with dialysis in a crisis, and thiamine-responsive variants exist because the enzyme complex uses thiamine pyrophosphate.
M ~ A tall boy has downward lens subluxation, marfanoid habitus, thromboembolism and raised urinary homocystine. Which enzyme is deficient and which vitamin may help? ~ Cystathionine beta-synthase, with pyridoxine responsive in some patients ~ Fibrillin synthase, with vitamin C ~ Methionine synthase, with vitamin A ~ Lysyl oxidase, with copper ~ A ~ Downward rather than upward lens dislocation and a thrombotic tendency distinguish it clinically from Marfan syndrome, and treatment combines a low-methionine diet with betaine and pyridoxine.
H ~ An infant has developmental regression, an exaggerated startle response and a macular cherry-red spot with no hepatosplenomegaly. Which enzyme is deficient? ~ Sphingomyelinase ~ Hexosaminidase A ~ Glucocerebrosidase ~ Arylsulphatase A ~ B ~ Absence of organomegaly is the point that separates Tay-Sachs disease from Niemann-Pick disease, which shares the cherry-red spot but has marked hepatosplenomegaly, while Sandhoff disease results from loss of both hexosaminidase A and B.
H ~ A male neonate has vomiting, coma and hyperammonaemia with high urinary orotic acid and low citrulline. Which urea cycle enzyme is deficient? ~ Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I ~ Ornithine transcarbamylase ~ Argininosuccinate lyase ~ Arginase ~ B ~ Accumulated carbamoyl phosphate spills into pyrimidine synthesis to raise orotic acid, which distinguishes this X-linked defect from carbamoyl phosphate synthetase deficiency where orotic acid is low, and treatment uses sodium benzoate and phenylbutyrate to provide alternative nitrogen routes.
@ Biochemistry ~ Vitamins & deficiency diseases ~ BC_VIT ~ Vasudevan Textbook of Biochemistry for Medical Students 9e, Ch. Vitamins
M ~ A man whose diet is almost entirely maize develops dermatitis on sun-exposed skin, diarrhoea and dementia. Which vitamin is deficient and why does this diet cause it? ~ Riboflavin, because maize destroys it on cooking ~ Niacin, because the niacin in maize is bound and unavailable and maize protein is low in tryptophan ~ Thiamine, because maize contains an antagonist ~ Pyridoxine, because maize binds it ~ B ~ About 60 mg of tryptophan can substitute for 1 mg of the vitamin, which is why the same deficiency appears in Hartnup disease and in carcinoid syndrome where tryptophan is diverted to serotonin.
M ~ A patient on isoniazid develops a peripheral neuropathy. Which vitamin is deficient and what is the mechanism? ~ Pyridoxine, because the drug forms a hydrazone with pyridoxal phosphate and increases its excretion ~ Thiamine, because the drug blocks its absorption ~ Cyanocobalamin, because the drug destroys intrinsic factor ~ Folate, because the drug inhibits dihydrofolate reductase ~ A ~ The same deficiency also causes sideroblastic anaemia because the vitamin is a cofactor for ALA synthase, and routine supplementation during treatment prevents both.
E ~ Prolonged consumption of raw egg white causes deficiency of which vitamin, and by what mechanism? ~ Biotin, because avidin in raw egg white binds it and prevents absorption ~ Riboflavin, because egg white destroys it ~ Folate, because egg white contains an antagonist ~ Vitamin K, because egg white binds bile salts ~ A ~ The vitamin is a cofactor for carboxylases including pyruvate and acetyl-CoA carboxylase, so deficiency causes alopecia, a scaly periorificial dermatitis and organic acidaemia, and cooking denatures avidin.
M ~ A woman taking large doses of a vitamin supplement develops headache, papilloedema and diplopia with no mass lesion. Which vitamin excess explains this? ~ Vitamin A ~ Vitamin C ~ Thiamine ~ Niacin ~ A ~ Being fat soluble it accumulates, causing raised intracranial pressure, dry skin, bone pain and hepatotoxicity, and in pregnancy high doses and the retinoid derivatives are teratogenic, which is why isotretinoin requires strict contraception.
@ Biochemistry ~ DNA/RNA, replication, mutations ~ BC_DNA ~ Harper's Illustrated Biochemistry 32e, Ch. DNA Organization, Replication and Repair
E ~ Which enzyme unwinds the parental duplex at the replication fork, and which enzyme relieves the resulting torsional strain? ~ Helicase unwinds it and topoisomerase relieves the supercoiling ~ Primase unwinds it and ligase relieves the strain ~ Polymerase unwinds it and nuclease relieves the strain ~ Ligase unwinds it and helicase relieves the strain ~ A ~ Single-strand binding proteins then keep the separated strands apart, and because bacterial topoisomerases differ from human ones they are the target of the fluoroquinolones.
M ~ How does a transition mutation differ from a transversion? ~ A transition substitutes a purine for a purine or a pyrimidine for a pyrimidine, whereas a transversion exchanges a purine for a pyrimidine ~ A transition deletes a base pair ~ A transition always creates a stop codon ~ A transition occurs only in mitochondrial DNA ~ A ~ Spontaneous deamination of cytosine to uracil and of methylcytosine to thymine makes transitions the commoner event, which is why CpG sites are mutational hotspots.
M ~ A family shows a disorder that appears at a younger age and more severely in each successive generation. Which mutational mechanism explains this? ~ Trinucleotide repeat expansion, which lengthens as it passes through the germline ~ Uniparental disomy ~ Mosaicism ~ A balanced translocation ~ A ~ This anticipation is characteristic of Huntington disease, myotonic dystrophy and fragile X syndrome, and the parent of origin matters because expansion occurs preferentially in paternal transmission for some of these disorders and maternal for others.
