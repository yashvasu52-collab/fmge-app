@ Microbiology ~ Neisseria, Haemophilus, Bordetella, Brucella ~ MIC_NEI ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Neisseria and Haemophilus
E ~ Which selective medium is used to isolate Neisseria gonorrhoeae from genital specimens? ~ Thayer-Martin medium with vancomycin, colistin and nystatin ~ Thiosulphate citrate bile salt sucrose agar ~ Lowenstein-Jensen medium ~ Bordet-Gengou medium ~ A ~ The antibiotic supplement suppresses commensal flora while the chocolate agar base and carbon dioxide atmosphere support this fastidious oxidase-positive Gram-negative diplococcus.
E ~ Which capsular serogroups of Neisseria meningitidis are covered by the quadrivalent conjugate vaccine? ~ A, C, W and Y ~ B and C only ~ A and B only ~ X and Z ~ A ~ Group B polysaccharide resembles human neural tissue so it is poorly immunogenic and required a separate protein-based vaccine, which is why it is absent from the quadrivalent conjugate product.
E ~ Which organism requires both factor X and factor V for growth and shows satellitism around Staphylococcus aureus on blood agar? ~ Haemophilus influenzae ~ Bordetella pertussis ~ Brucella melitensis ~ Neisseria meningitidis ~ A ~ Haemin and NAD are released by staphylococcal haemolysis, so colonies grow only near the streak; chocolate agar supplies both factors directly, which is why it is the routine medium.
M ~ Which Haemophilus influenzae serotype caused most invasive childhood disease before vaccination, and what is the capsular polysaccharide? ~ Type b, with a polyribosyl ribitol phosphate capsule ~ Type a, with a galactose capsule ~ Type f, with a glucose capsule ~ Non-typeable strains ~ A ~ Conjugating the polyribosyl ribitol phosphate capsule to a protein carrier made it immunogenic in infants and nearly eliminated invasive type b disease, leaving non-typeable strains as the main cause of otitis and exacerbations.
M ~ Which medium is used for the primary isolation of Bordetella pertussis, and why is it potato-based with charcoal or blood? ~ Bordet-Gengou or Regan-Lowe medium, because charcoal and blood absorb inhibitory fatty acids ~ MacConkey agar, because of lactose fermentation ~ Thayer-Martin, because of antibiotic selection ~ Deoxycholate citrate agar ~ A ~ The organism is extremely fastidious and sensitive to fatty acids and peroxides, and the mercury-drop colonies appear only after several days, so PCR on a nasopharyngeal swab is now the preferred test.
M ~ Which toxin of Bordetella pertussis causes lymphocytosis and blocks Gi-mediated signalling by ADP-ribosylation? ~ Pertussis toxin ~ Tracheal cytotoxin ~ Adenylate cyclase toxin ~ Dermonecrotic toxin ~ A ~ Inhibiting Gi leaves adenylate cyclase disinhibited and impairs chemotaxis, while tracheal cytotoxin destroys ciliated cells, producing the paroxysmal cough that persists after the organism has been cleared.
M ~ Brucellosis is best diagnosed serologically by which test, and why must the prozone phenomenon be considered? ~ Standard tube agglutination test, because very high antibody can give a false negative at low dilutions ~ Weil-Felix test ~ Widal test ~ Paul-Bunnell test ~ A ~ Testing must extend to high dilutions to avoid missing a positive; blood culture on biphasic Castaneda medium held for several weeks remains the definitive test.
H ~ Why does Neisseria gonorrhoeae not confer lasting immunity after infection, allowing repeated episodes? ~ Antigenic variation of pili and Opa proteins with no protective capsular immunity ~ It is intracellular and hidden from antibody ~ It suppresses complement entirely ~ It produces an IgA protease that eliminates all antibody ~ A ~ Continuous pilin gene rearrangement means each infection presents new epitopes, which is also the principal obstacle to a gonococcal vaccine, unlike the meningococcus where capsular immunity is protective.
@ Microbiology ~ Rabies, polio & enteroviruses ~ MIC_RAB ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Rhabdoviruses and Picornaviruses
E ~ Which intracytoplasmic inclusion is pathognomonic of rabies and where is it best sought? ~ Negri bodies in hippocampal and cerebellar Purkinje neurons ~ Cowdry type A bodies in the cortex ~ Owl-eye inclusions in glia ~ Guarnieri bodies in the medulla ~ A ~ Negri bodies are present in about 70 percent of cases, so their absence does not exclude rabies, and the direct fluorescent antibody test on brain tissue is the reference standard.
E ~ Which category of animal exposure requires both rabies vaccine and immunoglobulin? ~ Category III, with transdermal bites, scratches with bleeding or mucosal contamination with saliva ~ Category I, touching or feeding an animal ~ Category II, minor scratches without bleeding ~ Any contact with a vaccinated dog ~ A ~ Immunoglobulin is infiltrated into and around the wound to neutralise virus locally before vaccine-induced antibody appears, and thorough washing with soap and water for 15 minutes is the essential first step.
E ~ Which polio vaccine can cause vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis and circulating vaccine-derived virus? ~ Oral live attenuated (Sabin) vaccine ~ Injectable inactivated (Salk) vaccine ~ Conjugate polysaccharide vaccine ~ Toxoid vaccine ~ A ~ The attenuated strains can revert to neurovirulence, especially type 2, which is why type 2 was withdrawn from the oral vaccine and inactivated vaccine has been introduced into routine schedules.
M ~ Why does the inactivated polio vaccine provide less intestinal mucosal immunity than the oral vaccine? ~ It induces systemic IgG but little secretory IgA in the gut, so it does not interrupt faecal transmission as effectively ~ It contains fewer serotypes ~ It is given at a later age ~ It requires more doses ~ A ~ This difference explains why oral vaccine was preferred for outbreak control and eradication campaigns, while inactivated vaccine reliably prevents paralysis in the vaccinated individual.
M ~ Which enterovirus is the commonest cause of hand, foot and mouth disease, and which is associated with severe neurological complications in outbreaks? ~ Coxsackievirus A16 commonly, with enterovirus 71 causing brainstem encephalitis ~ Echovirus 11 and coxsackievirus B3 ~ Poliovirus type 1 and type 3 ~ Rhinovirus and coronavirus ~ A ~ Enterovirus 71 outbreaks in the Asia-Pacific region have caused fatal brainstem encephalitis and pulmonary oedema, which is why surveillance distinguishes it from the milder coxsackie A16 disease.
M ~ Which clinical syndrome is characteristic of coxsackie B virus and presents with fever and severe pleuritic chest or abdominal pain? ~ Bornholm disease (epidemic pleurodynia) ~ Herpangina ~ Aseptic meningitis only ~ Acute haemorrhagic conjunctivitis ~ A ~ Coxsackie B also causes myocarditis and pericarditis, whereas herpangina with posterior oral vesicles is typical of coxsackie A, and both groups cause aseptic meningitis.
M ~ In the pathogenesis of rabies, why is post-exposure prophylaxis effective even after the bite? ~ The virus replicates locally in muscle for days to weeks before entering peripheral nerves ~ Antibody can neutralise virus within neurons ~ The virus is bloodborne throughout ~ The vaccine eliminates the virus from the brain ~ A ~ The long and variable incubation period reflects this local phase and the distance to the central nervous system, so proximal head and neck bites have shorter incubation and greater urgency.
H ~ Why is the intradermal rabies vaccine regimen used in India, and what is its principal advantage? ~ It uses one-fifth of the intramuscular dose at multiple sites, giving equivalent immunogenicity at lower cost ~ It avoids the need for immunoglobulin ~ It gives lifelong immunity in one dose ~ It can be given after symptoms appear ~ A ~ Dose sparing greatly extends limited vaccine supplies in high-burden settings, but it demands trained staff and correct technique, and it does not remove the need for immunoglobulin in category III exposure.
@ Microbiology ~ Respiratory viruses: influenza, SARS-CoV-2, measles, mumps, rubella ~ MIC_RSP ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Orthomyxoviruses and Paramyxoviruses
E ~ Which influenza surface protein is the target of oseltamivir? ~ Neuraminidase ~ Haemagglutinin ~ M2 ion channel ~ Nucleoprotein ~ A ~ Neuraminidase cleaves sialic acid to release progeny virions, so its inhibition traps virus at the cell surface; the M2 channel is the target of the older and now largely useless amantadine.
E ~ Antigenic shift in influenza A refers to: ~ Reassortment of gene segments producing a new subtype with pandemic potential ~ Point mutation causing gradual drift ~ Loss of the matrix protein ~ Change in the polymerase only ~ A ~ Because the genome is segmented, coinfection of an intermediate host such as the pig allows whole-segment exchange, generating a haemagglutinin to which the population has no immunity.
E ~ Which complication of mumps is the commonest cause of acquired unilateral sensorineural deafness in children? ~ Mumps labyrinthitis ~ Orchitis ~ Pancreatitis ~ Aseptic meningitis ~ A ~ Orchitis and aseptic meningitis are commoner overall, but the abrupt permanent hearing loss from labyrinthine involvement is the classic long-term sequela, and vaccination has largely removed it.
M ~ Which measles complication appears years after infection and presents with progressive myoclonus and dementia? ~ Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis ~ Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis ~ Giant cell pneumonia ~ Otitis media ~ A ~ Defective viral matrix protein permits persistent infection of neurons, and the electroencephalogram shows periodic high-amplitude complexes with very high antibody titre in cerebrospinal fluid; it is uniformly fatal.
M ~ Why is the measles vaccine given at 9 months in India but at 12 to 15 months in many high-income countries? ~ Higher force of infection means earlier exposure, so protection is needed before maternal antibody has fully waned ~ The vaccine is more potent in India ~ Cold chain limitations ~ Higher risk of febrile seizures later ~ A ~ Giving it earlier accepts slightly lower seroconversion in exchange for protecting infants during the period of highest mortality, and a second dose at 16 to 24 months covers primary vaccine failure.
M ~ Which specimen and test are preferred for laboratory confirmation of SARS-CoV-2 in the first week of illness? ~ Nasopharyngeal swab for real-time reverse transcription PCR ~ Serum IgG antibody ~ Stool culture ~ Blood culture ~ A ~ Antibody appears only after the first week and reflects past infection, so molecular detection of viral RNA from the upper respiratory tract is the diagnostic test during acute illness.
M ~ Congenital rubella syndrome risk is highest when maternal infection occurs: ~ Before 11 weeks of gestation ~ Between 20 and 28 weeks ~ After 30 weeks ~ At the time of delivery ~ A ~ Organogenesis determines the pattern of cataract, deafness and cardiac defect, and infection after 20 weeks rarely causes structural damage, which is why gestational age determines counselling.
H ~ Why does an influenza vaccine need annual reformulation, unlike the measles vaccine? ~ Continuous antigenic drift in haemagglutinin and neuraminidase erodes strain-specific immunity ~ Influenza vaccine is inactivated and short-lived ~ Measles vaccine is given twice ~ Influenza has no surface antigens ~ A ~ Error-prone polymerase and immune selection drive drift each season, whereas measles is antigenically stable with a single serotype, which is precisely why it is theoretically eradicable.
@ Microbiology ~ Rickettsia, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma ~ MIC_RIC ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Rickettsia, Chlamydia and Mycoplasma
E ~ Which feature of Mycoplasma explains its resistance to beta-lactam antibiotics? ~ It lacks a cell wall and has sterols in its membrane ~ It produces beta-lactamase ~ It is intracellular ~ It forms spores ~ A ~ With no peptidoglycan there is no target for beta-lactams, so macrolides, tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones are used, and the organism requires cholesterol-enriched media forming fried-egg colonies.
E ~ Which Chlamydia trachomatis serovars cause lymphogranuloma venereum? ~ L1, L2 and L3 ~ A, B and C ~ D to K ~ Serovar J only ~ A ~ Serovars A to C cause trachoma and D to K cause genital infection and inclusion conjunctivitis, whereas the L serovars are more invasive and target lymphatic tissue, producing buboes and the groove sign.
E ~ Which stain demonstrates the intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies of Chlamydia trachomatis in conjunctival scrapings? ~ Giemsa stain showing Halberstaedter-Prowazek bodies ~ Ziehl-Neelsen stain ~ India ink ~ Albert stain ~ A ~ Iodine staining of glycogen-containing inclusions and direct immunofluorescence are alternatives, but nucleic acid amplification has become the test of choice for both ocular and genital disease.
M ~ Which characteristic of the chlamydial developmental cycle explains why treatment must be prolonged? ~ The metabolically inactive extracellular elementary body is not susceptible, and only the intracellular reticulate body replicates ~ It forms spores ~ It has a thick capsule ~ It resides in neurons ~ A ~ Only the replicating form is drug-susceptible, so a single dose of azithromycin works because of the drug's long intracellular half-life, and shorter-acting agents need a week or more.
M ~ Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection is associated with which extrapulmonary complication mediated by cold agglutinins? ~ Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia ~ Nephrotic syndrome ~ Aplastic anaemia ~ Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura ~ A ~ IgM cold agglutinins against the erythrocyte I antigen arise from cross-reactivity, and other extrapulmonary features include erythema multiforme, Stevens-Johnson syndrome and Guillain-Barre syndrome.
M ~ Which rickettsial disease is transmitted by the human body louse and is associated with war and famine, with recrudescence as Brill-Zinsser disease? ~ Epidemic typhus due to Rickettsia prowazekii ~ Endemic typhus due to Rickettsia typhi ~ Scrub typhus due to Orientia tsutsugamushi ~ Indian tick typhus ~ A ~ The organism persists in lymphoid tissue for years, so recrudescence can seed a new louse-borne outbreak, which is why delousing is central to control.
M ~ Which reproductive complication of untreated chlamydial cervicitis makes screening of young sexually active women worthwhile? ~ Tubal factor infertility and ectopic pregnancy from silent salpingitis ~ Cervical carcinoma ~ Endometriosis ~ Vulval carcinoma ~ A ~ Because up to 70 percent of infections are asymptomatic, damage accumulates silently, and opportunistic screening with nucleic acid amplification on a self-taken vulvovaginal swab detects it.
H ~ Why is doxycycline effective against Rickettsia, Chlamydia and Mycoplasma despite their very different cell structures? ~ All three are susceptible because tetracyclines penetrate cells and inhibit the 30S ribosome, a target common to all bacteria ~ All three lack a cell wall ~ All three are obligate intracellular organisms ~ Doxycycline acts on host cells ~ A ~ Intracellular penetration plus a conserved ribosomal target explains the breadth, which is why doxycycline is the empirical choice for atypical pneumonia and for undifferentiated fever in rickettsial endemic areas.
@ Microbiology ~ Specimen collection, serology & molecular diagnostics ~ MIC_SPC ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Diagnostic Microbiology
E ~ Blood for culture in suspected bacteraemia should ideally be collected: ~ As two or three sets from separate venepuncture sites before antibiotics, with adequate volume ~ As a single sample from an indwelling line ~ Only during a fever spike ~ After the first antibiotic dose ~ A ~ Volume is the single most important determinant of yield, and separate sets distinguish true bacteraemia from skin contamination; timing to fever spikes adds little because bacteraemia is usually continuous or intermittent.
E ~ Which transport medium is used for stool specimens when culture will be delayed? ~ Cary-Blair medium ~ Stuart medium ~ Amies medium ~ Alkaline peptone water ~ A ~ Cary-Blair preserves enteric pathogens including Vibrio, Salmonella and Shigella, whereas Stuart and Amies suit swabs of other sites and alkaline peptone water is an enrichment rather than a transport medium.
E ~ Which specimen is preferred for the diagnosis of typhoid fever in the first week of illness? ~ Blood culture ~ Stool culture ~ Urine culture ~ Bone marrow aspirate ~ A ~ Bacteraemia is present from the outset, whereas stool and urine become positive in the second and third weeks; bone marrow culture has the highest yield overall and remains positive after antibiotics.
M ~ Which molecular method allows quantification of a pathogen's nucleic acid in real time and is used for HIV and hepatitis viral loads? ~ Real-time quantitative PCR with fluorescent probes ~ Conventional gel-based PCR ~ Southern blotting ~ Sanger sequencing ~ A ~ Signal accumulation is measured cycle by cycle, so the threshold cycle relates directly to starting template amount, which permits monitoring of treatment response and detection of virological failure.
M ~ Why must a paired serum sample be used when a single antibody titre is being interpreted in an endemic area? ~ A fourfold rise between acute and convalescent samples demonstrates recent infection against a high background seroprevalence ~ Antibody is unstable in storage ~ A single sample cannot be diluted ~ IgM is undetectable in acute illness ~ A ~ In areas of high prior exposure a single raised titre may reflect past infection or vaccination, so demonstrating a rise, or detecting IgM, is what establishes recency.
M ~ Which technique identifies bacteria and fungi directly from a colony within minutes by protein fingerprinting? ~ MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry ~ Latex agglutination ~ Automated antibiotic susceptibility testing ~ Gram staining ~ A ~ Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionisation time-of-flight compares ribosomal protein spectra with a database, shortening identification from days to minutes, though susceptibility testing still requires separate methods.
M ~ Which sample is most appropriate for diagnosing tuberculous meningitis by molecular testing? ~ Cerebrospinal fluid, sent in adequate volume for a cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test ~ Blood for PCR ~ Sputum only ~ Urine for antigen ~ A ~ Sensitivity depends heavily on volume because the disease is paucibacillary, and the ultra version of the cartridge assay was developed specifically to improve detection in cerebrospinal fluid.
H ~ Why is a nucleic acid amplification test unsuitable as a test of cure immediately after treating gonorrhoea or chlamydia? ~ Residual nucleic acid from non-viable organisms can remain detectable for weeks, giving a false positive ~ The test loses sensitivity after treatment ~ Antibiotics inhibit the polymerase ~ The organism becomes uncultivable ~ A ~ For this reason a test of cure, when indicated, is deferred for several weeks, and culture is preferred where confirmation of viability and susceptibility testing are needed.
@ Microbiology ~ Spirochaetes: syphilis, leptospira, borrelia ~ MIC_SPI ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Spirochaetes
E ~ Which test remains reactive for life after treated syphilis and therefore cannot be used to monitor treatment? ~ Treponemal specific tests such as TPHA or FTA-ABS ~ VDRL ~ Rapid plasma reagin ~ Dark ground microscopy ~ A ~ Non-treponemal tests measuring anticardiolipin antibody fall with successful treatment and are used to monitor response, whereas specific antitreponemal antibody persists indefinitely.
E ~ Which condition causes a biological false-positive VDRL? ~ Systemic lupus erythematosus and antiphospholipid syndrome ~ Iron deficiency anaemia ~ Hypothyroidism ~ Peptic ulcer disease ~ A ~ Because the test detects antibody to cardiolipin rather than to the organism, autoimmune disease, pregnancy, malaria and some viral infections can give false positives, which is why a specific treponemal test is used to confirm.
E ~ Which spirochaete is transmitted through skin abraded by contact with water contaminated by rodent urine? ~ Leptospira interrogans ~ Treponema pallidum ~ Borrelia recurrentis ~ Treponema pertenue ~ A ~ Occupational and recreational water exposure explains outbreaks after flooding, and the organism is best seen by dark ground microscopy or cultured in Ellinghausen-McCullough-Johnson-Harris medium.
M ~ Which stage of syphilis is most infectious, and why? ~ Secondary syphilis, because moist mucocutaneous lesions teem with treponemes ~ Late latent syphilis ~ Tertiary gummatous syphilis ~ Neurosyphilis ~ A ~ Condylomata lata and mucous patches are highly infectious, whereas tertiary lesions contain few organisms and represent a hypersensitivity response rather than active proliferation.
M ~ Which relapsing fever spirochaete is louse-borne and shows antigenic variation explaining the recurrent fever pattern? ~ Borrelia recurrentis ~ Borrelia burgdorferi ~ Leptospira canicola ~ Treponema carateum ~ A ~ Sequential expression of variable major proteins allows the organism to escape antibody, producing successive febrile episodes as each new antigenic variant proliferates before being cleared.
M ~ Which serological test is the reference standard for leptospirosis and requires paired sera? ~ Microscopic agglutination test using live serovars ~ Weil-Felix test ~ Widal test ~ VDRL ~ A ~ It is technically demanding and available only in reference laboratories, so IgM ELISA or rapid tests are used in the field, with culture of blood in the first week and urine thereafter.
M ~ Which clinical stage of Lyme disease is characterised by erythema migrans, and what is the vector? ~ Early localised disease, transmitted by Ixodes ticks ~ Early disseminated disease, transmitted by sandflies ~ Late disease, transmitted by lice ~ Congenital disease, transmitted transplacentally ~ A ~ The expanding annular lesion with central clearing appears days to weeks after the bite, and early doxycycline prevents the later carditis, neuroborreliosis and arthritis.
H ~ Why can Treponema pallidum not be grown in artificial culture, and how does this shape diagnosis? ~ It is a microaerophilic obligate parasite with limited biosynthetic capacity, so diagnosis relies on microscopy, serology and molecular tests ~ It requires anaerobic conditions only ~ It grows only in mosquitoes ~ It forms spores that resist culture ~ A ~ Its reduced genome lacks many biosynthetic pathways, so it is propagated only in rabbit testis, and this is why dark ground microscopy of a moist lesion plus paired serology remains the diagnostic mainstay.
@ Microbiology ~ Sterilization, disinfection, stains, culture media ~ MIC_SDS ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Sterilisation and Disinfection
E ~ Which biological indicator is used to validate autoclave performance? ~ Spores of Geobacillus stearothermophilus ~ Spores of Bacillus atrophaeus ~ Escherichia coli culture ~ Candida albicans culture ~ A ~ These spores require moist heat at 121 degrees to be killed, whereas Bacillus atrophaeus spores are used for hot air oven and ethylene oxide validation, so matching indicator to process matters.
E ~ Which method is used to sterilise heat-labile items such as flexible endoscopes and plastic implants? ~ Ethylene oxide gas or low-temperature hydrogen peroxide plasma ~ Autoclaving at 134 degrees ~ Hot air oven at 160 degrees ~ Boiling for 10 minutes ~ A ~ Ethylene oxide is a highly penetrating alkylating agent but is toxic and needs prolonged aeration, and plasma or peracetic acid systems have shorter cycles for the same class of items.
E ~ Which process removes microorganisms from a heat-labile solution such as serum without heat? ~ Membrane filtration through a 0.22 micrometre filter ~ Pasteurisation ~ Tyndallisation ~ Inspissation ~ A ~ Filtration excludes bacteria and fungi but not viruses or mycoplasma, which is why filtered biological products are still tested for viral contamination.
M ~ Which level of disinfection is required for an instrument that contacts mucous membranes but does not penetrate tissue, such as a laryngoscope blade? ~ High-level disinfection ~ Sterilisation ~ Intermediate-level disinfection ~ Low-level disinfection ~ A ~ Under the Spaulding classification, critical items entering sterile tissue require sterilisation, semi-critical items contacting mucosa require high-level disinfection, and non-critical items touching intact skin need only low-level disinfection.
M ~ Which chemical agent is a high-level disinfectant that is not inactivated by organic material and is widely used for endoscopes? ~ Glutaraldehyde 2 percent ~ Ethyl alcohol 70 percent ~ Chlorhexidine ~ Quaternary ammonium compound ~ A ~ It is sporicidal with prolonged contact and non-corrosive, though it is irritant and sensitising to staff, which is why ortho-phthalaldehyde and peracetic acid have gained ground.
M ~ Which principle explains why moist heat sterilises at a lower temperature than dry heat? ~ Steam denatures and coagulates proteins and delivers latent heat of condensation, whereas dry heat kills by oxidation ~ Moist heat penetrates spores through pores ~ Dry heat cannot kill spores at all ~ Steam is chemically toxic ~ A ~ Because coagulation needs less energy than oxidation, 121 degrees for 15 minutes suffices with steam whereas the hot air oven needs 160 degrees for 2 hours.
M ~ Which medium is described as an enriched medium rather than an enrichment medium? ~ Blood agar and chocolate agar ~ Selenite F broth ~ Alkaline peptone water ~ Tetrathionate broth ~ A ~ An enriched solid medium has nutrients added to support fastidious organisms, whereas an enrichment liquid medium selectively encourages one organism while inhibiting others, a distinction frequently confused.
H ~ Why is Tyndallisation used for media containing sugars or gelatin? ~ Intermittent heating at 100 degrees on successive days kills spores as they germinate between cycles, without the caramelisation caused by autoclaving ~ It is faster than autoclaving ~ It sterilises at room temperature ~ It relies on chemical action ~ A ~ Discontinuous sterilisation exploits the vulnerability of germinating spores, and inspissation at lower temperature serves the same purpose for egg-based media such as Lowenstein-Jensen.
H ~ Why does a Bowie-Dick test complement a chemical and biological indicator in autoclave monitoring? ~ It detects air removal and steam penetration failure in a porous load, which the other indicators may miss ~ It measures spore killing directly ~ It records cycle temperature only ~ It tests for residual ethylene oxide ~ A ~ Trapped air prevents steam from reaching the load even when the recorded temperature is correct, so the three approaches together cover physical, chemical and biological assurance.
