@ Surgery ~ Urinary stones, BPH, hematuria ~ SUR_USB ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Urinary Tract
E ~ Which imaging is the investigation of choice for acute renal colic? ~ Non-contrast computed tomography of the kidneys, ureters and bladder ~ Intravenous urography ~ Plain radiograph ~ Magnetic resonance urography ~ A ~ It detects stones of almost any composition including radiolucent uric acid stones, defines size and site, and identifies alternative diagnoses, though ultrasound is preferred in pregnancy and in children.
E ~ Which stone type is radiolucent on plain radiography and may dissolve with urinary alkalinisation? ~ Uric acid stone ~ Calcium oxalate stone ~ Struvite stone ~ Cystine stone ~ A ~ Alkalinising the urine to a pH above 6.5 with potassium citrate, with allopurinol where indicated, can dissolve these stones, which is the only stone type reliably amenable to medical dissolution.
E ~ Which organism is associated with struvite staghorn calculi, and by what mechanism? ~ Urease-producing organisms such as Proteus, which raise urinary pH and precipitate magnesium ammonium phosphate ~ Escherichia coli, by lowering pH ~ Pseudomonas, by producing oxalate ~ Staphylococcus, by producing cystine ~ A ~ Because the stone harbours bacteria, complete surgical clearance rather than antibiotics alone is required, and residual fragments cause recurrent infection.
M ~ Which stone size and location is most likely to pass spontaneously with medical expulsive therapy? ~ A stone under 5 mm in the distal ureter ~ A 10 mm stone in the proximal ureter ~ A staghorn calculus ~ A 15 mm renal pelvic stone ~ A ~ Analgesia with hydration and, in selected cases, an alpha blocker supports passage, while larger or proximal stones and any obstruction with infection require intervention.
M ~ Which presentation of urolithiasis requires emergency decompression rather than elective treatment? ~ An obstructed infected system with fever and systemic sepsis ~ Renal colic with vomiting ~ A 4 mm stone with microscopic haematuria ~ Bilateral non-obstructing renal stones ~ A ~ Percutaneous nephrostomy or a ureteric stent relieves the obstruction, and definitive stone treatment is deferred until the infection is controlled because instrumenting an infected system can precipitate septic shock.
M ~ Which class of drug shrinks the prostate over months by inhibiting conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone? ~ A 5-alpha reductase inhibitor such as finasteride ~ An alpha blocker such as tamsulosin ~ An anticholinergic ~ A phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor ~ A ~ Alpha blockers relieve symptoms within days by relaxing smooth muscle, whereas 5-alpha reductase inhibitors take 6 months, reduce prostate volume and lower the risk of retention and surgery, and they halve the prostate-specific antigen.
H ~ Why does painless visible haematuria in an adult require full urinary tract investigation even if it settles? ~ It is the cardinal presentation of urothelial malignancy, and intermittency does not reduce the risk ~ It is always due to infection ~ It resolves without significance ~ Only persistent bleeding matters ~ A ~ Evaluation combines upper tract imaging with cystoscopy, because ultrasound alone misses small bladder tumours and cytology has limited sensitivity for low-grade disease.
@ Surgery ~ Urology: renal, bladder & testicular tumours; hydrocele, phimosis ~ SUR_URO ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Kidneys and Genitourinary Tract
E ~ Which finding on transillumination and palpation distinguishes a hydrocele from a testicular tumour? ~ A hydrocele transilluminates and the testis cannot be felt separately, whereas a tumour is opaque and hard ~ Both transilluminate ~ A tumour transilluminates brightly ~ Neither can be examined clinically ~ A ~ Ultrasound is used when the testis cannot be palpated, because a tumour with a secondary hydrocele must not be mistaken for a simple one.
E ~ Which tumour marker pattern indicates a non-seminomatous element in a testicular germ cell tumour? ~ A raised alpha-fetoprotein ~ A raised lactate dehydrogenase only ~ A raised placental alkaline phosphatase only ~ A normal beta-hCG ~ A ~ Pure seminoma never raises alpha-fetoprotein, so its elevation mandates treatment as a non-seminomatous tumour regardless of the histology reported.
E ~ Which surgical approach is used for a suspected testicular tumour, and why? ~ Inguinal orchidectomy with high cord ligation, avoiding a scrotal incision which would alter lymphatic drainage ~ Scrotal orchidectomy ~ Trans-scrotal biopsy first ~ Laparoscopic orchidectomy ~ A ~ Testicular lymphatics drain to the para-aortic nodes along the cord, so violating the scrotum introduces inguinal nodal spread and complicates staging and radiotherapy planning.
M ~ Which type of bladder cancer is associated with chronic schistosomiasis, and how does it differ in behaviour? ~ Squamous cell carcinoma, which tends to be muscle-invasive at presentation and less responsive to intravesical therapy ~ Urothelial carcinoma, which is superficial ~ Adenocarcinoma from urachal remnants ~ Small cell carcinoma ~ A ~ In non-endemic areas urothelial carcinoma predominates and smoking is the main risk factor, and most cases present as non-muscle-invasive disease managed by resection with intravesical therapy.
M ~ Which intravesical agent reduces recurrence and progression in high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer? ~ Bacille Calmette-Guerin immunotherapy ~ Mitomycin as the preferred agent for high risk ~ Gemcitabine ~ Cisplatin ~ A ~ Mitomycin is used for intermediate-risk disease and as a single postoperative instillation, whereas BCG with maintenance is standard for high-grade and carcinoma in situ.
H ~ Why is circumcision indicated for pathological phimosis but not for physiological non-retractility in a young boy? ~ Balanitis xerotica obliterans produces a scarred non-retractile prepuce that will not resolve, whereas physiological adhesions separate spontaneously with growth ~ All non-retractile foreskins require surgery ~ Physiological phimosis causes obstruction ~ Circumcision is never indicated ~ A ~ Forcible retraction of a physiologically adherent foreskin causes scarring and can create the very problem the operation would then be needed to correct.
@ Surgery ~ Vascular surgery, varicose veins & peripheral arterial disease ~ SUR_VAS ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. Arterial and Venous Disorders
E ~ Which bedside measurement quantifies the severity of peripheral arterial disease, and what value indicates critical ischaemia? ~ The ankle brachial pressure index, with a value below 0.4 indicating critical ischaemia ~ Capillary refill time, with 3 seconds indicating critical ischaemia ~ Toe temperature ~ Pulse volume alone ~ A ~ The index is falsely high in diabetes and renal failure because of medial calcification, in which case toe pressures or transcutaneous oxygen measurement are used instead.
E ~ Which intervention gives the greatest improvement in walking distance in stable intermittent claudication? ~ A supervised exercise programme with smoking cessation and best medical therapy ~ Immediate angioplasty ~ Bypass surgery ~ Vasodilator drugs ~ A ~ Because claudication rarely progresses to limb loss, the priorities are cardiovascular risk reduction with a statin and antiplatelet and functional improvement, with intervention reserved for lifestyle-limiting or critical disease.
E ~ Which classification describes the six-hour window and the features distinguishing viable from irreversibly ischaemic limb? ~ The Rutherford classification of acute limb ischaemia, based on sensory loss, motor deficit and Doppler signals ~ The Fontaine classification ~ The CEAP classification ~ The Wagner classification ~ A ~ A limb with profound paralysis and absent Doppler signals is irreversible and requires amputation, whereas a threatened limb requires immediate revascularisation.
M ~ Which finding indicates that a varicose vein problem arises from saphenofemoral incompetence? ~ Reflux at the saphenofemoral junction on duplex ultrasound with a positive tourniquet test at the groin ~ Reflux at the popliteal fossa ~ Perforator incompetence in the calf ~ Deep venous obstruction ~ A ~ Duplex has replaced clinical tests as the mapping tool, and treatment is directed at the highest point of reflux, most often by endovenous thermal or chemical ablation.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes a venous from an arterial leg ulcer? ~ A shallow ulcer in the gaiter area with surrounding pigmentation and oedema, with palpable pulses ~ A deep punched-out ulcer over the malleolus with absent pulses ~ A painless plantar ulcer with intact pulses ~ An ulcer with rolled everted edges ~ A ~ Compression is the treatment for venous ulceration but is dangerous in arterial disease, which is why the ankle brachial index must be measured before bandaging.
H ~ Why is an abdominal aortic aneurysm repaired at a diameter of about 5.5 cm rather than earlier? ~ Below that size the annual rupture risk is lower than the operative mortality, so surveillance is safer ~ Smaller aneurysms never rupture ~ Repair is technically impossible when smaller ~ The diameter cannot be measured accurately ~ A ~ Rapid expansion of more than 1 cm per year, symptoms, or a lower threshold in women shift the balance toward earlier repair, and the decision is individualised.
@ Surgery ~ Wound healing, surgical site infection & sutures ~ SUR_WND ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. Wound Healing and Surgical Infection
E ~ Which phase of wound healing accounts for the gain in tensile strength over weeks to months? ~ The maturation and remodelling phase, with replacement of type III by cross-linked type I collagen ~ The inflammatory phase ~ The proliferative phase ~ The haemostatic phase ~ A ~ Tensile strength is only about 10 percent at one week and plateaus at 70 to 80 percent of normal, so a wound never regains its original strength.
E ~ Which classification of surgical wound carries the highest expected infection rate? ~ Dirty or infected, as in surgery on established infection or perforated viscus ~ Clean ~ Clean-contaminated ~ Contaminated ~ A ~ Expected rates rise from under 2 percent for clean wounds to over 25 percent for dirty wounds, which is why the classification guides prophylaxis and consent.
E ~ Which suture material is absorbable, monofilament and suitable for a subcuticular closure? ~ Poliglecaprone or polydioxanone ~ Silk ~ Polypropylene ~ Braided polyester ~ A ~ Monofilament materials harbour fewer bacteria than braided ones, and matching the absorption time to the tissue healing rate governs the choice, with polydioxanone lasting far longer than poliglecaprone.
M ~ Which factor most strongly increases the risk of surgical site infection and is modifiable before elective surgery? ~ Smoking, along with poor glycaemic control, obesity and malnutrition ~ Blood group ~ Male sex ~ Time of day of surgery ~ A ~ Perioperative normothermia, normoglycaemia, appropriate hair removal with clippers and correctly timed antibiotic prophylaxis form the evidence-based bundle for prevention.
M ~ Which timing of antibiotic prophylaxis achieves the lowest surgical site infection rate? ~ Within 60 minutes before skin incision, with redosing for long operations or major blood loss ~ At induction of anaesthesia only for clean cases ~ Immediately after wound closure ~ For 5 days postoperatively ~ A ~ Prolonging prophylaxis beyond 24 hours adds resistance and Clostridioides difficile risk without reducing infection, which is a central stewardship message in surgery.
H ~ Why does a wound heal by secondary intention with more contraction and a worse scar than one closed primarily? ~ The larger tissue defect is filled by granulation tissue with abundant myofibroblasts, which contract the wound margins ~ Collagen is not produced ~ Epithelialisation does not occur ~ Inflammation is absent ~ A ~ This is why contractures form across joints and why grafting or flap cover is used to prevent functional loss in large defects.
