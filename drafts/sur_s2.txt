@ Surgery ~ Gallstones, pancreatitis ~ SUR_GP ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Gallbladder and Bile Ducts
E ~ Which investigation is first line for suspected gallstones, and what is its main limitation? ~ Ultrasonography, which is insensitive for stones in the common bile duct ~ Computed tomography, which misses gallbladder stones ~ Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography, which is unavailable ~ Plain radiography, which shows most stones ~ A ~ Only about 10 to 15 percent of gallstones are radio-opaque, and a dilated duct with deranged liver tests prompts magnetic resonance cholangiography or endoscopic ultrasound.
E ~ Which clinical picture distinguishes acute cholecystitis from biliary colic? ~ Persistent right upper quadrant pain beyond a few hours with fever, leucocytosis and Murphy sign ~ Colicky pain lasting minutes ~ Painless jaundice ~ Pain radiating to the back with vomiting ~ A ~ Biliary colic results from transient cystic duct obstruction without inflammation, whereas persistent obstruction produces inflammation and, if untreated, empyema, gangrene or perforation.
E ~ Which triad describes acute cholangitis, and what is the priority of treatment? ~ Charcot triad of fever with rigors, jaundice and right upper quadrant pain, treated by antibiotics and urgent biliary drainage ~ Reynolds triad, treated with cholecystectomy ~ Courvoisier triad, treated with stenting ~ Grey Turner triad, treated conservatively ~ A ~ Addition of hypotension and confusion gives the Reynolds pentad of suppurative cholangitis, which mandates emergency decompression by endoscopic retrograde cholangiography.
M ~ Which finding indicates that cholecystectomy should be performed during the index admission for mild gallstone pancreatitis? ~ Resolution of the pancreatitis, because delay carries a high risk of recurrent biliary events ~ Presence of a pseudocyst ~ A raised amylase alone ~ Age over 60 ~ A ~ Trials show fewer readmissions with same-admission surgery in mild disease, whereas in severe pancreatitis with collections surgery is deferred for several weeks.
M ~ Which score or marker is used to predict severity in acute pancreatitis within the first 48 hours? ~ Modified Glasgow or APACHE II score with C-reactive protein above 150 mg per litre at 48 hours ~ Serum amylase level ~ Serum lipase level ~ Ranson score at admission only ~ A ~ Enzyme levels correlate with diagnosis rather than severity, whereas persistent organ failure beyond 48 hours defines severe disease under the revised Atlanta classification.
M ~ Which complication of acute pancreatitis presents at 4 weeks or later with a persistent mass and early satiety? ~ A pseudocyst, a collection with a defined wall and no solid debris ~ Acute necrotic collection ~ Walled-off necrosis ~ Pancreatic abscess ~ A ~ Intervention is reserved for symptoms, infection or obstruction rather than size alone, and endoscopic transgastric drainage has largely replaced open surgery.
H ~ Why does Courvoisier's law state that a palpable non-tender gallbladder with jaundice is unlikely to be due to stones? ~ Chronic inflammation from stones makes the gallbladder fibrotic and unable to distend, so painless distension suggests malignant distal obstruction ~ Stones never cause jaundice ~ Malignant obstruction is always painful ~ The gallbladder is never palpable ~ A ~ Exceptions include a stone impacted in the cystic duct with a mucocele and double pathology, which is why it is a guide rather than a rule.
H ~ Why is the critical view of safety sought during laparoscopic cholecystectomy? ~ Demonstrating two and only two structures entering the gallbladder within a cleared hepatocystic triangle prevents misidentification of the common bile duct ~ It shortens operating time ~ It confirms the absence of stones ~ It identifies the cystic artery only ~ A ~ Bile duct injury is the most serious complication and usually results from misidentification rather than from technical failure, which is why this anatomical checkpoint is emphasised.
@ Surgery ~ Head injury & neurosurgical emergencies ~ SUR_HED ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. Neurosurgery
E ~ Which lesion classically presents with a lucid interval after a blow to the temple, and what is its source? ~ Extradural haematoma from the middle meningeal artery ~ Subdural haematoma from bridging veins ~ Subarachnoid haemorrhage from a berry aneurysm ~ Intracerebral haematoma from small vessels ~ A ~ The convex biconvex collection limited by suture lines on computed tomography is characteristic, and prompt evacuation gives an excellent outcome because the underlying brain is often undamaged.
E ~ Which appearance on computed tomography indicates a chronic subdural haematoma? ~ A crescentic hypodense collection crossing suture lines ~ A biconvex hyperdense collection ~ Blood in the basal cisterns ~ Diffuse petechial haemorrhage ~ A ~ It is commonest in the elderly and in alcohol users after trivial trauma, presents with fluctuating confusion or headache, and is drained through burr holes.
E ~ Which measure lowers intracranial pressure most rapidly in a deteriorating head-injured patient? ~ Head elevation with hyperosmolar therapy such as mannitol or hypertonic saline ~ Prophylactic hyperventilation to a carbon dioxide of 25 mmHg ~ High-dose corticosteroid ~ Fluid restriction ~ A ~ Corticosteroids increase mortality in head injury as shown in CRASH, and prolonged aggressive hyperventilation causes ischaemic vasoconstriction, so both are avoided.
M ~ Which cerebral perfusion pressure is generally targeted in severe traumatic brain injury, and how is it calculated? ~ About 60 to 70 mmHg, calculated as mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure ~ Above 100 mmHg ~ Below 50 mmHg ~ Equal to the mean arterial pressure ~ A ~ Because both hypotension and raised intracranial pressure reduce perfusion, avoiding a single episode of hypotension is as important as controlling pressure.
M ~ Which finding on examination indicates uncal herniation and imminent brainstem compression? ~ An ipsilateral fixed dilated pupil with contralateral hemiparesis and deteriorating consciousness ~ Bilateral pinpoint pupils ~ Isolated sixth nerve palsy ~ Nystagmus ~ A ~ The oculomotor nerve is compressed against the tentorial edge, and Kernohan notch phenomenon can produce ipsilateral weakness, which is a well-recognised false localising sign.
H ~ Why is diffuse axonal injury associated with poor outcome despite a normal initial computed tomogram? ~ Shearing of axons at grey-white junctions and the corpus callosum is not visible on early computed tomography but causes widespread functional disconnection ~ It causes massive haemorrhage ~ It always shows on the first scan ~ It is fully reversible ~ A ~ Magnetic resonance imaging with gradient echo and diffusion sequences demonstrates it, and the mismatch between imaging and the depth of coma is the classic clue.
@ Surgery ~ Liver, spleen & portal hypertension surgery ~ SUR_LIV ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Liver and Spleen
E ~ Which vaccination is required before elective splenectomy, and how long in advance? ~ Pneumococcal, meningococcal and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccines, ideally 2 weeks before surgery ~ Hepatitis B alone, on the day of surgery ~ Influenza only ~ No vaccination is required ~ A ~ Lifelong penicillin prophylaxis in children and in the first years after surgery, together with patient education and an emergency antibiotic supply, complete the protection against overwhelming post-splenectomy infection.
E ~ Which imaging finding suggests an amoebic rather than a pyogenic liver abscess? ~ A single lesion in the right lobe in a patient with a travel or dysentery history, with positive amoebic serology ~ Multiple small abscesses with gas ~ A thick-walled calcified cyst ~ A lesion with daughter cysts ~ A ~ Amoebic abscess responds to metronidazole with a luminal agent and rarely needs drainage, whereas pyogenic abscess needs drainage and treatment of the biliary or portal source.
E ~ Which cystic liver lesion must not be aspirated without precautions, and why? ~ Hydatid cyst, because spillage risks anaphylaxis and dissemination ~ Simple cyst ~ Amoebic abscess ~ Pyogenic abscess ~ A ~ The PAIR technique with albendazole cover, or surgery with careful field protection, is used, and the Casoni test is obsolete with serology and imaging making the diagnosis.
M ~ Which operation decompresses portal hypertension while preserving hepatic portal perfusion? ~ A selective distal splenorenal (Warren) shunt ~ A portacaval end-to-side shunt ~ A mesocaval shunt ~ Splenectomy alone ~ A ~ Non-selective shunts decompress effectively but divert portal flow away from the liver, increasing encephalopathy, which is why selective shunts and transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt have largely replaced them.
M ~ Which measure is first line for acute variceal bleeding after resuscitation? ~ Endoscopic band ligation with a vasoactive drug such as terlipressin and antibiotic prophylaxis ~ Immediate surgical shunt ~ Balloon tamponade as definitive treatment ~ Beta blockade alone ~ A ~ Antibiotics reduce mortality independently by preventing bacterial infection, and balloon tamponade is only a bridge for up to 24 hours to definitive treatment.
H ~ Why does splenic injury in a haemodynamically stable patient increasingly not require splenectomy? ~ Non-operative management with observation, and angioembolisation where needed, preserves immune function with good success rates ~ The spleen never bleeds significantly ~ Splenectomy carries no risk ~ Imaging cannot grade injury ~ A ~ Splenic preservation avoids lifelong infection risk, and failure of non-operative management is signalled by haemodynamic instability rather than by the imaging grade alone.
@ Surgery ~ Oesophagus, stomach & gastric carcinoma ~ SUR_OES ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Oesophagus and Stomach
E ~ Which investigation is the gold standard for diagnosing achalasia? ~ High-resolution oesophageal manometry showing failed relaxation of the lower sphincter with absent peristalsis ~ Barium swallow ~ Endoscopy ~ Computed tomography ~ A ~ Barium shows the bird-beak narrowing with a dilated body and endoscopy excludes pseudoachalasia from tumour, but the functional diagnosis and subtype require manometry.
E ~ Which premalignant condition follows chronic gastro-oesophageal reflux, and what does it predispose to? ~ Barrett oesophagus with intestinal metaplasia, predisposing to adenocarcinoma of the lower oesophagus ~ Squamous metaplasia, predisposing to squamous carcinoma ~ Atrophic gastritis, predisposing to lymphoma ~ Plummer-Vinson syndrome, predisposing to adenocarcinoma ~ A ~ Squamous carcinoma is instead linked to smoking, alcohol, achalasia, corrosive stricture and Plummer-Vinson syndrome, and it dominates the upper two thirds of the oesophagus.
E ~ Which sign indicates that a gastric carcinoma has spread to the left supraclavicular node? ~ Virchow node with Troisier sign ~ Sister Mary Joseph nodule ~ Blumer shelf ~ Krukenberg tumour ~ A ~ Each of these eponymous findings indicates incurable disease, with the umbilical nodule, the rectal shelf on digital examination and the bilateral ovarian deposits representing other routes of spread.
M ~ Which operation is appropriate for a carcinoma of the antrum of the stomach, and what lymphadenectomy is standard? ~ Subtotal gastrectomy with a D2 lymphadenectomy ~ Total gastrectomy with D1 dissection ~ Local excision ~ Wedge resection with no nodal clearance ~ A ~ D2 dissection improves staging and, in experienced hands, survival, and a proximal or diffuse tumour requires total gastrectomy to obtain an adequate margin.
M ~ Which complication of gastrectomy presents with sweating, palpitations and diarrhoea within 30 minutes of a meal? ~ Early dumping syndrome from rapid delivery of hyperosmolar chyme into the small bowel ~ Late dumping from reactive hypoglycaemia ~ Afferent loop syndrome ~ Bile reflux gastritis ~ A ~ Late dumping occurs 2 to 3 hours after eating from an exaggerated insulin response, and both are managed with small dry meals low in refined carbohydrate.
H ~ Why is a Helicobacter pylori eradication regimen offered to a patient with gastric MALT lymphoma? ~ Early-stage disease frequently regresses completely after eradication because the lymphoma is antigen driven ~ It prevents transformation to carcinoma only ~ It treats the pain ~ It has no effect on the lymphoma ~ A ~ The t(11;18) translocation predicts failure to respond, so those cases proceed to chemotherapy or radiotherapy rather than relying on antibiotics.
@ Surgery ~ Paediatric surgery: intussusception, pyloric stenosis, hypospadias ~ SUR_PDS ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. Paediatric Surgery
E ~ Which metabolic abnormality must be corrected before pyloromyotomy, and why? ~ Hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, because it causes postoperative apnoea if uncorrected ~ Metabolic acidosis ~ Hypernatraemia ~ Hypocalcaemia ~ A ~ Alkalosis blunts the central respiratory drive, so correction with saline and potassium over 24 to 48 hours is a prerequisite rather than a delay.
E ~ Which imaging confirms hypertrophic pyloric stenosis? ~ Ultrasonography showing a pyloric muscle thickness above 3 mm and channel length above 15 mm ~ Plain radiograph showing a double bubble ~ Barium meal in every case ~ Computed tomography ~ A ~ Ultrasound has replaced contrast studies because it is quick, non-irradiating and directly measures the muscle, and the string sign on contrast is now of historical interest.
E ~ Which principle governs the timing of hypospadias repair? ~ Repair at about 6 to 18 months, before body image awareness and after the penis has grown sufficiently ~ Repair in the neonatal period ~ Repair after puberty ~ Repair only if there is obstruction ~ A ~ Circumcision must be avoided beforehand because the foreskin is used in reconstruction, and associated chordee and cryptorchidism are addressed at the same time.
M ~ Which age and lead point pattern distinguishes idiopathic from pathological intussusception? ~ Idiopathic disease peaks at 6 to 18 months from hypertrophied Peyer patches, whereas beyond 2 years a lead point such as a polyp, Meckel diverticulum or lymphoma is likely ~ Pathological cases are commoner in infants ~ Lead points are never found ~ Age has no relevance ~ A ~ Finding a lead point changes management because reduction alone will be followed by recurrence, so resection of the causative lesion is needed.
M ~ Which finding contraindicates air enema reduction of intussusception? ~ Peritonitis, perforation or haemodynamic instability ~ Duration of symptoms over 12 hours ~ Presence of a palpable mass ~ Age under 1 year ~ A ~ Successful reduction is confirmed by free reflux of air into the small bowel, and recurrence occurs in about 10 percent, most within 72 hours.
H ~ Why is an undescended testis brought down before the age of about 1 year? ~ Germ cell degeneration begins in the first year and early orchidopexy improves fertility and allows surveillance for malignancy ~ It prevents malignancy entirely ~ It is purely cosmetic ~ Spontaneous descent continues until puberty ~ A ~ Orchidopexy does not abolish the increased cancer risk but makes the testis examinable, and a non-palpable testis requires laparoscopic assessment.
@ Surgery ~ Salivary glands, neck swellings & oral cavity carcinoma ~ SUR_NEK ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Salivary Glands and Neck
E ~ Which is the commonest salivary gland tumour, and what is the risk of enucleation rather than formal excision? ~ Pleomorphic adenoma, which recurs because of pseudopodia extending beyond an incomplete capsule ~ Warthin tumour, which never recurs ~ Mucoepidermoid carcinoma ~ Adenoid cystic carcinoma ~ A ~ Superficial parotidectomy with facial nerve preservation is the standard operation, and long-standing tumours carry a small risk of carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma.
E ~ Which malignant salivary tumour is notorious for perineural spread and late distant metastasis? ~ Adenoid cystic carcinoma ~ Mucoepidermoid carcinoma ~ Acinic cell carcinoma ~ Warthin tumour ~ A ~ Its indolent but relentless course means recurrence and pulmonary metastasis may appear a decade later, so long-term follow-up and attention to nerve margins are required.
E ~ Which neck swelling moves upward on protrusion of the tongue, and which operation prevents recurrence? ~ Thyroglossal cyst, treated by the Sistrunk operation removing the tract and the central hyoid ~ Branchial cyst, treated by simple excision ~ Cystic hygroma, treated by sclerotherapy ~ Dermoid cyst, treated by enucleation ~ A ~ Because the tract passes behind the body of the hyoid to the foramen caecum, simple excision leaves it behind and recurrence follows.
M ~ Which nerve is at greatest risk during excision of the submandibular gland, and what is the deficit? ~ The marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve, causing drooping of the corner of the mouth ~ The hypoglossal nerve, causing tongue deviation ~ The lingual nerve, causing loss of taste ~ The glossopharyngeal nerve ~ A ~ The lingual and hypoglossal nerves are also at risk deep in the field, so the incision is placed at least two finger-breadths below the mandible to protect the marginal branch.
M ~ Which factor most influences the management of a squamous carcinoma of the oral cavity with a clinically negative neck? ~ Depth of invasion, since beyond about 4 mm elective neck dissection is indicated because of occult nodal disease ~ Tumour colour ~ Patient age ~ Presence of leukoplakia ~ A ~ The randomised evidence favouring elective over therapeutic neck dissection for depth-positive tumours changed practice, and depth is now part of the staging system.
H ~ Why does a persistent lateral neck lump with cystic squamous cells on aspiration in an adult require investigation of the oropharynx? ~ It is more likely a cystic metastasis from a human papillomavirus-associated tonsillar or base of tongue carcinoma than a branchial cyst ~ Branchial cysts are commonest in adults ~ It is always tuberculous ~ It requires only excision ~ A ~ Excising it as a benign cyst delays diagnosis and compromises later treatment, so panendoscopy with tonsillectomy and imaging is undertaken to find the primary.
