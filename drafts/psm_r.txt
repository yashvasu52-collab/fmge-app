@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Health economics, insurance, Ayushman Bharat & financing ~ PSM_ECO ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Health Planning and Management
E ~ Which term describes health expenditure that pushes a household below the poverty line? ~ Catastrophic health expenditure leading to impoverishment ~ Capitation payment ~ Cost-effectiveness ratio ~ Opportunity cost ~ A ~ Out-of-pocket payment at the point of care is the mechanism, which is why prepayment through tax funding or insurance and free drugs and diagnostics are the policy responses.
E ~ Which measure of disease burden combines years of life lost from premature death with years lived with disability? ~ Disability-adjusted life year ~ Case fatality rate ~ Standardised mortality ratio ~ Proportional mortality ratio ~ A ~ One DALY equals one lost year of healthy life, which allows fatal and non-fatal outcomes to be compared and priorities to be set across very different conditions.
M ~ In an economic evaluation, which analysis expresses both costs and consequences in monetary terms? ~ Cost-benefit analysis ~ Cost-effectiveness analysis ~ Cost-utility analysis ~ Cost-minimisation analysis ~ A ~ Cost-effectiveness uses natural units such as cases prevented, cost-utility uses quality-adjusted life years, and cost-minimisation applies only when outcomes are known to be equivalent.
M ~ Which component of Ayushman Bharat addresses comprehensive primary care rather than hospitalisation? ~ Health and wellness centres delivering an expanded twelve-service package ~ The Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana insurance scheme ~ The National Digital Health Mission ~ Employees State Insurance ~ A ~ The two pillars are deliberately complementary, with the wellness centres intended to reduce the need for hospitalisation that the insurance arm then funds.
M ~ Which financing mechanism pays a provider a fixed amount per enrolled person per period regardless of services used? ~ Capitation ~ Fee-for-service ~ Diagnosis-related group payment ~ Line-item budgeting ~ A ~ Capitation contains cost and encourages prevention but risks under-provision and cherry-picking of healthy enrollees, whereas fee-for-service encourages over-provision.
M ~ Which social insurance scheme in India covers organised-sector workers and their dependants through its own hospitals and dispensaries? ~ Employees State Insurance Scheme ~ Central Government Health Scheme ~ Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana ~ Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY ~ A ~ It is contributory, funded by employer and employee shares, and provides medical care plus cash benefits for sickness, maternity, disablement and dependants, distinguishing it from purely purchasing schemes.
H ~ Why does a high proportion of out-of-pocket expenditure in total health spending indicate a weak health financing system? ~ It shifts financial risk to the individual at the moment of illness, deterring care-seeking and causing impoverishment ~ It reduces total health spending ~ It improves efficiency ~ It guarantees quality ~ A ~ Risk pooling through prepayment is the defining function of a health financing system, so the share of pooled public expenditure is a core indicator of universal health coverage progress.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Health planning, committees & health policy ~ PSM_PLN ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Health Planning and Management
E ~ Which national policy set the target of raising public health expenditure to 2.5 percent of gross domestic product? ~ National Health Policy 2017 ~ National Health Policy 1983 ~ National Population Policy 2000 ~ National Nutrition Policy 1993 ~ A ~ The policy also set the goal of increasing state health spending to more than 8 percent of the state budget and emphasised assured comprehensive primary care as the route to universal health coverage.
E ~ Which committee recommended the creation of the multipurpose health worker by integrating the roles of separate vertical programme workers? ~ Kartar Singh Committee, 1973 ~ Bhore Committee, 1946 ~ Mukherjee Committee, 1965 ~ Bajaj Committee, 1986 ~ A ~ Combining the malaria, smallpox, family planning and other single-purpose workers into one cadre was intended to improve efficiency and coverage at the village level.
E ~ Which planning technique breaks a programme into activities with defined durations to identify the critical path and total time required? ~ Programme evaluation and review technique with critical path method ~ Cost-benefit analysis ~ Systems analysis ~ Network of health indicators ~ A ~ Identifying which activities cannot be delayed without delaying the whole project allows resources to be concentrated where slippage matters most.
M ~ Which step comes first in the health planning cycle? ~ Analysis of the health situation to identify and prioritise problems ~ Implementation of the plan ~ Evaluation of outcomes ~ Allocation of the budget ~ A ~ The cycle proceeds from situation analysis through setting objectives, assessing alternatives, programming, implementation and evaluation, and feeds back into a fresh analysis.
M ~ Which committee recommended the establishment of a University of Health Sciences in every state and the development of a health manpower plan? ~ Bajaj Committee, 1986 ~ Srivastava Committee, 1975 ~ Mudaliar Committee, 1962 ~ Shrivastav Committee, 1981 ~ A ~ The committee addressed the mismatch between the numbers and types of health personnel produced and the needs of the service, proposing an integrated educational and manpower policy.
M ~ Which indicator would best evaluate the process rather than the outcome of an antenatal care programme? ~ Proportion of pregnant women receiving four or more antenatal visits ~ Maternal mortality ratio ~ Low birth weight rate ~ Neonatal mortality rate ~ A ~ Process indicators measure whether activities were delivered as planned and change quickly, whereas outcome indicators reflect the eventual health effect and respond slowly to programme change.
H ~ Why are input and process indicators monitored alongside outcome indicators in a national programme? ~ Outcomes change slowly and are influenced by factors outside the programme, so process measures allow timely correction ~ Outcome indicators are unreliable ~ Input indicators measure health status directly ~ Process indicators are cheaper to fabricate ~ A ~ Attributing a change in mortality to a programme requires long observation and control of confounders, so managers use the logic model of inputs, processes, outputs and outcomes together.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ International health, WHO, SDGs & disaster management ~ PSM_INT ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. International Health
E ~ Which Sustainable Development Goal is specifically concerned with health and wellbeing? ~ Goal 3 ~ Goal 1 ~ Goal 6 ~ Goal 11 ~ A ~ The health goal includes targets for maternal and child mortality, communicable and non-communicable disease, substance use, road injury, universal health coverage and reproductive health, and other goals on poverty, water and inequality act on health determinants.
E ~ Which is the decision-making body of the World Health Organization, meeting annually in Geneva? ~ World Health Assembly ~ Executive Board ~ Secretariat ~ Regional Committee ~ A ~ The Assembly of all member states sets policy and approves the budget, the Executive Board of elected members gives effect to its decisions, and the Secretariat under the Director-General carries out the work.
E ~ In the disaster management cycle, which phase includes hazard mapping, building codes and early warning systems? ~ Mitigation and preparedness, before the event ~ Response ~ Rehabilitation ~ Reconstruction ~ A ~ Investment before an event reduces both mortality and cost far more than response after it, which is the central lesson of modern disaster management.
M ~ Which of the following is the first priority in the immediate response phase after an earthquake? ~ Search and rescue with triage and treatment of the injured, and restoration of safe water ~ Mass immunisation against typhoid and cholera ~ Mass administration of antibiotics ~ Spraying of insecticide ~ A ~ Mass vaccination campaigns in the immediate aftermath divert resources from life-saving activity, and safe water, sanitation, shelter and surveillance prevent the epidemics that vaccination is feared for.
M ~ Which triage category, using the standard colour code, denotes a casualty needing immediate life-saving intervention? ~ Red ~ Yellow ~ Green ~ Black ~ A ~ Yellow marks urgent but delayable care, green marks the walking wounded, and black or blue marks the dead or those with unsurvivable injury, and the aim of triage is the greatest good for the greatest number.
M ~ Which international legal instrument obliges states to detect, assess, notify and respond to public health emergencies of international concern? ~ International Health Regulations 2005 ~ Alma-Ata Declaration ~ Ottawa Charter ~ Framework Convention on Tobacco Control ~ A ~ The regulations shifted from a list of specified diseases to any event that may constitute an emergency, and they require core surveillance and response capacities in every state party.
H ~ Why is disease surveillance strengthened rather than relaxed in the weeks after a natural disaster, even when no outbreak has occurred? ~ Displacement, crowding, damaged water and sanitation and interrupted immunisation raise the risk of outbreaks that early detection can contain ~ Surveillance replaces treatment ~ Outbreaks are inevitable ~ It satisfies donor requirements ~ A ~ The classic post-disaster risks are diarrhoeal disease, measles, acute respiratory infection and vector-borne disease, and early warning systems with rapid response teams are what prevent them becoming epidemics.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Maternal health, RCH & safe motherhood programmes ~ PSM_MAT ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Preventive Medicine in Obstetrics
E ~ How many antenatal visits does the current national guidance recommend as a minimum, and when does the first occur? ~ At least four visits, with the first in the first trimester ~ Two visits, both in the third trimester ~ One visit at term ~ Eight visits, all after 20 weeks ~ A ~ India follows a minimum of four visits with registration in the first trimester, while WHO 2016 guidance recommends eight contacts, and the first-trimester visit permits dating, screening and early risk identification.
E ~ Which scheme provides a conditional cash transfer to promote institutional delivery among poor women? ~ Janani Suraksha Yojana ~ Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram ~ Mid-day Meal Scheme ~ National Deworming Day ~ A ~ Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram complements it by entitling every pregnant woman to free delivery including caesarean, drugs, diagnostics, diet and transport in public facilities.
E ~ Which iron and folic acid regimen is recommended for a non-anaemic pregnant woman under the national programme? ~ One tablet containing 60 mg elemental iron with 500 micrograms folic acid daily for at least 180 days from the second trimester, continued postpartum ~ Two tablets daily throughout pregnancy ~ Weekly supplementation only ~ Iron only in the third trimester ~ A ~ Anaemia Mukt Bharat sets out this schedule with a doubled dose for anaemic women, alongside deworming, testing and behaviour change, because anaemia is a major contributor to maternal death.
M ~ Which cause accounts for the largest share of direct maternal deaths in India? ~ Haemorrhage ~ Sepsis ~ Hypertensive disorders ~ Obstructed labour ~ A ~ Haemorrhage, mostly postpartum, is followed by sepsis and hypertensive disorders, which is why active management of the third stage, skilled attendance and prompt referral are the core interventions.
M ~ Which indicator measures the proportion of deliveries attended by a doctor, nurse or midwife and is a key process indicator for maternal survival? ~ Skilled birth attendance ~ Institutional delivery rate ~ Antenatal registration rate ~ Couple protection rate ~ A ~ Skilled attendance captures the competence available at the moment when most maternal deaths occur, and it is more informative than the place of delivery alone.
M ~ Which programme component addresses the largest remaining gap in maternal survival once institutional delivery is high? ~ Quality of intrapartum and immediate postpartum care, including management of complications and referral ~ Increasing the number of antenatal visits ~ Distribution of iron tablets ~ Family planning counselling ~ A ~ Once women are delivering in facilities, deaths concentrate among those who reach care but do not receive timely, competent management, which is the rationale for LaQshya and the labour room quality initiative.
H ~ Why does the maternal mortality ratio remain a poor tool for monitoring progress in a single district year to year? ~ Maternal death is a rare event so small numbers give wide confidence intervals and unstable estimates ~ It is never measured ~ It excludes indirect deaths ~ It is confounded by fertility ~ A ~ This is why process indicators, facility-based audits and confidential enquiry into maternal deaths are used locally, while the ratio is estimated at state and national level through the Sample Registration System.

@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ National immunization schedule ~ PSM_NIS ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Immunization
E ~ Which vaccines are given at 6, 10 and 14 weeks under the Universal Immunization Programme? ~ Pentavalent, oral polio, rotavirus and pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, with fractional inactivated polio at 6 and 14 weeks ~ BCG, hepatitis B and oral polio ~ Measles-rubella and vitamin A ~ DPT booster and typhoid conjugate ~ A ~ The primary series is compressed into the first months because protection is needed before maternal antibody wanes, and fractional inactivated polio is given intradermally at two of the three visits.
E ~ At what age is the second dose of measles-rubella vaccine given in the national schedule? ~ 16 to 24 months ~ 9 months ~ 5 years ~ 10 years ~ A ~ The second dose covers primary vaccine failure from the 9-month dose, and it is given with the DPT and oral polio boosters and vitamin A at the same visit.
E ~ Which vaccine is given only up to 1 year of age in the national schedule and is not given later? ~ BCG, which may be given up to 1 year if missed ~ Measles-rubella ~ Tetanus toxoid ~ Hepatitis B ~ A ~ Beyond 1 year the likelihood of prior natural infection or established immunity rises and the reaction is more marked, so BCG is not given as catch-up in older children in the programme.
M ~ Which vaccine in the national schedule is given by the intradermal route? ~ BCG and fractional inactivated polio vaccine ~ Pentavalent vaccine ~ Rotavirus vaccine ~ Measles-rubella vaccine ~ A ~ Route errors are a recurrent cause of vaccine failure and adverse events, so knowing that pentavalent and pneumococcal are intramuscular, rotavirus and oral polio are oral, and measles-rubella is subcutaneous, matters practically.
M ~ Which dose of tetanus and adult diphtheria toxoid is recommended for a pregnant woman with no previous immunisation record? ~ Two doses 4 weeks apart, with the second at least 4 weeks before delivery ~ A single dose at term ~ Three doses monthly ~ None if she is over 30 ~ A ~ A woman immunised in a previous pregnancy within the last 3 years receives a single booster, and maternal antibody crossing the placenta is what prevents neonatal tetanus.
M ~ Which adverse event following immunisation is classified as a vaccine product-related reaction rather than a programme error? ~ Fever and local induration after pentavalent vaccine ~ Abscess from an unsterile syringe ~ Toxic shock from a contaminated multi-dose vial ~ Paralysis from injecting the wrong vaccine ~ A ~ Classification into product-related, quality defect, immunisation error, immunisation anxiety and coincidental events determines the corrective action, and errors point to training and logistics rather than to the vaccine itself.
H ~ Why is the open vial policy applied to some vaccines but not to reconstituted BCG and measles-rubella? ~ Reconstituted vaccines contain no preservative and support contamination, so they are discarded within 4 hours or at the end of the session ~ They lose potency within minutes ~ They are single-dose only ~ They are frozen after opening ~ A ~ Preservative-containing liquid vaccines such as oral polio, pentavalent, hepatitis B and tetanus toxoid may be used for up to 28 days if cold chain and labelling conditions are met, which reduces wastage substantially.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Non-communicable disease epidemiology: CVD, diabetes, cancer, tobacco ~ PSM_NCD ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Epidemiology of Chronic Non-communicable Diseases
E ~ Which set of measures forms the WHO best buys for population-level non-communicable disease prevention? ~ Tobacco and alcohol taxation, salt reduction, restriction of trans fats and promotion of physical activity ~ Mass screening of the whole adult population ~ Statins for everyone over 40 ~ Annual whole-body imaging ~ A ~ These interventions are cost-effective and act on the whole distribution of risk, which yields more prevented events than treating only high-risk individuals, the prevention paradox described by Rose.
E ~ Under the national programme, opportunistic screening for hypertension, diabetes and common cancers is offered from what age? ~ 30 years and above, at health and wellness centres ~ 18 years and above ~ 45 years and above ~ 60 years and above ~ A ~ Population-based screening under the NP-NCD covers hypertension, diabetes and oral, breast and cervical cancer, delivered with a community-based assessment checklist for risk stratification.
E ~ Which tobacco control measure has the strongest evidence for reducing consumption, especially among the young? ~ Significant increases in tobacco taxation and price ~ Voluntary industry codes ~ Graphic warnings alone ~ Cessation clinics alone ~ A ~ Demand is price-elastic and more so in adolescents and low-income groups, which is why the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control places taxation first among demand-reduction measures.
M ~ Which cancer is the commonest among Indian women, and what is the principal screening approach in the national programme? ~ Breast cancer, screened by clinical breast examination at the community level ~ Cervical cancer, screened by HPV DNA testing of all women ~ Oral cancer, screened by biopsy ~ Ovarian cancer, screened by CA-125 ~ A ~ Breast cancer has overtaken cervical cancer as the commonest female cancer in India, and the programme uses clinical breast examination with referral because mammographic screening is not feasible at scale.
M ~ Which risk factor accounts for the largest attributable share of oral cancer in India? ~ Smokeless tobacco and areca nut chewing ~ Alcohol alone ~ Human papillomavirus ~ Poor dental hygiene ~ A ~ Because the exposure is visible and the lesion is accessible, oral cancer is one of the few malignancies where community-based visual screening by trained workers has been shown to reduce mortality in high-risk groups.
M ~ Which measure of population salt intake is recommended by WHO as a target to reduce cardiovascular disease? ~ Less than 5 grams of salt, or 2 grams of sodium, per person per day ~ Less than 12 grams per day ~ Less than 10 grams per day ~ There is no recommended limit ~ A ~ Achieving it requires reformulation of processed food and front-of-pack labelling as well as consumer education, because most salt in an increasingly processed diet is added before purchase.
M ~ In the epidemiological transition, what pattern characterises a country like India today? ~ A double burden, with communicable disease still substantial while non-communicable disease and injury dominate overall mortality ~ Communicable disease only ~ Non-communicable disease only ~ Neither, as mortality is dominated by injury ~ A ~ The transition is also unequal within the country, so programmes must simultaneously sustain infectious disease control and build chronic care systems, which is the central challenge of health system design.
H ~ Why does a high-risk approach alone fail to reduce the population burden of cardiovascular disease? ~ Most events arise from the large number of people at modest risk rather than the small number at high risk, the prevention paradox ~ High-risk individuals do not benefit from treatment ~ Risk cannot be measured ~ Population measures are ineffective ~ A ~ Rose's insight is that a small shift in the whole distribution prevents more events than intensive treatment of the tail, which is why population and high-risk approaches are complementary rather than alternatives.
