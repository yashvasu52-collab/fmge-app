@ Surgery ~ Acute abdomen, appendicitis, obstruction ~ SUR_AAA ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Acute Abdomen
M ~ Which scoring system uses clinical features and inflammatory markers to stratify the probability of appendicitis and reduce negative appendicectomy? ~ The Alvarado or AIR score ~ The Glasgow score ~ The Ranson score ~ The Boey score ~ A ~ Low scores permit observation and high scores permit surgery without imaging, while intermediate scores are those in which ultrasound or computed tomography adds most value.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes small bowel from large bowel obstruction on a plain abdominal radiograph? ~ Central dilated loops with valvulae spanning the full width in small bowel, and peripheral loops with haustra not crossing the lumen in large bowel ~ Central loops with haustra ~ Peripheral loops with valvulae ~ Free gas under the diaphragm in small bowel obstruction ~ A ~ Recognising the level directs the search for the cause, since adhesions and hernia dominate small bowel obstruction while carcinoma, volvulus and diverticular disease dominate the large bowel.
H ~ Which finding in a patient with adhesive small bowel obstruction indicates that conservative management should be abandoned? ~ Localised tenderness with fever, tachycardia, leucocytosis or a rising lactate suggesting strangulation ~ Vomiting on the second day ~ Absence of flatus for 24 hours ~ Dilated loops on radiograph ~ A ~ Most adhesive obstruction settles with drip and suck, so the skill lies in detecting the minority with compromised bowel before infarction occurs, and water-soluble contrast both predicts resolution and hastens it.
H ~ Why does a caecal volvulus require operative treatment whereas a sigmoid volvulus is usually first managed endoscopically? ~ The caecum cannot be reliably decompressed or fixed endoscopically, so resection or caecopexy is needed, while a flatus tube decompresses the sigmoid ~ Caecal volvulus never strangulates ~ Sigmoid volvulus cannot be reached endoscopically ~ Both are managed identically ~ A ~ Recurrence after sigmoid decompression is high, so elective sigmoid resection follows during the same admission in a fit patient.
@ Surgery ~ Blood transfusion & coagulation in surgery ~ SUR_TRF ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. Transfusion of Blood
E ~ Which transfusion strategy is supported by evidence in a stable surgical patient without cardiac disease? ~ A restrictive threshold, transfusing at a haemoglobin around 7 to 8 g per dL ~ A liberal threshold of 10 g per dL ~ Transfusing all patients preoperatively ~ Transfusing only whole blood ~ A ~ Restrictive strategies reduce exposure without worsening outcomes in most groups, and the decision should also consider symptoms and ongoing bleeding rather than a number alone.
E ~ Which complication is caused by transfusing large volumes of stored blood rapidly? ~ Hypothermia with hypocalcaemia from citrate, hyperkalaemia and a coagulopathy of dilution ~ Iron overload ~ Delayed haemolysis ~ Graft versus host disease ~ A ~ Warming the blood, monitoring ionised calcium and giving components in balanced ratios in major haemorrhage address these problems, which is why massive transfusion protocols exist.
E ~ Which ratio of components is used in a major haemorrhage protocol for trauma? ~ Approximately equal units of red cells, plasma and platelets ~ Red cells alone until the haemoglobin is corrected ~ Plasma alone ~ Crystalloid until 4 litres have been given ~ A ~ Damage control resuscitation combines balanced components with permissive hypotension, limited crystalloid, tranexamic acid and rapid haemorrhage control.
M ~ Which drug reduces mortality in a bleeding trauma patient if given within 3 hours of injury? ~ Tranexamic acid ~ Recombinant factor VIIa ~ Desmopressin ~ Vitamin K ~ A ~ The CRASH-2 trial showed benefit that was lost and possibly reversed if given later, which is why the time window rather than the laboratory result determines the decision.
M ~ Which laboratory-guided approach reduces blood product use in major surgery? ~ Viscoelastic testing with thromboelastography or rotational thromboelastometry to target the specific defect ~ Empirical plasma for all patients ~ Routine platelet transfusion ~ Fixed-ratio transfusion in all settings ~ A ~ Because it distinguishes fibrinogen deficiency, platelet dysfunction and hyperfibrinolysis at the bedside, it allows targeted rather than blanket component therapy.
H ~ Why does stored red cell transfusion not immediately improve tissue oxygen delivery as much as expected? ~ Depleted 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate shifts the dissociation curve left, so unloading is impaired until it is regenerated over hours ~ Stored cells carry no haemoglobin ~ Viscosity falls ~ Nitric oxide rises ~ A ~ Reduced deformability and the accumulation of microparticles are the other components of the storage lesion, which is one argument for restrictive transfusion.
@ Surgery ~ Breast lumps & carcinoma breast ~ SUR_BLC ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Breast
E ~ Which imaging modality is preferred for evaluating a discrete breast lump in a woman under 35? ~ Ultrasonography, because dense breast tissue limits mammographic sensitivity ~ Mammography ~ Magnetic resonance imaging ~ Computed tomography ~ A ~ Mammography becomes the primary modality after about 40 as the breast becomes fattier, and both are combined with clinical examination and needle biopsy in triple assessment.
E ~ Which axillary procedure has replaced routine clearance for a clinically node-negative breast cancer? ~ Sentinel lymph node biopsy using dye and radioisotope ~ Level III clearance ~ Axillary sampling of four nodes ~ Axillary radiotherapy in all cases ~ A ~ It stages the axilla accurately with far less lymphoedema and shoulder morbidity, and further treatment of the axilla is then guided by the sentinel node result.
E ~ Which patient with breast cancer requires trastuzumab, and what must be monitored? ~ One whose tumour overexpresses HER2, with monitoring of left ventricular ejection fraction ~ One with a triple-negative tumour ~ One with an oestrogen-receptor-positive tumour only ~ One with a BRCA mutation ~ A ~ Cardiotoxicity is usually reversible unlike that of anthracyclines, but the two are not given concurrently because the risk is additive.
M ~ Which factor makes breast-conserving surgery inappropriate and mandates mastectomy? ~ Multicentric disease, a large tumour relative to breast size, or inability to achieve clear margins ~ Any invasive carcinoma ~ Node-positive disease ~ Age under 40 ~ A ~ Because conservation with radiotherapy gives equivalent survival to mastectomy when margins are clear, the decision rests on achieving local control and an acceptable cosmetic result.
M ~ Which condition presents as a lump with skin tethering and a history of trauma or surgery, and can mimic carcinoma on imaging? ~ Fat necrosis ~ Fibroadenoma ~ Duct papilloma ~ Galactocele ~ A ~ Because it can produce a hard irregular mass with microcalcification, biopsy is often required, and it should not be diagnosed on history alone.
M ~ Which endocrine treatment is appropriate for a premenopausal woman with an oestrogen-receptor-positive tumour? ~ Tamoxifen, with ovarian suppression added in higher-risk disease ~ An aromatase inhibitor alone ~ Megestrol ~ No endocrine therapy ~ A ~ Aromatase inhibitors are ineffective while the ovaries respond to rising gonadotropins, so they are used only with ovarian suppression or after the menopause.
M ~ Which presentation of breast cancer carries the worst prognosis and is treated with primary systemic therapy? ~ Inflammatory breast cancer with peau d'orange from dermal lymphatic involvement ~ A 1 cm node-negative tumour ~ Ductal carcinoma in situ ~ Paget disease of the nipple ~ A ~ It is a clinical diagnosis, it is understaged by imaging, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery and radiotherapy is the standard sequence.
H ~ Why does a BRCA1 mutation carrier require different surveillance and risk-reduction advice from a sporadic case? ~ The lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer is high and bilateral, so MRI surveillance and risk-reducing surgery are considered ~ The tumours are less aggressive ~ Screening is unnecessary ~ Only ovarian risk is raised ~ A ~ BRCA1 tumours are typically triple negative and high grade, they respond to platinum agents and PARP inhibitors, and cascade testing of relatives follows identification.
@ Surgery ~ Burns & basics of plastic surgery ~ SUR_BRN ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. Burns
E ~ Which formula estimates the fluid requirement in the first 24 hours after a major burn? ~ The Parkland formula, 4 mL per kg per percent burn of Ringer lactate, half in the first 8 hours ~ 2 mL per kg per percent of colloid ~ 10 mL per kg per percent of saline ~ Maintenance fluid alone ~ A ~ The time is calculated from the moment of injury rather than from admission, and urine output of 0.5 to 1 mL per kg per hour is the practical guide to adequacy.
E ~ Which depth of burn is characterised by blistering with a moist pink painful base and brisk capillary refill? ~ Superficial partial thickness, which heals without grafting ~ Deep partial thickness ~ Full thickness ~ Superficial epidermal ~ A ~ Deep partial thickness burns are drier, mottled and less painful with sluggish refill, and full thickness burns are insensate, leathery and dry, requiring excision and grafting.
E ~ Which feature suggests inhalational injury and mandates early consideration of intubation? ~ Facial burns with singed nasal hair, soot in the oropharynx, hoarseness and stridor ~ Circumferential limb burn ~ Burn over 20 percent surface area alone ~ Carbon monoxide level of 5 percent ~ A ~ Airway oedema progresses over hours and makes later intubation extremely difficult, so a low threshold for early definitive airway control is the safest approach.
M ~ Which procedure relieves the respiratory or circulatory embarrassment of a circumferential full thickness burn? ~ Escharotomy through the inelastic eschar ~ Fasciotomy of the compartment ~ Amputation ~ Skin grafting ~ A ~ Escharotomy addresses the constricting burnt skin whereas fasciotomy addresses raised compartment pressure, and the two are needed in different situations though they may be combined in high-voltage injury.
M ~ Which graft is used to cover a large full thickness burn where donor site is limited? ~ A meshed split thickness autograft, expanding the available donor skin ~ A full thickness graft ~ A free flap ~ A composite graft ~ A ~ Split thickness grafts contract more and give poorer cosmesis than full thickness grafts, which are reserved for the face and hands where quality matters most.
H ~ Why is albumin or colloid generally withheld in the first 8 to 12 hours after a major burn? ~ Capillary permeability is greatly increased so colloid leaks into the interstitium and worsens oedema ~ Colloid causes renal failure ~ Crystalloid is more expensive ~ Colloid causes haemolysis ~ A ~ As permeability recovers after about 12 to 24 hours colloid becomes more useful, which is why some protocols introduce it in the second phase of resuscitation.
@ Surgery ~ Chest trauma & thoracic surgery ~ SUR_CHT ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. Thoracic Trauma
E ~ Which injury is diagnosed clinically and treated before any imaging? ~ Tension pneumothorax, treated by immediate needle or finger thoracostomy ~ Simple pneumothorax ~ Haemothorax ~ Pulmonary contusion ~ A ~ Waiting for a radiograph in a hypotensive patient with absent breath sounds and distended neck veins risks cardiac arrest, so decompression precedes confirmation.
E ~ Which finding on chest drain output after insertion indicates the need for thoracotomy in haemothorax? ~ An immediate drainage of more than 1500 mL, or more than 200 mL per hour for 2 to 4 hours ~ Any blood in the drain ~ Air leak for 24 hours ~ Drainage of 500 mL over 24 hours ~ A ~ These thresholds identify ongoing surgical bleeding from a systemic vessel or the heart, which will not stop with drainage alone.
E ~ Which chest injury produces paradoxical movement of a segment of the chest wall? ~ Flail chest from segmental fractures of three or more adjacent ribs ~ Simple rib fracture ~ Sternal fracture ~ Open pneumothorax ~ A ~ The underlying pulmonary contusion rather than the mechanical instability usually determines outcome, and management centres on analgesia, physiotherapy and ventilatory support with surgical fixation in selected cases.
M ~ Which injury should be suspected in a patient with blunt chest trauma, widened mediastinum and a fractured first rib? ~ Traumatic aortic injury, investigated by contrast computed tomography angiography ~ Cardiac tamponade ~ Tracheobronchial injury ~ Oesophageal rupture ~ A ~ The commonest site is just distal to the left subclavian at the ligamentum arteriosum, and blood pressure and heart rate control precede definitive endovascular or open repair.
M ~ Which finding suggests tracheobronchial injury after blunt trauma? ~ A large persistent air leak with pneumomediastinum and failure of the lung to re-expand after drainage ~ A small apical pneumothorax ~ Subcutaneous emphysema alone ~ Haemothorax ~ A ~ Bronchoscopy confirms the site, and repair is usually required because a major airway disruption will not heal with drainage alone.
H ~ Why does a patient with cardiac tamponade from penetrating trauma have a normal chest radiograph? ~ The acute pericardium does not distend, so a small volume raises pressure without enlarging the cardiac silhouette ~ Blood is radiolucent ~ The film is taken supine ~ Tamponade never occurs in trauma ~ A ~ This is why the diagnosis rests on clinical signs and focused ultrasound, and why pericardiocentesis of even 20 mL can be life-saving as a bridge to thoracotomy.
@ Surgery ~ Colon, rectum & anal canal disorders ~ SUR_COL ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. The Colon, Rectum and Anus
E ~ Which condition presents with severe pain on defaecation with bright bleeding and a sentinel pile at the posterior midline? ~ Anal fissure ~ Haemorrhoids ~ Perianal abscess ~ Rectal carcinoma ~ A ~ Internal sphincter hypertonia maintains the fissure, so treatment aims to relax it with a topical nitrate or calcium channel blocker, with botulinum toxin or lateral sphincterotomy for chronic cases.
E ~ Which classification describes haemorrhoids that prolapse on straining and reduce spontaneously? ~ Second degree ~ First degree ~ Third degree ~ Fourth degree ~ A ~ First degree bleed without prolapse, third degree require manual reduction and fourth degree are irreducible, and the grade guides the choice between banding, sclerotherapy and haemorrhoidectomy.
E ~ Which finding indicates a fistula in ano rather than a simple perianal abscess? ~ A chronic discharging external opening with an internal opening in the anal canal ~ Acute painful swelling ~ Fever with leucocytosis ~ Fluctuant tender mass ~ A ~ Goodsall's rule predicts the tract, though it is unreliable posteriorly, and magnetic resonance imaging is used for complex or recurrent fistulae, especially in Crohn disease.
M ~ Which operation is appropriate for an obstructing carcinoma of the sigmoid colon in an unstable patient? ~ Hartmann procedure with resection, end colostomy and closure of the rectal stump ~ Primary anastomosis without stoma ~ Defunctioning loop ileostomy alone ~ Abdominoperineal resection ~ A ~ Avoiding an anastomosis in an unprepared, contaminated and physiologically compromised patient removes the risk of leak, with reversal considered later once the patient has recovered.
M ~ Which margin determines whether a rectal cancer requires abdominoperineal resection rather than anterior resection? ~ Involvement of or proximity to the sphincter complex in a very low tumour ~ Nodal status ~ Tumour grade ~ Length of the specimen ~ A ~ Total mesorectal excision with an adequate circumferential resection margin is the oncological principle in both operations, and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy may permit sphincter preservation.
H ~ Why is a defunctioning stoma often created after a low anterior resection? ~ It does not prevent an anastomotic leak but reduces the septic consequences if one occurs ~ It prevents leak entirely ~ It improves oncological clearance ~ It is required for continence ~ A ~ Because the low pelvic anastomosis has the highest leak rate and the most serious consequences, diversion is a damage-limitation measure rather than a preventive one.
@ Surgery ~ Fluid, electrolyte & nutritional support in surgery ~ SUR_FLU ~ Bailey & Love's Short Practice of Surgery 27e, Ch. Metabolic Response to Injury and Nutritional Support
E ~ Which route of nutritional support is preferred whenever the gut is usable, and why? ~ Enteral, because it maintains mucosal integrity, reduces bacterial translocation and costs less with fewer complications ~ Parenteral, because absorption is guaranteed ~ Peripheral parenteral ~ Intravenous dextrose alone ~ A ~ Even small trophic volumes preserve villous height and gut-associated lymphoid tissue, which is why the maxim if the gut works use it governs practice.
E ~ Which electrolyte abnormality should be anticipated when feeding is started in a severely malnourished surgical patient? ~ Hypophosphataemia with hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia of the refeeding syndrome ~ Hypernatraemia ~ Hypercalcaemia ~ Metabolic alkalosis ~ A ~ Insulin release drives these ions intracellularly for phosphorylation, so calories are increased gradually with thiamine and daily electrolyte monitoring in the first days.
E ~ Which fluid is inappropriate as the sole maintenance fluid in a postoperative adult, and why? ~ Five percent dextrose alone, because it provides no sodium and risks dilutional hyponatraemia ~ Balanced crystalloid ~ Ringer lactate ~ Dextrose saline with potassium ~ A ~ The postoperative state with raised vasopressin impairs free water excretion, so hypotonic fluid in excess is a recognised cause of avoidable hyponatraemia and cerebral oedema.
M ~ Which metabolic change characterises the catabolic ebb and flow phases of the response to major surgery? ~ An initial hypometabolic ebb phase followed by a hypermetabolic catabolic flow phase with negative nitrogen balance ~ Sustained anabolism ~ Immediate hypermetabolism only ~ No change in metabolic rate ~ A ~ Understanding the timing explains why aggressive feeding in the earliest phase is unhelpful and why enhanced recovery protocols aim to blunt the response with analgesia, early feeding and mobilisation.
M ~ Which complication is specific to central parenteral nutrition and requires vigilant prevention? ~ Catheter-related bloodstream infection ~ Aspiration pneumonia ~ Diarrhoea ~ Tube blockage ~ A ~ A dedicated lumen, strict aseptic handling and prompt removal when no longer needed reduce it, and metabolic complications include hyperglycaemia, hepatic steatosis and electrolyte disturbance.
H ~ Why does the classic starvation response differ from the response to sepsis or major injury? ~ In starvation ketone adaptation spares protein, whereas the cytokine-driven catabolism of injury continues to break down muscle despite feeding ~ Both spare protein equally ~ Sepsis causes greater ketosis ~ Starvation causes greater protein loss ~ A ~ This is why nutritional support in critical illness attenuates but cannot abolish loss of lean mass, and why control of the underlying insult remains the priority.
