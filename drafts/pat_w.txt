@ Pathology ~ Anemias (micro/macro/hemolytic) ~ PAT_AMM ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Red Blood Cell Disorders
M ~ A patient with chronic rheumatoid arthritis has a haemoglobin of 9 g per dL with a mean corpuscular volume of 78, a ferritin of 180 and a low transferrin saturation. The mechanism is: ~ Hepcidin-mediated sequestration of iron in macrophages with reduced erythropoietin response ~ True iron deficiency from blood loss ~ Marrow infiltration ~ Haemolysis ~ A ~ Interleukin-6 drives hepatic hepcidin, which degrades ferroportin so iron cannot leave enterocytes or macrophages, giving a normal or high ferritin with restricted delivery to the marrow.
M ~ Which peripheral smear finding indicates a mechanical or microangiopathic cause of haemolysis? ~ Schistocytes with thrombocytopenia and a raised lactate dehydrogenase ~ Spherocytes with a positive Coombs test ~ Target cells with basophilic stippling ~ Bite cells with Heinz bodies ~ A ~ Fragmentation on fibrin strands or a mechanical valve gives this picture, which points to thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, haemolytic uraemic syndrome, disseminated intravascular coagulation or malignant hypertension.
H ~ Which laboratory pattern distinguishes intravascular from extravascular haemolysis? ~ Low haptoglobin with haemoglobinuria and haemosiderinuria in intravascular haemolysis, and splenomegaly with urobilinogen in extravascular ~ Raised bilirubin only in intravascular ~ Reticulocytosis only in extravascular ~ Raised lactate dehydrogenase only in extravascular ~ A ~ Free plasma haemoglobin saturates haptoglobin and is filtered, so measuring haptoglobin and urinary haemosiderin localises the site and narrows the differential.
H ~ Why does a patient with severe pernicious anaemia sometimes have a normal or only mildly raised mean corpuscular volume? ~ Coexisting iron deficiency or thalassaemia trait produces a mixed picture with a raised red cell distribution width ~ B12 deficiency does not affect cell size ~ The marrow becomes hypoplastic ~ Reticulocytosis lowers the volume ~ A ~ Because the two populations average out, the distribution width and the blood film showing both microcytes and macrocytes with hypersegmented neutrophils reveal the dimorphic picture.
@ Pathology ~ Bleeding & coagulation disorders ~ PAT_BCD ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Hemostasis and Bleeding Disorders
M ~ Which pattern of bleeding suggests a platelet or vessel wall disorder rather than a coagulation factor deficiency? ~ Petechiae, purpura and mucosal bleeding immediately after injury ~ Deep muscle haematoma with delayed bleeding ~ Recurrent haemarthrosis ~ Retroperitoneal bleeding ~ A ~ Primary haemostasis controls the initial plug, so its failure gives immediate superficial bleeding, whereas factor deficiency permits the plug to form and then break down, giving delayed deep bleeding.
M ~ A patient has a prolonged bleeding time with a normal platelet count and normal prothrombin and partial thromboplastin times, and platelet aggregation fails with ristocetin but is normal with ADP. The diagnosis is: ~ Von Willebrand disease or Bernard-Soulier syndrome affecting adhesion ~ Glanzmann thrombasthenia ~ Haemophilia A ~ Factor XIII deficiency ~ A ~ Ristocetin tests the von Willebrand factor and GpIb adhesion axis, so a selective failure there localises the defect, and adding normal plasma corrects von Willebrand disease but not Bernard-Soulier.
M ~ Which laboratory picture indicates disseminated intravascular coagulation? ~ Prolonged prothrombin and partial thromboplastin times with low fibrinogen, low platelets and raised D-dimer ~ Isolated prolonged partial thromboplastin time ~ Isolated thrombocytopenia with normal clotting times ~ Raised fibrinogen with normal platelets ~ A ~ Simultaneous consumption of factors and platelets with secondary fibrinolysis is the signature, and management is directed at the precipitating cause with product support.
M ~ Which inherited thrombophilia results from a mutation making factor V resistant to inactivation? ~ Factor V Leiden, causing activated protein C resistance ~ Antithrombin deficiency ~ Protein S deficiency ~ Prothrombin G20210A mutation ~ A ~ Although it is the commonest inherited thrombophilia in people of European descent, the relative risk per carrier is modest, so it rarely alters the duration of anticoagulation by itself.
H ~ Why does heparin-induced thrombocytopenia cause thrombosis rather than bleeding despite a falling platelet count? ~ Antibody to the heparin-platelet factor 4 complex activates platelets and generates procoagulant microparticles ~ Heparin directly activates thrombin ~ Platelets are destroyed in the spleen ~ Fibrinolysis is inhibited ~ A ~ The paradox demands stopping all heparin and starting a non-heparin anticoagulant such as argatroban rather than transfusing platelets, which may worsen thrombosis.
H ~ Why does liver disease produce a prolonged prothrombin time that does not necessarily indicate a bleeding tendency? ~ Both procoagulant and anticoagulant factor synthesis fall, so haemostasis is rebalanced at a lower level ~ The test is technically invalid in liver disease ~ Fibrinogen rises ~ Platelet function is enhanced ~ A ~ This is why the international normalised ratio predicts prognosis in liver disease better than bleeding risk, and why prophylactic plasma before procedures is now discouraged.
@ Pathology ~ Bone & soft tissue tumours ~ PAT_BON ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Bones, Joints and Soft Tissue Tumors
E ~ Which bone tumour arises in the metaphysis of an adolescent and shows malignant cells producing osteoid with a sunburst periosteal reaction? ~ Osteosarcoma ~ Ewing sarcoma ~ Chondrosarcoma ~ Giant cell tumour ~ A ~ Loss of RB1 or TP53 predisposes, which is why survivors of hereditary retinoblastoma have a greatly increased risk, and the lung is the usual site of metastasis.
E ~ Which soft tissue sarcoma is the commonest in adults and typically arises in the retroperitoneum or thigh with lipoblasts on histology? ~ Liposarcoma ~ Rhabdomyosarcoma ~ Synovial sarcoma ~ Angiosarcoma ~ A ~ The well-differentiated subtype is locally aggressive with a low metastatic rate whereas the dedifferentiated and pleomorphic subtypes behave far more aggressively, so subtyping guides management.
M ~ Which small round blue cell tumour of bone is CD99 positive with an onion-skin periosteal reaction and a t(11;22) translocation? ~ Ewing sarcoma ~ Osteosarcoma ~ Lymphoma of bone ~ Chondroblastoma ~ A ~ The EWSR1-FLI1 fusion is diagnostic, and periodic acid-Schiff-positive intracytoplasmic glycogen with a diaphyseal location distinguishes it from the metaphyseal osteosarcoma.
M ~ Which soft tissue tumour of childhood shows rhabdomyoblasts with desmin and myogenin positivity, and which subtype has the worst prognosis? ~ Rhabdomyosarcoma, with the alveolar subtype carrying a worse prognosis than the embryonal ~ Fibrosarcoma, with the infantile subtype worst ~ Synovial sarcoma ~ Leiomyosarcoma ~ A ~ The alveolar subtype carries PAX3 or PAX7 fusions with FOXO1, whereas the embryonal subtype including the botryoid vaginal variant does better, so molecular typing is prognostically important.
M ~ Which lesion is a benign fibro-osseous proliferation with a ground-glass radiographic appearance and Chinese-letter trabeculae on histology? ~ Fibrous dysplasia ~ Osteoid osteoma ~ Non-ossifying fibroma ~ Paget disease ~ A ~ A GNAS1 mutation underlies it, and the polyostotic form with cafe-au-lait macules and endocrinopathy constitutes McCune-Albright syndrome.
H ~ Which histological feature distinguishes a chondrosarcoma from an enchondroma? ~ Permeation of bone marrow spaces and entrapment of pre-existing trabeculae with increased cellularity and binucleate cells ~ Presence of any cartilage ~ Calcification ~ A well-defined margin ~ A ~ Because low-grade chondrosarcoma and enchondroma overlap cytologically, the pattern of invasion together with the clinical and radiological context, particularly axial location and pain, determines the diagnosis.
H ~ Why does Paget disease of bone predispose to osteosarcoma, and how does it present? ~ Markedly increased bone turnover with disorganised remodelling permits malignant transformation, presenting as new pain or a mass in a previously affected bone ~ It causes only fractures ~ Transformation is to chondrosarcoma ~ Turnover is reduced ~ A ~ Sarcomatous change occurs in about 1 percent, and a mosaic pattern of lamellar bone with a markedly raised alkaline phosphatase characterises the underlying disease.
@ Pathology ~ Breast pathology ~ PAT_BRE ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. The Breast
E ~ Which benign breast lesion is a well-circumscribed mobile mass in a young woman with a biphasic proliferation of stroma and glands? ~ Fibroadenoma ~ Phyllodes tumour ~ Intraductal papilloma ~ Fat necrosis ~ A ~ It is hormone responsive so it may enlarge in pregnancy and regress after the menopause, and it carries essentially no increased cancer risk unless complex features are present.
E ~ Which breast lesion presents with a bloody nipple discharge and is usually a solitary lesion in a large duct? ~ Intraductal papilloma ~ Fibroadenoma ~ Fibrocystic change ~ Duct ectasia ~ A ~ Solitary large-duct papillomas carry little increased risk, whereas multiple peripheral papillomas are associated with a modest increase, so the site and number matter.
M ~ Which histological type of invasive breast carcinoma characteristically loses E-cadherin and infiltrates in single files without forming a discrete mass? ~ Invasive lobular carcinoma ~ Invasive ductal carcinoma ~ Mucinous carcinoma ~ Medullary carcinoma ~ A ~ Loss of the adhesion molecule explains both the diffuse growth that makes it hard to detect on mammography and its tendency to be bilateral and multicentric, and it metastasises to unusual sites such as the peritoneum.
M ~ Which receptor profile defines triple-negative breast carcinoma, and what is its therapeutic implication? ~ Negative for oestrogen and progesterone receptors and for HER2, so endocrine and anti-HER2 therapy are ineffective ~ Positive for all three ~ Negative for HER2 only ~ Positive for oestrogen receptor only ~ A ~ These tumours are commoner in younger women and in BRCA1 carriers, are managed with chemotherapy including platinum agents, and immunotherapy and PARP inhibitors now have a role.
M ~ Which in-situ lesion is characterised by comedo necrosis with linear branching calcification on mammography? ~ High-grade ductal carcinoma in situ ~ Lobular carcinoma in situ ~ Atypical ductal hyperplasia ~ Sclerosing adenosis ~ A ~ Lobular carcinoma in situ is usually an incidental finding without calcification and is a risk marker for cancer in either breast, whereas ductal carcinoma in situ is a direct precursor at the same site.
H ~ Which pathological finding explains the clinical picture of inflammatory breast carcinoma? ~ Tumour emboli obstructing dermal lymphatics, producing peau d'orange and erythema without a discrete mass ~ Bacterial infection of the breast ~ Fat necrosis with inflammation ~ Ductal ectasia with periductal inflammation ~ A ~ It is a clinical and pathological diagnosis rather than a histological subtype, carries a poor prognosis and is treated with neoadjuvant systemic therapy before surgery.
H ~ Why does Paget disease of the nipple almost always indicate an underlying carcinoma? ~ Malignant ductal cells migrate along the ductal epithelium to the nipple skin, so an in-situ or invasive carcinoma is nearly always present ~ It is a primary skin disease ~ It is caused by chronic eczema ~ It is a benign reactive change ~ A ~ Therefore a persistent unilateral eczematous nipple change requires full-thickness biopsy and breast imaging rather than treatment with topical steroid.
@ Pathology ~ Carcinogenesis, molecular basis & metastasis ~ PAT_CAR ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Neoplasia
E ~ Which class of gene requires only a single activating mutation to contribute to malignancy? ~ Proto-oncogene, which is dominant at the cellular level ~ Tumour suppressor gene ~ DNA mismatch repair gene ~ Caretaker gene ~ A ~ Tumour suppressors generally require loss of both alleles by the two-hit hypothesis, so their inheritance pattern in cancer syndromes is dominant at the level of the individual but recessive at the level of the cell.
E ~ Which viral infection is causally linked to hepatocellular carcinoma through chronic inflammation and integration? ~ Hepatitis B virus ~ Hepatitis A virus ~ Epstein-Barr virus ~ Human herpesvirus 8 ~ A ~ Hepatitis C also causes hepatocellular carcinoma but almost always through cirrhosis, whereas hepatitis B can cause it in a non-cirrhotic liver because of integration and the effects of the HBx protein.
M ~ Which molecular abnormality defines the mismatch repair deficient or microsatellite unstable phenotype, and what is its therapeutic relevance? ~ Loss of MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 or PMS2 function producing a high mutational burden that predicts response to immune checkpoint inhibition ~ Amplification of HER2 ~ Loss of RB1 ~ BCR-ABL1 fusion ~ A ~ Lynch syndrome results from germline mutation in the same genes, and testing every colorectal and endometrial cancer for mismatch repair status now serves both a familial and a therapeutic purpose.
M ~ Which step of the metastatic cascade is rate-limiting and explains why most circulating tumour cells fail to form metastases? ~ Successful colonisation of the distant site, requiring survival and growth in a foreign microenvironment ~ Detachment from the primary ~ Intravasation into vessels ~ Survival in the circulation ~ A ~ The seed and soil hypothesis captures this dependence on a permissive niche, which is why organ tropism follows patterns not explained by blood flow alone.
M ~ Which change in cellular metabolism is characteristic of malignant cells even in the presence of oxygen? ~ Aerobic glycolysis with lactate production, the Warburg effect ~ Increased oxidative phosphorylation ~ Reduced glucose uptake ~ Increased beta-oxidation ~ A ~ Diverting glucose into biosynthetic intermediates supports rapid proliferation, and increased glucose uptake is what makes fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography useful in staging.
H ~ Which mechanism allows a cancer cell to divide indefinitely despite progressive telomere shortening? ~ Reactivation of telomerase, or an alternative lengthening of telomeres pathway ~ Loss of the mitotic spindle checkpoint ~ Increased DNA repair fidelity ~ Reduced apoptosis alone ~ A ~ Limitless replicative potential is one of the recognised hallmarks of cancer, and telomerase is absent from most normal somatic cells, which is why it is an attractive but difficult therapeutic target.
H ~ Why does the field effect explain multiple primary tumours in the aerodigestive tract of a smoker? ~ Widespread carcinogen exposure produces independent genetic changes across a large mucosal field, so several primaries arise separately ~ All tumours are metastases from one primary ~ It reflects a germline mutation ~ It is due to viral spread ~ A ~ This is why surveillance after treatment of one head and neck or lung cancer must cover the whole exposed field, and why smoking cessation reduces the risk of a second primary.
@ Pathology ~ Cell injury, adaptation, necrosis vs apoptosis ~ PAT_CIA ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Cell Injury and Adaptation
E ~ Which type of necrosis characterises infarction of the brain, in contrast to other solid organs? ~ Liquefactive necrosis, because of high lipid content and abundant lysosomal enzymes with little supporting stroma ~ Coagulative necrosis ~ Caseous necrosis ~ Fibrinoid necrosis ~ A ~ Elsewhere ischaemia produces coagulative necrosis in which the tissue architecture is preserved for days, which is why the pattern of necrosis identifies the organ and the mechanism.
E ~ Which adaptive response describes the replacement of one differentiated cell type with another, as in Barrett oesophagus? ~ Metaplasia ~ Hyperplasia ~ Hypertrophy ~ Dysplasia ~ A ~ Metaplasia is reversible and represents reprogramming of stem cells, but the new environment predisposes to dysplasia and carcinoma if the stimulus persists.
E ~ Which morphological feature distinguishes apoptosis from necrosis on light microscopy? ~ Cell shrinkage with chromatin condensation and an intact membrane, without inflammation ~ Cell swelling with membrane rupture ~ Neutrophilic infiltration ~ Dystrophic calcification ~ A ~ Because the membrane remains intact and the fragments are phagocytosed, apoptosis provokes no inflammatory response, which is central to its role in development and homeostasis.
M ~ Which intracellular change marks the transition from reversible to irreversible cell injury? ~ Severe membrane damage with mitochondrial permeability transition and massive calcium influx ~ Cellular swelling ~ Detachment of ribosomes from the endoplasmic reticulum ~ Formation of membrane blebs ~ A ~ Cytosolic calcium activates phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases and ATPases, and once the mitochondrial pore opens ATP generation ceases irrecoverably.
M ~ Which mechanism explains reperfusion injury after restoration of blood flow to ischaemic tissue? ~ Generation of reactive oxygen species with calcium overload, neutrophil influx and complement activation ~ Continued hypoxia ~ Reduced substrate delivery ~ Loss of glycolysis ~ A ~ Paradoxically, restoring oxygen to cells with damaged mitochondria produces a burst of free radicals, which is why antioxidant and complement-directed strategies have been explored in reperfusion.
M ~ Which type of calcification occurs in normal tissue in the presence of hypercalcaemia, as in hyperparathyroidism or vitamin D excess? ~ Metastatic calcification, favouring the stomach, kidney and lung because of their alkaline interstitium ~ Dystrophic calcification ~ Amyloid deposition ~ Lipofuscin accumulation ~ A ~ Dystrophic calcification instead occurs in dead or dying tissue with a normal serum calcium, as in an atheromatous plaque or a calcified tuberculous focus.
H ~ Which pathway of apoptosis is triggered by loss of survival signals and DNA damage, and how is it regulated? ~ The intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, controlled by the balance of BCL2 family proteins releasing cytochrome c ~ The extrinsic pathway via Fas ~ The perforin-granzyme pathway ~ The caspase-independent pathway ~ A ~ Overexpression of BCL2 in follicular lymphoma through the t(14;18) translocation prevents this release, which is why it prolongs cell survival rather than accelerating division.
H ~ Why does brown atrophy of the heart in an elderly patient show lipofuscin accumulation? ~ Lipofuscin is an indigestible polymer of peroxidised lipid and protein that accumulates in lysosomes with age and free radical injury ~ It represents iron deposition ~ It is a product of glycation ~ It is intracellular calcium ~ A ~ It is a marker rather than a cause of injury, and its accumulation in heart, liver and neurons gives the organ a brown colour, distinguishing it from haemosiderin which stains with Prussian blue.
@ Pathology ~ Cytopathology, IHC, autopsy & lab techniques ~ PAT_CYT ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Laboratory Techniques and Diagnostic Pathology
E ~ Which immunohistochemical marker is used to confirm epithelial origin in a tumour of uncertain lineage? ~ Cytokeratin ~ Vimentin ~ S100 ~ Leukocyte common antigen ~ A ~ Vimentin marks mesenchymal cells, S100 marks melanocytes and nerve sheath, and leukocyte common antigen marks lymphoid cells, so a small panel can separate carcinoma, sarcoma, melanoma and lymphoma.
E ~ Which stain is used to demonstrate amyloid, and what appearance confirms it? ~ Congo red, giving apple-green birefringence under polarised light ~ Periodic acid-Schiff, giving magenta staining ~ Prussian blue, giving blue granules ~ Masson trichrome, giving green collagen ~ A ~ The birefringence reflects the beta-pleated sheet structure common to all amyloid proteins, and typing then requires immunohistochemistry or mass spectrometry.
E ~ Which fixative is standard for routine histopathology, and what is its principal action? ~ Ten percent neutral buffered formalin, which cross-links proteins ~ Absolute alcohol, which dehydrates ~ Bouin fluid, which is used for all specimens ~ Glutaraldehyde, used for light microscopy ~ A ~ Glutaraldehyde is reserved for electron microscopy, and prolonged or inadequate formalin fixation degrades antigens and nucleic acids, which compromises immunohistochemistry and molecular testing.
M ~ Which cytological reporting system uses categories from non-diagnostic through to malignant with implied risks of malignancy for thyroid nodules? ~ The Bethesda system for reporting thyroid cytopathology ~ The Ridley-Jopling classification ~ The Gleason system ~ The Ann Arbor system ~ A ~ Attaching a malignancy risk to each category allows the report to guide management directly, which is the same principle used in the Paris system for urine and the Milan system for salivary gland.
M ~ Which marker distinguishes a lymphoid proliferation as clonal and therefore likely neoplastic? ~ Immunoglobulin light chain restriction to kappa or lambda, or a clonal receptor gene rearrangement ~ Expression of CD45 ~ Presence of tingible body macrophages ~ A high mitotic rate ~ A ~ Reactive proliferations remain polyclonal, so demonstrating restriction by flow cytometry or molecular studies is the pivotal step in separating hyperplasia from lymphoma.
M ~ Which technique detects a specific gene fusion in a paraffin section using labelled probes? ~ Fluorescence in situ hybridisation ~ Immunohistochemistry ~ Western blotting ~ Flow cytometry ~ A ~ It is used routinely to confirm HER2 amplification in breast cancer and ALK rearrangement in lung cancer, and it preserves tissue architecture so the abnormal cells can be localised.
H ~ Why is a medicolegal autopsy performed with a complete rather than a limited dissection even when the cause of death seems obvious? ~ Findings that alter the interpretation of the manner of death may be present elsewhere, and the examination must be able to withstand challenge in court ~ It is faster ~ Organs cannot be examined selectively ~ Consent requires it ~ A ~ Documentation, photography, chain of custody and retention of samples for toxicology and histology are as important as the dissection itself, because the report is evidence rather than merely a clinical record.
