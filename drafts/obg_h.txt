@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Genital infections, PID & STIs in women ~ OBG_PID ~ Shaw's Textbook of Gynaecology 17e, Ch. Infections of the Genital Tract
E ~ A woman has a thin grey homogeneous vaginal discharge with a pH of 5.5, a positive whiff test and clue cells on microscopy. The diagnosis is: ~ Bacterial vaginosis ~ Vulvovaginal candidiasis ~ Trichomoniasis ~ Atrophic vaginitis ~ A ~ Amsel criteria require three of four features, and the condition reflects replacement of lactobacilli by anaerobes and Gardnerella rather than a true inflammatory vaginitis, which is why there is little itch or erythema.
E ~ A woman has a profuse frothy yellow-green discharge with vulval itching and a strawberry cervix. The treatment must include: ~ Oral metronidazole for the patient and her sexual partner ~ Topical clotrimazole ~ Oral fluconazole ~ Topical oestrogen ~ A ~ Trichomonas vaginalis is a sexually transmitted flagellate that also colonises the urethra and Skene glands, so topical treatment fails and partner treatment is essential to prevent reinfection.
E ~ Which organism most commonly causes vulvovaginal candidiasis and produces pseudohyphae on a potassium hydroxide mount? ~ Candida albicans ~ Candida glabrata ~ Candida krusei ~ Saccharomyces cerevisiae ~ A ~ Candida albicans accounts for the great majority and responds to azoles, whereas Candida glabrata forms no pseudohyphae and is often azole-resistant, requiring boric acid or nystatin.
M ~ A young woman has lower abdominal pain, cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness and a temperature of 38.2 degrees. Which organisms must the empirical regimen cover? ~ Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis and anaerobes ~ Candida and Gardnerella ~ Escherichia coli alone ~ Mycobacterium tuberculosis ~ A ~ Pelvic inflammatory disease is polymicrobial, so regimens combine ceftriaxone with doxycycline and metronidazole, and treatment is started on clinical suspicion because delay causes tubal damage.
M ~ A woman with pelvic inflammatory disease develops right upper quadrant pain, and laparoscopy shows violin-string adhesions between the liver and the diaphragm. This is: ~ Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome ~ Acute cholecystitis ~ Subphrenic abscess ~ Budd-Chiari syndrome ~ A ~ Perihepatitis from transperitoneal spread of chlamydial or gonococcal infection produces the characteristic adhesions and can be mistaken for biliary disease.
M ~ A woman with a tubo-ovarian abscess of 7 cm fails to improve after 72 hours of intravenous antibiotics. The appropriate step is: ~ Image-guided drainage or laparoscopic surgery ~ Change to oral antibiotics ~ Continue the same antibiotics for a further week ~ Perform total abdominal hysterectomy ~ A ~ Abscesses larger than about 5 cm often fail antibiotics alone; drainage preserves fertility, whereas hysterectomy is reserved for rupture with peritonitis in women who have completed childbearing.
M ~ Which sexually transmitted infection causes painful genital ulcers with tender suppurative inguinal nodes and school-of-fish organisms on Gram stain? ~ Chancroid due to Haemophilus ducreyi ~ Primary syphilis ~ Lymphogranuloma venereum ~ Donovanosis ~ A ~ The ragged undermined ulcer with painful buboes that may rupture through a single sinus, and the pleomorphic Gram-negative rods in chains, characterise chancroid; treatment is azithromycin or ceftriaxone.
H ~ A woman has painless beefy-red granulomatous genital ulcers that bleed readily, with Donovan bodies on a tissue crush preparation. The organism is: ~ Klebsiella granulomatis ~ Chlamydia trachomatis serovar L2 ~ Treponema pallidum ~ Haemophilus ducreyi ~ A ~ Donovanosis produces highly vascular ulcers with pseudobuboes rather than true lymphadenopathy, and the intracytoplasmic Donovan bodies in mononuclear cells confirm it; prolonged azithromycin is needed.
H ~ Which feature distinguishes genital tuberculosis from ordinary pelvic inflammatory disease in an infertile woman? ~ Infertility with scanty menses and a hysterosalpingogram showing beaded tubes with a lead-pipe appearance ~ Purulent cervical discharge ~ Acute onset with fever ~ Response to a single course of doxycycline ~ A ~ Genital tuberculosis is usually secondary and indolent, destroying the endometrium to cause hypomenorrhoea or amenorrhoea and the tubes to cause infertility, and endometrial sampling in the premenstrual phase gives the best yield.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Gestational trophoblastic disease & vulvo-vaginal lesions ~ OBG_GTD ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Gestational Trophoblastic Disease
E ~ A woman at 14 weeks has a uterus larger than dates, absent fetal heart, a snowstorm appearance on ultrasound and a beta-hCG of 400000 IU/L. The management is: ~ Suction evacuation with oxytocin only after evacuation has begun ~ Hysterectomy ~ Medical induction with prostaglandin ~ Expectant management ~ A ~ Suction curettage is the method of choice; oxytocics before evacuation are avoided because uterine contraction may embolise trophoblast, and prostaglandin induction is contraindicated for the same reason.
E ~ Which genetic constitution characterises a complete hydatidiform mole? ~ 46,XX entirely of paternal origin (androgenetic diploid) ~ 69,XXY triploid ~ 46,XY of biparental origin ~ 45,X ~ A ~ Fertilisation of an empty ovum with duplication of the paternal genome gives a diploid androgenetic conceptus with no fetal tissue, diffuse villous hydrops and marked trophoblastic proliferation.
E ~ Which feature favours a partial rather than a complete mole? ~ Presence of fetal parts with focal villous hydrops and a triploid karyotype ~ Uterus much larger than dates ~ Beta-hCG above 100000 IU/L ~ Absence of any fetal tissue ~ A ~ Partial moles arise from fertilisation of a normal ovum by two sperm, so a triploid fetus is present, hCG is lower and the risk of persistent trophoblastic disease is only 1 to 5 percent rather than 15 to 20 percent.
M ~ After molar evacuation, which pattern of beta-hCG indicates gestational trophoblastic neoplasia requiring chemotherapy? ~ A plateau over four consecutive weekly values or a rise over three consecutive values ~ A steady fall to normal in 8 weeks ~ A single value of 500 IU/L at 4 weeks ~ Any detectable level at 2 weeks ~ A ~ These FIGO criteria, together with a histological diagnosis of choriocarcinoma or metastatic disease, define the need for chemotherapy, and a normal fall over weeks needs only continued surveillance.
M ~ A woman with a WHO prognostic score of 4 after a complete mole has persistent disease confined to the uterus. The appropriate treatment is: ~ Single-agent methotrexate or actinomycin D ~ EMA-CO combination chemotherapy ~ Hysterectomy alone ~ Pelvic radiotherapy ~ A ~ A score of 6 or below defines low-risk disease with cure rates approaching 100 percent on single-agent therapy, whereas a score of 7 or more requires multi-agent EMA-CO.
M ~ Contraception after molar evacuation should be: ~ Reliable hormonal or barrier contraception until hCG has been normal for the recommended surveillance period ~ Avoided entirely ~ An intrauterine device inserted immediately ~ Sterilisation in all cases ~ A ~ Pregnancy would confound hCG surveillance, so effective contraception is essential; an intrauterine device is avoided until hCG normalises because of the risk of uterine perforation of residual disease.
M ~ A woman presents with a vulval lesion showing well-demarcated white plaques, loss of architecture with fusion of the labia minora and introital narrowing. The diagnosis and treatment are: ~ Lichen sclerosus, treated with a potent topical corticosteroid ~ Vulval candidiasis, treated with an antifungal ~ Vulval intraepithelial neoplasia, treated by excision ~ Lichen planus, treated with antihistamines ~ A ~ Lichen sclerosus causes the classic figure-of-eight parchment-like change with resorption of architecture, responds to clobetasol and carries a small long-term risk of squamous carcinoma, so follow-up is needed.
H ~ Which site is the commonest for metastasis of choriocarcinoma, and why does it change management? ~ Lung, so a chest radiograph is part of initial staging and lesion count contributes to the prognostic score ~ Bone, requiring a skeletal survey ~ Liver, requiring biopsy ~ Skin, requiring excision ~ A ~ Choriocarcinoma spreads haematogenously with the lung as the first filter, and the number of metastases feeds directly into the WHO score that decides single-agent versus combination chemotherapy.
H ~ Why is biopsy of a suspected vaginal or cervical metastasis of choriocarcinoma avoided? ~ These lesions are extremely vascular and biopsy can cause torrential haemorrhage ~ It seeds tumour along the needle track ~ Histology is never diagnostic ~ It delays chemotherapy by weeks ~ A ~ Diagnosis rests on the hCG level and imaging rather than histology, and the friable highly vascular deposits bleed catastrophically if instrumented.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Induction, augmentation & operative obstetrics (forceps, vacuum, LSCS) ~ OBG_IND ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Induction of Labour and Operative Obstetrics
E ~ A woman with a previous classical caesarean section requests induction at term. The correct advice is: ~ Induction and labour are contraindicated; elective repeat caesarean is indicated ~ Induce with prostaglandin gel ~ Induce with oxytocin only ~ Use mechanical ripening with a balloon ~ A ~ A classical upper-segment incision carries a rupture risk of 4 to 9 percent, far higher than a lower-segment scar, so labour is avoided altogether.
E ~ Which Bishop score indicates a favourable cervix likely to respond to amniotomy and oxytocin alone? ~ 8 or more ~ 3 ~ 5 ~ 0 ~ A ~ The score combines dilatation, effacement, station, consistency and position; a score of 6 or less usually requires prostaglandin or mechanical ripening first, whereas 8 or more predicts successful induction.
E ~ Which condition is an absolute contraindication to vacuum extraction but not to forceps? ~ Face presentation ~ Occipito-transverse position ~ Maternal exhaustion in the second stage ~ Prolonged second stage with a fully dilated cervix ~ A ~ The cup cannot be applied to the fetal face, and vacuum is also avoided before 34 weeks and in suspected fetal coagulopathy, whereas forceps can deliver a mento-anterior face.
M ~ A woman having oxytocin augmentation develops contractions lasting 90 seconds occurring six times in 10 minutes with a fetal bradycardia. The first action is: ~ Stop the oxytocin, turn her to the left lateral position, give oxygen and consider a tocolytic ~ Increase the oxytocin to establish a regular pattern ~ Perform immediate forceps delivery ~ Start magnesium sulphate ~ A ~ Uterine hyperstimulation reduces placental perfusion during contraction, so removing the stimulus and improving uteroplacental flow is the immediate remedy before deciding on delivery.
M ~ Which instrument-related complication is more common with vacuum than with forceps delivery? ~ Cephalhaematoma and subgaleal haemorrhage ~ Facial nerve palsy ~ Third and fourth degree perineal tears ~ Maternal vaginal wall laceration ~ A ~ Suction shears scalp vessels, causing cephalhaematoma and the more dangerous subgaleal bleed, whereas forceps cause more maternal perineal trauma and facial nerve injury from blade pressure.
M ~ At caesarean section for a deeply engaged head, which manoeuvre reduces the risk of extension of the uterine incision? ~ Delivery of the head by the reverse breech extraction or Patwardhan technique ~ Vigorous fundal pressure ~ Extending the incision as a J-shape routinely ~ Using a classical incision ~ A ~ Disimpacting a deeply engaged head by pushing from below risks lateral tears into the uterine vessels, so delivering the trunk first, or using a push technique with an assistant, reduces extension and bleeding.
M ~ Which prophylactic measure most reduces surgical site infection at caesarean section? ~ A single dose of intravenous antibiotic given before skin incision ~ Antibiotics started 24 hours after surgery ~ Skin preparation with alcohol alone ~ Subcutaneous drain in all cases ~ A ~ Pre-incision dosing achieves adequate tissue levels at the time of contamination and halves endometritis and wound infection compared with dosing after cord clamping.
H ~ Which criterion must be satisfied before applying forceps for a mid-cavity rotational delivery? ~ Full dilatation with the head at or below the ischial spines, no more than one-fifth palpable abdominally, and adequate analgesia with an empty bladder ~ Cervix 8 cm dilated with the head at the brim ~ Any station provided the operator is experienced ~ Membranes intact ~ A ~ Rotational forceps demand exact knowledge of position and station; applying them above the spines or before full dilatation risks severe maternal and fetal trauma and is no longer taught in most units.
H ~ In a caesarean section complicated by placenta accreta with torrential bleeding, which stepwise measure comes before hysterectomy? ~ Uterine and internal iliac artery ligation or balloon tamponade with a uterine compression suture ~ Immediate subtotal hysterectomy in all cases ~ Manual removal of the placenta ~ Methotrexate ~ A ~ Conservative measures may control bleeding and preserve fertility, but manual removal of a morbidly adherent placenta provokes catastrophic haemorrhage and should not be attempted.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Infections in pregnancy & Rh isoimmunisation ~ OBG_INF ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Infections in Pregnancy and Rh Isoimmunisation
E ~ An Rh-negative woman at 28 weeks with no antibodies receives routine antenatal anti-D. What dose consideration applies at delivery? ~ A further dose is given within 72 hours of delivery if the baby is Rh positive ~ No further dose is needed ~ Anti-D is given only if the baby is Rh negative ~ Anti-D should be withheld if the direct Coombs test is positive in the baby ~ A ~ Routine antenatal prophylaxis at 28 weeks does not remove the need for postnatal prophylaxis, and a Kleihauer test guides additional doses when a large fetomaternal bleed is suspected.
E ~ Which maternal infection in the first trimester carries the highest risk of severe fetal damage but the lowest risk of transmission later in pregnancy? ~ Rubella ~ Toxoplasmosis ~ Cytomegalovirus ~ Syphilis ~ A ~ Rubella causes severe defects in up to 90 percent when contracted before 11 weeks but is rarely teratogenic after 20 weeks, the reverse of toxoplasmosis where transmission rises but severity falls with advancing gestation.
E ~ Group B streptococcal prophylaxis in labour is indicated in which situation? ~ A previous baby with invasive group B streptococcal disease ~ Any woman in preterm labour with negative cultures ~ Elective caesarean with intact membranes ~ Any woman with a urinary tract infection in the first trimester ~ A ~ Risk factors include a previous affected infant, group B streptococcal bacteriuria in this pregnancy, a positive culture, prematurity, prolonged rupture and intrapartum fever; elective caesarean with intact membranes needs no prophylaxis.
M ~ A pregnant woman is found to have a positive VDRL confirmed by TPHA at 16 weeks. The treatment is: ~ Benzathine penicillin, with desensitisation if she is penicillin allergic ~ Doxycycline ~ Azithromycin ~ Ceftriaxone for 3 days ~ A ~ Penicillin is the only agent proven to treat the fetus, so a truly allergic woman is desensitised rather than given an alternative; treatment before 16 to 20 weeks usually prevents congenital syphilis entirely.
M ~ Which antenatal finding suggests congenital cytomegalovirus infection? ~ Fetal growth restriction with intracranial periventricular calcification and ventriculomegaly ~ Bilateral cataract with cardiac defect ~ Limb hypoplasia with cutaneous scarring ~ Hydrops with a raised middle cerebral artery velocity ~ A ~ Periventricular calcification, microcephaly and echogenic bowel point to cytomegalovirus, whereas cataract with a patent ductus suggests rubella, limb scarring suggests varicella and raised cerebral velocity suggests anaemia.
M ~ An Rh-negative woman has an anti-D titre of 1 in 64 at 24 weeks in her second pregnancy. The next step is: ~ Serial middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity measurement ~ Immediate intrauterine transfusion ~ Repeat anti-D immunoglobulin ~ Delivery at 28 weeks ~ A ~ Once a critical titre is reached, anti-D is useless and non-invasive Doppler surveillance identifies significant fetal anaemia, which is then treated by intrauterine transfusion rather than by empirical early delivery.
M ~ A woman develops chickenpox 3 days before delivery. The neonate should receive: ~ Varicella zoster immunoglobulin, with aciclovir if lesions develop ~ Live varicella vaccine at birth ~ Nothing, as maternal antibody is protective ~ Intravenous immunoglobulin only if the mother is seronegative ~ A ~ Delivery within 7 days before to 2 days after the onset of maternal rash gives no time for protective maternal IgG to cross, so passive immunisation of the neonate is needed to prevent severe disseminated neonatal varicella.
H ~ Which laboratory test quantifies the volume of fetomaternal haemorrhage to guide additional anti-D dosing? ~ Kleihauer-Betke acid elution test or flow cytometry for fetal cells ~ Indirect Coombs test ~ Direct Coombs test on maternal blood ~ Maternal haemoglobin electrophoresis ~ A ~ Fetal haemoglobin resists acid elution so fetal cells stand out against ghosted maternal cells, and each 4 mL of fetal red cells requires an additional 500 IU of anti-D.
H ~ Why does ABO incompatibility between mother and fetus partly protect against Rh isoimmunisation? ~ ABO-incompatible fetal red cells entering the maternal circulation are destroyed rapidly before Rh sensitisation can occur ~ Maternal anti-A and anti-B block the Rh antigen ~ The placenta becomes impermeable ~ Fetal red cells lose the D antigen ~ A ~ Naturally occurring maternal isohaemagglutinins clear the incompatible fetal cells from the circulation before the immune system can mount a primary anti-D response.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Infertility & assisted reproduction ~ OBG_INFT ~ Shaw's Textbook of Gynaecology 17e, Ch. Infertility
E ~ A couple has been trying to conceive for 14 months with regular intercourse. The initial investigations should include: ~ Semen analysis, confirmation of ovulation and assessment of tubal patency ~ Diagnostic laparoscopy first ~ Karyotyping of both partners ~ Immediate in vitro fertilisation ~ A ~ Infertility is defined by 12 months of failure to conceive, and the three basic axes to test are sperm, ovulation and tubes before any invasive or advanced treatment.
E ~ Which test best confirms that ovulation has occurred in a woman with a 28-day cycle? ~ Mid-luteal serum progesterone above 3 to 10 ng/mL on day 21 ~ Day 3 follicle-stimulating hormone ~ Basal body temperature chart alone ~ Serum prolactin ~ A ~ Progesterone is produced only by the corpus luteum, so a raised mid-luteal level is direct evidence of ovulation, whereas day 3 gonadotropins assess reserve and prolactin screens for a cause of anovulation.
E ~ Which measure of ovarian reserve is least affected by cycle day and hormonal contraception? ~ Anti-Mullerian hormone ~ Day 3 follicle-stimulating hormone ~ Day 3 oestradiol ~ Inhibin B ~ A ~ Anti-Mullerian hormone is produced by small antral follicles throughout the cycle, so it can be measured on any day, and together with antral follicle count it predicts response to ovarian stimulation.
M ~ A woman with polycystic ovary syndrome and anovulatory infertility has a normal body mass index. The first-line ovulation induction agent is: ~ Letrozole ~ Gonadotropins ~ Metformin alone ~ Clomiphene with dexamethasone ~ A ~ Head-to-head trials showed higher live birth rates with letrozole than clomiphene in polycystic ovary syndrome, and aromatase inhibition avoids the antioestrogenic endometrial effect of clomiphene.
M ~ A man has a semen volume of 2 mL with a total sperm count of zero, normal testicular volume and normal follicle-stimulating hormone. The next investigation is: ~ Assessment for obstructive azoospermia including vasal agenesis and cystic fibrosis mutation testing ~ Karyotype for Klinefelter syndrome ~ Testicular biopsy for non-obstructive azoospermia ~ Serum prolactin ~ A ~ Normal testicular size with normal follicle-stimulating hormone points to obstruction rather than failure of spermatogenesis, and congenital bilateral absence of the vas is associated with CFTR mutations that must be identified before assisted conception.
M ~ Which complication of controlled ovarian stimulation presents with abdominal distension, ascites, haemoconcentration and oliguria? ~ Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome ~ Ovarian torsion ~ Pelvic infection ~ Ectopic pregnancy ~ A ~ Vascular endothelial growth factor released by luteinised granulosa cells increases capillary permeability, causing third-space fluid loss; risk is highest in young lean women with polycystic ovaries and high oestradiol, and a gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist protocol with an agonist trigger reduces it.
M ~ Intracytoplasmic sperm injection is specifically indicated rather than conventional in vitro fertilisation when: ~ There is severe oligoasthenoteratozoospermia or previous failed fertilisation ~ The woman is over 38 ~ There is tubal blockage with normal semen ~ Endometriosis is present ~ A ~ Injecting a single sperm bypasses the need for it to penetrate the zona, so the indication is a sperm problem; using it for non-male factor infertility adds cost and risk without improving live birth rates.
H ~ A hysterosalpingogram shows bilateral cornual block with no spill. Before proceeding to in vitro fertilisation, which possibility should be considered? ~ Tubal spasm or a false-positive result, so laparoscopy with dye or selective salpingography may be needed ~ Immediate salpingectomy ~ Hysteroscopic septal resection ~ Endometrial biopsy ~ A ~ Cornual obstruction on hysterosalpingography is frequently spurious because of spasm or mucus plugging, and confirming it before committing a couple to in vitro fertilisation avoids unnecessary treatment.
H ~ Why is a hydrosalpinx removed or occluded before in vitro fertilisation? ~ Hydrosalpinx fluid refluxing into the cavity impairs implantation and halves pregnancy rates ~ It causes ovarian hyperstimulation ~ It prevents oocyte retrieval ~ It raises the risk of multiple pregnancy ~ A ~ Salpingectomy or proximal occlusion restores implantation rates to those of women without hydrosalpinx, which is one of the clearest surgical indications in reproductive medicine.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Medical disorders in pregnancy: anaemia, diabetes, heart disease ~ OBG_MED ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Medical Disorders in Pregnancy
E ~ A woman with pre-existing type 1 diabetes plans pregnancy. Which target reduces the risk of congenital malformation most? ~ Achieving an HbA1c below 6.5 percent before conception, with high-dose folic acid ~ Strict control only after the first trimester ~ Switching to oral agents ~ Delaying pregnancy until age 35 ~ A ~ Organogenesis is complete by 8 weeks, so periconceptional glycaemia determines malformation risk; caudal regression and cardiac defects are the classic anomalies of poor control.
E ~ Which anaemia in pregnancy is characterised by a normal or raised serum ferritin with a low mean corpuscular volume and a raised HbA2 on electrophoresis? ~ Beta-thalassaemia trait ~ Iron deficiency anaemia ~ Folate deficiency ~ Anaemia of chronic disease ~ A ~ Distinguishing trait from iron deficiency matters because iron loading is harmful and because partner screening and prenatal diagnosis are needed to prevent thalassaemia major in the fetus.
E ~ In a woman with heart disease, which stage of labour and delivery carries the greatest risk of decompensation? ~ Immediately after delivery, when the autotransfusion of uterine blood increases preload ~ Early first stage ~ The latent phase ~ Six weeks postpartum ~ A ~ Uterine contraction and relief of caval compression abruptly increase venous return, so the first 24 to 48 hours postpartum is when pulmonary oedema most often occurs in stenotic lesions.
M ~ A woman with gestational diabetes on diet alone has fasting values of 100 mg/dL and 2-hour postprandial values of 150 mg/dL. The next step is: ~ Start insulin or metformin, as targets are not being met ~ Continue diet alone ~ Repeat the glucose tolerance test ~ Deliver at 37 weeks ~ A ~ The usual targets are a fasting value below 95 mg/dL and a 2-hour postprandial value below 120 mg/dL, and failing them for two weeks on diet is the trigger for pharmacological treatment.
M ~ Which cardiac condition carries a maternal mortality high enough that pregnancy is contraindicated and termination advised if it occurs? ~ Severe pulmonary arterial hypertension ~ Mild mitral stenosis ~ Corrected atrial septal defect ~ Mitral valve prolapse ~ A ~ The fixed pulmonary vascular resistance cannot accommodate the pregnancy-related increase in cardiac output, and maternal mortality remains around 30 percent, so pregnancy is classed as WHO risk category IV.
M ~ Which antihypertensive must be stopped as soon as pregnancy is confirmed? ~ Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor ~ Labetalol ~ Nifedipine ~ Methyldopa ~ A ~ Renin-angiotensin blockade in the second and third trimesters causes fetal renal failure, oligohydramnios, skull hypoplasia and limb contractures, so these drugs are replaced before or immediately on conception.
M ~ A pregnant woman with a mechanical mitral valve is on warfarin at 8 weeks. The recommended change is: ~ Substitute low molecular weight heparin during the period of organogenesis, with careful anti-Xa monitoring ~ Continue warfarin unchanged throughout ~ Switch to aspirin alone ~ Switch to a direct oral anticoagulant ~ A ~ Warfarin embryopathy risk is greatest between 6 and 12 weeks, but heparin carries a higher risk of valve thrombosis, so regimens balance the two; direct oral anticoagulants are contraindicated in pregnancy.
H ~ A woman with sickle cell disease in pregnancy develops acute chest pain, hypoxia and new pulmonary infiltrates. The management includes: ~ Oxygen, analgesia, antibiotics, hydration and exchange transfusion for severe acute chest syndrome ~ Diuresis alone ~ Immediate delivery in every case ~ Withholding transfusion to avoid alloimmunisation ~ A ~ Acute chest syndrome is a leading cause of death in sickle cell disease and pregnancy increases its frequency; exchange transfusion reduces the sickle fraction and is indicated when hypoxia is severe or progressive.
H ~ Which change in thyroid function testing is expected in normal pregnancy and must not be misread as disease? ~ A rise in total thyroxine with a fall in TSH in the first trimester ~ A fall in total thyroxine with a rise in TSH ~ A rise in free thyroxine throughout pregnancy ~ Unchanged TSH across gestation ~ A ~ Oestrogen raises thyroxine-binding globulin so total hormone rises, and human chorionic gonadotropin weakly stimulates the TSH receptor to suppress TSH early, so trimester-specific reference ranges and free hormone levels must be used.
