@ Physiology ~ Renal tubular transport, acid-base regulation & micturition ~ PY_REN ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Renal Tubular Processing and Acid-Base Regulation
E ~ Which segment reabsorbs the largest fraction of filtered sodium and water? ~ The proximal convoluted tubule, reabsorbing about two-thirds isosmotically ~ The thick ascending limb ~ The distal convoluted tubule ~ The collecting duct ~ A ~ Because reabsorption here is isosmotic, tubular fluid osmolality at the end of the proximal tubule equals plasma, and the concentrating work begins in the loop.
E ~ Which transporter is inhibited by furosemide, and in which segment? ~ The sodium-potassium-two-chloride cotransporter in the thick ascending limb ~ The sodium-chloride cotransporter in the distal tubule ~ The epithelial sodium channel in the collecting duct ~ Carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule ~ A ~ Blocking it abolishes the medullary gradient so both concentrating and diluting ability are lost, and the increased distal sodium delivery drives potassium and hydrogen loss.
E ~ Which cell of the collecting duct secretes hydrogen ion and reabsorbs bicarbonate? ~ The alpha-intercalated cell, using an apical hydrogen ATPase ~ The beta-intercalated cell ~ The principal cell ~ The macula densa cell ~ A ~ Beta-intercalated cells do the reverse, secreting bicarbonate in alkalosis, and failure of the alpha cell causes distal renal tubular acidosis with an inability to acidify the urine.
M ~ Which buffer allows the kidney to excrete the largest quantity of acid, and how is its capacity increased in chronic acidosis? ~ Ammonium, whose production from glutamine is upregulated over days ~ Phosphate, whose filtered load is fixed ~ Bicarbonate, which is reabsorbed ~ Protein, which is not filtered ~ A ~ Titratable acid excretion is limited by the filtered phosphate load, whereas ammoniagenesis can increase severalfold, which is why the urinary anion gap is used to assess it clinically.
M ~ A patient has arterial pH 7.32, bicarbonate 16 mmol per litre and carbon dioxide tension 32 mmHg. Which disorder is present? ~ Metabolic acidosis with appropriate respiratory compensation ~ Respiratory acidosis ~ Metabolic alkalosis ~ Mixed respiratory and metabolic alkalosis ~ A ~ Winter's formula predicts an expected carbon dioxide of about 32 mmHg for this bicarbonate, so the compensation is appropriate and no second disorder need be invoked.
M ~ Which finding indicates that a metabolic acidosis has a raised anion gap? ~ Accumulation of an unmeasured anion such as lactate, ketoacid or a toxin metabolite ~ Loss of bicarbonate with chloride retention ~ Renal tubular acidosis ~ Diarrhoeal bicarbonate loss ~ A ~ A normal gap acidosis reflects bicarbonate loss with hyperchloraemia, and calculating the delta ratio detects a coexisting metabolic alkalosis or normal-gap acidosis.
M ~ Which reflex initiates micturition, and which centre coordinates it? ~ Bladder stretch receptors acting through the pontine micturition centre, with voluntary control from the cortex ~ A purely spinal reflex with no supraspinal input ~ The sacral centre alone ~ Sympathetic outflow from T10 to L2 alone ~ A ~ A suprasacral cord lesion therefore produces a reflex or spastic bladder after the initial areflexic phase, whereas a sacral or conus lesion produces a flaccid atonic bladder.
H ~ Why does aldosterone excess cause hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis? ~ It increases sodium reabsorption with potassium and hydrogen secretion in the collecting duct ~ It reduces bicarbonate reabsorption ~ It causes chloride retention ~ It inhibits the sodium pump ~ A ~ Volume expansion also raises the filtered load and distal flow, which augments potassium loss, and this explains the biochemical signature of primary hyperaldosteronism.
H ~ Why is potassium secretion dependent on distal sodium delivery? ~ Sodium entry through the epithelial sodium channel creates a lumen-negative potential that drives potassium out through apical channels ~ Potassium is exchanged directly for sodium at the apical membrane ~ Sodium inhibits potassium reabsorption ~ Distal flow has no effect ~ A ~ This coupling explains why loop and thiazide diuretics cause hypokalaemia and why potassium-sparing agents that block the channel, or the aldosterone receptor, prevent it.
@ Physiology ~ Reproductive physiology, pregnancy & lactation ~ PY_REP ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Reproductive and Hormonal Functions
E ~ Which hormone surge triggers ovulation, and what is its source? ~ The luteinising hormone surge from the anterior pituitary, provoked by rising oestradiol ~ Follicle-stimulating hormone from the hypothalamus ~ Progesterone from the corpus luteum ~ Human chorionic gonadotropin from the ovary ~ A ~ The switch of oestradiol from negative to positive feedback at high sustained concentrations is what generates the surge, and ovulation follows about 36 hours later.
E ~ Which cell of the testis produces testosterone, and under which trophic hormone? ~ The Leydig cell, under luteinising hormone ~ The Sertoli cell, under luteinising hormone ~ The Leydig cell, under follicle-stimulating hormone ~ The spermatogonium, under testosterone ~ A ~ Follicle-stimulating hormone acts on Sertoli cells to support spermatogenesis and to secrete inhibin B, which feeds back selectively on follicle-stimulating hormone.
E ~ Which hormone maintains the corpus luteum in early pregnancy until the placenta takes over? ~ Human chorionic gonadotropin from the syncytiotrophoblast ~ Luteinising hormone ~ Progesterone from the endometrium ~ Human placental lactogen ~ A ~ The luteoplacental shift occurs at about 8 to 10 weeks, after which the placenta produces sufficient progesterone and removal of the corpus luteum no longer causes miscarriage.
M ~ Which hormonal change immediately after delivery permits the onset of copious milk secretion? ~ The abrupt fall in progesterone while prolactin remains high ~ A rise in progesterone ~ A fall in prolactin ~ A rise in oestrogen ~ A ~ Retained placental fragments delay lactogenesis for exactly this reason, and suckling then maintains prolactin secretion by inhibiting hypothalamic dopamine release.
M ~ Which reflex is responsible for milk ejection, and which hormone mediates it? ~ The neuroendocrine let-down reflex mediated by oxytocin from the posterior pituitary ~ The prolactin reflex ~ A local myoepithelial reflex without hormones ~ The Ferguson reflex ~ A ~ Oxytocin contracts myoepithelial cells around the alveoli, and because the pathway is neural at its afferent limb it is readily inhibited by pain, anxiety and embarrassment.
M ~ Which mechanism explains lactational amenorrhoea? ~ High prolactin suppresses pulsatile gonadotropin-releasing hormone secretion, preventing ovulation ~ Oxytocin suppresses the ovary directly ~ Oestrogen levels are high ~ The endometrium is unresponsive ~ A ~ Because the effect depends on frequent suckling, its contraceptive reliability falls as feeds are spaced or supplemented, which is the physiological basis of the lactational amenorrhoea method's three conditions.
M ~ Which change in the ovarian cycle is responsible for the rise in basal body temperature in the second half? ~ Progesterone from the corpus luteum acting on the hypothalamic thermoregulatory centre ~ Oestradiol from the follicle ~ Luteinising hormone ~ Inhibin ~ A ~ The biphasic temperature chart is therefore evidence that ovulation has occurred, and its absence suggests an anovulatory cycle.
H ~ Why does inhibin allow selective feedback control of follicle-stimulating hormone without affecting luteinising hormone? ~ Inhibin acts specifically at the pituitary gonadotroph to suppress follicle-stimulating hormone synthesis ~ It acts on the hypothalamus ~ It converts luteinising hormone to follicle-stimulating hormone ~ It blocks gonadotropin-releasing hormone receptors ~ A ~ This selectivity underlies the fall in follicle-stimulating hormone that causes atresia of all but the dominant follicle, and its measurement is used as a tumour marker in granulosa cell tumours.
H ~ Why does the fetus not reject the mother, and the mother not reject the fetus, despite their genetic difference? ~ The trophoblast expresses little classical MHC class I and no class II, with local regulatory T cell and cytokine mechanisms creating tolerance ~ The placenta is an absolute barrier to cells ~ The fetus is immunologically identical ~ The uterus lacks lymphatic drainage ~ A ~ Fetal cells do cross into the maternal circulation, which is how cell-free fetal DNA testing works and how Rh sensitisation occurs, so the tolerance is active rather than a matter of separation.
@ Physiology ~ Respiratory regulation, high altitude & diving ~ PY_RSP ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Regulation of Respiration
E ~ Which chemoreceptor provides the principal drive to ventilation in response to a rise in arterial carbon dioxide? ~ Central chemoreceptors in the medulla, responding to cerebrospinal fluid hydrogen ion ~ Carotid body peripheral chemoreceptors ~ Aortic body chemoreceptors ~ Pulmonary stretch receptors ~ A ~ Carbon dioxide crosses the blood-brain barrier readily while hydrogen ion does not, so central receptors sense pH change in the cerebrospinal fluid and account for most of the carbon dioxide response.
E ~ Which receptor is responsible for the ventilatory response to a fall in arterial oxygen tension? ~ The carotid body, responding when tension falls below about 60 mmHg ~ The central chemoreceptor ~ The aortic arch baroreceptor ~ The J receptor ~ A ~ Because the response is flat until tension falls steeply, oxygen provides little drive in health, and this is why the hypoxic drive matters chiefly in chronic carbon dioxide retention.
E ~ Which reflex terminates inspiration when the lung is greatly inflated? ~ The Hering-Breuer inflation reflex, mediated by vagal slowly adapting stretch receptors ~ The J receptor reflex ~ The Head paradoxical reflex ~ The cough reflex ~ A ~ In adults the reflex is largely inactive at normal tidal volumes and becomes important only at high volumes, though it operates during normal breathing in the newborn.
M ~ Which acid-base change explains why ventilation increases only partially on arrival at high altitude and then rises further over days? ~ Hypoxic drive is initially opposed by hypocapnic alkalosis, which resolves as the kidney excretes bicarbonate ~ Carbon dioxide rises over days ~ Haemoglobin falls ~ The carotid body becomes desensitised ~ A ~ Acetazolamide accelerates this renal bicarbonate loss, which is why it both prevents acute mountain sickness and improves ventilation and sleep at altitude.
M ~ Why does a person hyperventilating before a breath-hold dive risk loss of consciousness under water? ~ Lowering carbon dioxide delays the urge to breathe until oxygen has fallen dangerously low ~ It raises oxygen stores substantially ~ It causes nitrogen narcosis ~ It increases pulmonary shunt ~ A ~ Because carbon dioxide rather than oxygen drives the urge to surface, removing that stimulus decouples the warning from the danger, producing shallow-water blackout.
M ~ Which condition causes decompression sickness in a diver ascending too rapidly? ~ Nitrogen dissolved at depth under pressure comes out of solution as bubbles ~ Oxygen toxicity ~ Carbon dioxide retention ~ Nitrogen narcosis ~ A ~ Controlled ascent with stops allows nitrogen to be eliminated through the lungs, and treatment of established disease is recompression with hyperbaric oxygen.
M ~ Which pattern of breathing is characterised by cycles of gradually increasing then decreasing tidal volume with intervening apnoea? ~ Cheyne-Stokes respiration, seen in heart failure and at altitude ~ Kussmaul breathing ~ Biot breathing ~ Apneustic breathing ~ A ~ It arises from an unstable feedback loop with prolonged circulation time and increased chemoreceptor gain, so the ventilatory response overshoots and undershoots repeatedly.
H ~ Why does giving high-concentration oxygen to a patient with chronic carbon dioxide retention risk worsening hypercapnia? ~ It abolishes hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, worsening ventilation-perfusion matching, and reduces the hypoxic ventilatory drive ~ It directly depresses the medulla ~ It increases dead space ~ It causes bronchospasm ~ A ~ The dominant mechanism is now understood to be the loss of ventilation-perfusion matching rather than drive alone, which is why controlled oxygen to a target saturation of 88 to 92 percent is used rather than withholding oxygen.
H ~ Why is the ventilatory response to exercise so precise that arterial carbon dioxide barely changes despite a large rise in production? ~ Feed-forward drive from the motor cortex and joint receptors acts with chemoreceptor feedback to match ventilation to metabolism ~ Chemoreceptors alone account for it ~ Carbon dioxide production does not rise ~ Dead space falls to zero ~ A ~ Because arterial carbon dioxide is nearly constant during moderate exercise, chemoreceptor feedback alone cannot explain the response, which is why anticipatory neural mechanisms are invoked.
@ Physiology ~ Special senses, CSF, temperature regulation & sleep ~ PY_SEN ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. The Special Senses and States of Brain Activity
E ~ Where is cerebrospinal fluid produced and where is it principally absorbed? ~ Produced by the choroid plexus and absorbed through arachnoid granulations into the dural venous sinuses ~ Produced by the arachnoid and absorbed by the choroid plexus ~ Produced by ependyma and absorbed by lymphatics only ~ Produced by the pia and absorbed by capillaries ~ A ~ About 500 mL is produced daily against a total volume of 150 mL, so the fluid turns over three times a day, and obstruction anywhere along the path produces hydrocephalus.
E ~ Which receptor cells mediate colour vision, and how many types are there in humans? ~ Cones, of three types with peak sensitivities in the short, medium and long wavelengths ~ Rods, of three types ~ Cones, of two types ~ Ganglion cells, of four types ~ A ~ Rods contain a single pigment so they cannot support colour discrimination, which is why colour vision fails in scotopic conditions and why the fovea, which is cone-rich, is used for colour and detail.
E ~ Which structure of the inner ear detects angular acceleration, and which detects linear acceleration? ~ The semicircular canals detect angular acceleration and the otolith organs detect linear acceleration and head tilt ~ The cochlea detects both ~ The utricle detects angular acceleration ~ The semicircular canals detect gravity ~ A ~ Fluid inertia deflects the cupula in the canals, whereas the weight of otoconia on the maculae signals tilt, and displacement of otoconia into a canal causes benign paroxysmal positional vertigo.
M ~ Which stage of sleep is characterised by muscle atonia, rapid eye movements and a desynchronised EEG resembling wakefulness? ~ Rapid eye movement sleep, in which most dreaming occurs ~ Stage 2 non-REM sleep ~ Slow wave sleep ~ Stage 1 non-REM sleep ~ A ~ Loss of the normal atonia produces REM sleep behaviour disorder, which may precede Parkinson disease by years, and REM proportion is greatest in the newborn.
M ~ Which set of thermoregulatory responses is initiated by the hypothalamus in a cold environment? ~ Cutaneous vasoconstriction, shivering, piloerection and increased thyroid and sympathetic activity ~ Sweating with vasodilatation ~ Vasodilatation with reduced metabolism ~ Panting with peripheral vasodilatation ~ A ~ The preoptic anterior hypothalamus integrates skin and core temperature, and non-shivering thermogenesis in brown fat is the dominant mechanism in the newborn who cannot shiver.
M ~ How does fever differ physiologically from hyperthermia? ~ In fever the hypothalamic set point is raised by prostaglandin E2 so heat-conserving responses operate, whereas in hyperthermia the set point is normal and the mechanisms are overwhelmed ~ Both raise the set point ~ Fever has no set point change ~ Hyperthermia responds to antipyretics ~ A ~ This is why antipyretics work in fever but not in heat stroke, and why a febrile patient feels cold and shivers while a hyperthermic patient does not.
M ~ Which mechanism explains presbyopia? ~ Loss of lens elasticity with age reduces the amplitude of accommodation ~ Weakness of the ciliary muscle alone ~ Increased axial length ~ Corneal flattening ~ A ~ Because the lens itself hardens, the near point recedes progressively from childhood and becomes symptomatic in the fifth decade, requiring a convex reading addition.
H ~ Why does conductive hearing loss cause the Weber test to lateralise to the affected ear? ~ Removal of masking ambient air-conducted sound makes the bone-conducted signal appear louder on the affected side ~ The cochlea is more sensitive ~ Bone conduction is impaired ~ The auditory nerve is stimulated directly ~ A ~ In sensorineural loss the cochlea itself is impaired so the sound lateralises to the better ear, and combining Weber with Rinne allows the type of loss to be identified at the bedside.
H ~ Why does the blood-brain barrier make cerebrospinal fluid analysis useful in diagnosing central nervous system infection? ~ The barrier normally excludes cells, most proteins and immunoglobulin, so their appearance indicates local inflammation or synthesis ~ Cerebrospinal fluid is identical to plasma ~ The barrier admits all solutes equally ~ Antibody is always transferred from blood ~ A ~ Oligoclonal bands present in cerebrospinal fluid but not serum demonstrate intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis, which is why paired samples are analysed in suspected multiple sclerosis.
@ Physiology ~ Thyroid, adrenal, pancreas & calcium homeostasis ~ PY_END ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Thyroid, Adrenal and Pancreatic Hormones
E ~ Which step in thyroid hormone synthesis is inhibited by carbimazole? ~ Thyroid peroxidase-mediated organification and coupling ~ Iodide trapping by the symporter ~ Release of stored hormone ~ Peripheral deiodination ~ A ~ Because stored hormone is unaffected, the clinical effect is delayed by weeks, which is why beta-blockade is used for symptom control while waiting.
E ~ Which is the most abundant and which the most active thyroid hormone in the circulation? ~ Thyroxine is most abundant while triiodothyronine is more active, most of it formed by peripheral deiodination ~ Triiodothyronine is most abundant ~ Reverse triiodothyronine is most active ~ Both are equally abundant ~ A ~ Illness, starvation and drugs such as propranolol and amiodarone shift deiodination toward the inactive reverse form, producing the low triiodothyronine of non-thyroidal illness.
E ~ Which hormone raises serum calcium by increasing bone resorption, renal calcium reabsorption and calcitriol synthesis? ~ Parathyroid hormone ~ Calcitonin ~ Cortisol ~ Growth hormone ~ A ~ Parathyroid hormone also lowers phosphate by inhibiting proximal reabsorption, which distinguishes primary hyperparathyroidism from other causes of hypercalcaemia biochemically.
M ~ Which enzyme in the kidney is stimulated by parathyroid hormone to activate vitamin D, and what inhibits it? ~ 1-alpha hydroxylase, inhibited by FGF23 and by calcitriol itself ~ 25-hydroxylase, inhibited by calcium ~ 24-hydroxylase, inhibited by phosphate ~ Vitamin D binding protein ~ A ~ Loss of renal 1-alpha hydroxylation is why chronic kidney disease causes hypocalcaemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism, and why active analogues rather than cholecalciferol are used.
M ~ Which zone of the adrenal cortex produces aldosterone, and what is its principal regulator? ~ The zona glomerulosa, regulated by angiotensin II and potassium rather than by ACTH ~ The zona fasciculata, regulated by ACTH ~ The zona reticularis, regulated by potassium ~ The medulla, regulated by sympathetic outflow ~ A ~ Because ACTH is not the main driver, aldosterone secretion is largely preserved in secondary adrenal insufficiency, which is why salt-wasting is a feature of primary but not secondary disease.
M ~ Which phase of insulin secretion is lost earliest in type 2 diabetes? ~ The first phase, the rapid release of pre-formed insulin granules after a glucose load ~ The second phase ~ Basal secretion ~ Glucagon suppression ~ A ~ Loss of the early spike allows an exaggerated postprandial glucose excursion, which is why postprandial hyperglycaemia often precedes fasting hyperglycaemia in the natural history.
M ~ Which effect of cortisol explains its use in reducing cerebral oedema around a tumour? ~ Reduction of capillary permeability and of inflammatory mediator production ~ Direct osmotic action ~ Increased renal water excretion ~ Vasoconstriction of cerebral arteries ~ A ~ Cortisol is effective in vasogenic oedema surrounding tumours and abscesses but not in cytotoxic oedema after ischaemia, which is why steroids are not used in acute stroke or head injury.
H ~ Why does chronic hypocalcaemia in chronic kidney disease not simply respond to calcium supplementation? ~ Impaired calcitriol synthesis limits intestinal calcium absorption, and phosphate retention with high FGF23 must also be corrected ~ Calcium is not absorbed at all ~ Parathyroid hormone is absent ~ Calcium is bound by albumin ~ A ~ Management therefore combines phosphate restriction and binders with active vitamin D analogues and sometimes calcimimetics, rather than calcium alone.
H ~ Why does the Wolff-Chaikoff effect limit the usefulness of iodide as a long-term antithyroid treatment? ~ The inhibition of organification is transient and escape occurs within 10 to 14 days ~ Iodide is toxic to the liver ~ It causes permanent hypothyroidism ~ It cannot be given orally ~ A ~ The transient nature is precisely why Lugol's iodine is confined to short preoperative preparation and to thyroid storm, given after a thionamide has blocked synthesis.
