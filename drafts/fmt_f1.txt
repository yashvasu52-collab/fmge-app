@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Alcohol, opioids, sedatives & drugs of abuse (medico-legal) ~ FM_ALC ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Alcohol and Drug Dependence
M ~ A man found unconscious after drinking country liquor has severe metabolic acidosis, blurred vision and a wide osmolar gap. Which antidote is used and how does it work? ~ Naloxone, which displaces the toxin from opioid receptors ~ Fomepizole or ethanol, which competitively inhibit alcohol dehydrogenase and prevent formation of formic acid ~ Atropine, which blocks muscarinic effects ~ Physostigmine, which inhibits cholinesterase ~ B ~ Formate rather than the parent alcohol causes the acidosis and optic damage, so blocking its formation with folinic acid to enhance formate clearance and haemodialysis to remove both is the standard approach.
M ~ Which term describes the state in which a person who is drunk commits an act and later claims not to remember it, and how does the law treat it? ~ Automatism, which excuses the act entirely ~ Alcoholic amnesia or blackout, which is not a defence because voluntary intoxication does not excuse a crime ~ Dipsomania, which is a complete defence ~ Delirium tremens, which is always a defence ~ B ~ Involuntary intoxication, where the person was made drunk without knowledge or against their will, is treated differently and can negate criminal intent, which is the distinction that matters in court.
E ~ Which term denotes an irresistible periodic craving for alcohol occurring in bouts with normal drinking in between? ~ Dipsomania ~ Delirium tremens ~ Korsakoff psychosis ~ Chronic alcoholism ~ A ~ It is described as an impulse disorder rather than continuous dependence, and it is distinguished from the fixed daily pattern of chronic alcoholism and from the withdrawal state of delirium tremens.
M ~ A drug user is brought in with pinpoint pupils and shallow respiration; naloxone restores consciousness but he deteriorates again after 40 minutes. Why? ~ Naloxone has a shorter duration of action than most opioids, so repeated doses or an infusion are needed ~ Naloxone is destroyed by the liver instantly ~ A second drug has been taken ~ Tolerance to naloxone develops within minutes ~ A ~ This is why every patient reversed with the antidote is observed rather than discharged, and long-acting or slow-release opioids and body-packer leakage require particularly prolonged monitoring.
E ~ Which specimen and preservative combination is correct for post-mortem alcohol estimation? ~ Blood with sodium fluoride and potassium oxalate, with vitreous humour or urine as a corroborating sample ~ Blood with formalin only ~ Blood with heparin only ~ Serum with no preservative ~ A ~ Fluoride prevents post-mortem fermentation by microorganisms that can generate alcohol in the sample, and the vitreous humour is preferred because it is relatively protected from putrefaction and diffusion.
H ~ Why is a body packer who has swallowed cocaine-filled packets not given an emetic or a whole-bowel lavage with a stimulant purgative? ~ It is ineffective at moving packets ~ Rupture of a packet would deliver a massive lethal dose, so gentle polyethylene glycol lavage and surgical removal for obstruction or leakage are preferred ~ It causes electrolyte imbalance only ~ It is legally prohibited ~ B ~ Radiography or computed tomography counts the packets, and any sign of sympathomimetic toxicity in a cocaine packer is an indication for urgent laparotomy rather than continued observation.
M ~ Which withdrawal syndrome is potentially fatal and requires benzodiazepine cover, in contrast to one that is distressing but rarely lethal? ~ Alcohol and barbiturate withdrawal can be fatal from seizures and delirium, whereas opioid withdrawal is distressing but rarely lethal ~ Opioid withdrawal is fatal and alcohol withdrawal is not ~ Both are equally lethal ~ Cannabis withdrawal is the most dangerous ~ A ~ This difference guides management, with a tapering benzodiazepine and thiamine for alcohol, and symptomatic treatment or opioid substitution for opioid withdrawal.
H ~ Under which provision is the punishment for consumption of a small quantity of a narcotic drug for personal use distinguished from trafficking, and what is the principle? ~ Punishment under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act is graded by the quantity involved, with small quantity attracting far lighter punishment and immunity available for addicts volunteering for treatment ~ All quantities attract the same punishment ~ Quantity is irrelevant and only the drug type matters ~ Consumption is never an offence ~ A ~ The graded scheme of small, more than small but less than commercial, and commercial quantity is what determines both the sentence and whether bail is available, and the treatment immunity provision is designed to encourage addicts to seek help.
@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Identification: age, sex, dactylography, DNA, anthropometry ~ FM_ID ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Identification
E ~ Which fingerprint pattern is the commonest in the general population? ~ Loops ~ Whorls ~ Arches ~ Composite patterns ~ A ~ Loops account for roughly two thirds of patterns, whorls about a quarter and arches the remainder, and because the ridge pattern is fixed by about the fourth month of fetal life and never changes it remains the most practical means of identification.
M ~ Which teeth are used in the Gustafson method, and which change is the single most reliable indicator of increasing age? ~ Molars, with attrition being most reliable ~ Single-rooted teeth, with secondary dentine deposition and root transparency among the most reliable changes ~ Deciduous teeth, with root resorption ~ Canines only, with periodontal recession ~ B ~ The method scores several age-related changes together because any one of them varies with diet and oral hygiene, and root dentine transparency has proved the most consistent of them.
M ~ Which region of the human genome is examined in modern forensic DNA profiling? ~ Short tandem repeats at multiple independent loci ~ The entire coding sequence ~ Only mitochondrial DNA ~ Single nucleotide substitutions in exons ~ A ~ Multiple loci are typed together so the chance of a coincidental match becomes vanishingly small, and mitochondrial DNA is reserved for degraded remains or hair shafts because it is present in high copy number and maternally inherited.
E ~ Which skeletal feature is most useful for determining sex, and why? ~ The pelvis, because childbirth adaptations produce the greatest sexual dimorphism ~ The femur, because it is longest ~ The sternum, because it ossifies last ~ The scapula, because it is flat ~ A ~ A wider subpubic angle, a broader sciatic notch and a preauricular sulcus are the key features, and the skull is the next most useful with its heavier supraorbital ridges and mastoid processes in the male.
M ~ Which ossification event is most useful for determining whether a living person has completed 18 years of age? ~ Fusion of the lower end of the radius and the medial end of the clavicle in the late teens and early twenties ~ Appearance of the carpal centres ~ Eruption of the first permanent molar ~ Closure of the anterior fontanelle ~ A ~ Because bone age has a range of error, courts are told an age range rather than a single figure, and the combination of dental, radiological and physical findings is used rather than any one criterion.
H ~ Why is a tattoo of limited value in establishing identity even though it is a distinctive mark? ~ Tattoos fade, can be removed or altered, and pigment may be traced only to the regional lymph node after removal ~ Tattoos are never recorded ~ Tattoo pigment disappears completely after death ~ Tattoos cannot be photographed ~ A ~ Deliberate obliteration is common in criminals, so a suspicious scar over an area where a tattoo was recorded, with pigment demonstrable in the draining node, may itself be evidence.
H ~ Which method allows identification when only fragmentary charred remains are available, and what is its limitation? ~ Comparison of dental records and DNA from tooth pulp, limited by the need for an antemortem record or a reference relative ~ Fingerprinting, limited only by print quality ~ Anthropometry, limited by clothing ~ Superimposition, limited by camera type ~ A ~ Teeth resist heat better than any other tissue, so the dentition survives fires that destroy soft tissue, but without an antemortem chart or a comparison sample the profile obtained cannot be matched to a name.
E ~ What is the medico-legal significance of a scar in identification? ~ It indicates the approximate age of the injury and may correspond to a recorded mark, though it cannot indicate the weapon with certainty ~ It reliably indicates the weapon used ~ It disappears within a year ~ It permits exact dating of the injury ~ A ~ A recent scar is reddish and soft, becoming white, tough and contracted with time, and keloid formation and site can suggest the nature of the original wound without proving it.
@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Medical jurisprudence: negligence, consumer protection, PC-PNDT, MTP & transplantation acts ~ FM_JUR ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Medical Law and Ethics
M ~ Under the amended Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, up to what gestation may a pregnancy be terminated for a specified category of woman, and who decides beyond that? ~ Up to 24 weeks for categories such as survivors of rape and women with disability, with no upper limit for substantial fetal abnormality as decided by a state medical board ~ Up to 20 weeks in all cases with no exceptions ~ Up to 28 weeks in all cases ~ Up to 12 weeks only ~ A ~ The amendment also replaced the requirement for two opinions beyond 12 weeks with a single registered practitioner up to 20 weeks, and made a state medical board the authority for the fetal abnormality exception.
M ~ Which principle protects a doctor who has exercised the ordinary skill of a competent practitioner even though the outcome was poor? ~ The Bolam principle, that a doctor is not negligent if acting in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of professional opinion ~ Res ipsa loquitur ~ Contributory negligence ~ Therapeutic privilege ~ A ~ Indian courts adopted this reasoning in Jacob Mathew, adding that an error of judgement is not by itself negligence, and criminal negligence requires a much higher degree of culpability than civil negligence.
E ~ Which situation constitutes contributory negligence on the part of a patient? ~ Failure to follow clear instructions or to attend follow-up, which contributed to the poor outcome ~ Failure of the doctor to obtain consent ~ Injury caused by a nurse ~ Absence of medical records ~ A ~ It reduces rather than removes the practitioner liability, and it is the reason detailed advice at discharge should be documented so that non-compliance can be demonstrated.
M ~ Under the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act, which relatives are treated as near relatives whose donation does not require authorisation committee approval? ~ Spouse, parents, children, siblings, grandparents and grandchildren ~ Only spouse and children ~ Any person willing to donate ~ Only siblings ~ A ~ Donation by anyone outside this list requires clearance by an authorisation committee to exclude commercial dealing, and swap donation between two such pairs is permitted under defined conditions.
M ~ Under the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, which record-keeping requirement applies to a registered ultrasound centre? ~ Maintenance of Form F for every pregnant woman scanned, retained for the prescribed period, with a display that sex determination is not done here ~ No records are needed for routine obstetric scans ~ Records need be kept only for invasive tests ~ Records are kept for one month only ~ A ~ Failure to maintain the form is itself an offence irrespective of whether sex determination occurred, which is how the Act is most commonly enforced in practice.
E ~ Which type of consent is required before an intimate examination of a woman, and who else should be present? ~ Informed written consent, with a female attendant present during the examination ~ Implied consent alone ~ Oral consent from a relative ~ No consent is needed if the police request it ~ A ~ Examination without valid consent may amount to assault, and this holds even for an accused person, who cannot be examined against their will beyond what the law specifically permits.
H ~ What is the difference between civil and criminal medical negligence in terms of the standard of proof and the outcome? ~ Civil negligence requires proof on the balance of probabilities and results in compensation, whereas criminal negligence requires proof beyond reasonable doubt of gross negligence and can result in imprisonment ~ Both require proof beyond reasonable doubt ~ Civil negligence results in imprisonment ~ Criminal negligence requires only a complaint ~ A ~ Courts have insisted that a doctor should not be prosecuted criminally without an independent medical opinion supporting gross negligence, precisely to prevent defensive practice.
H ~ Which document must accompany a medico-legal case examination, and what is the significance of an omission in it? ~ A contemporaneous, accurate and legible record, because the court treats poor or altered records as adverse to the doctor and they cannot be reconstructed later ~ Records may be written after the court summons ~ Only the discharge summary matters ~ Records are optional in emergencies ~ A ~ Alteration of a record is regarded far more seriously than the original omission, so a correction should be made openly with a dated countersignature rather than by overwriting.
@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Sexual offences, infanticide & abortion law ~ FM_SEX ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Sexual Offences
M ~ Which Act governs the examination and reporting of sexual offences against a child, and what duty does it place on a doctor? ~ The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, which makes reporting mandatory and provides for free treatment and a child-friendly examination ~ The Indian Evidence Act, which makes reporting optional ~ The Consumer Protection Act ~ The Mental Healthcare Act ~ A ~ Failure to report is itself punishable, and the same law requires that the child not be repeatedly examined and that the examination be conducted in the presence of a parent or a person the child trusts.
E ~ Which specimens should be collected from a survivor of recent sexual assault for laboratory examination? ~ Vaginal and other relevant swabs, clothing, pubic hair combings, nail clippings and a blood sample, all sealed and handed over with a chain-of-custody record ~ Only a vaginal swab ~ Only clothing ~ Only a blood sample for pregnancy testing ~ A ~ The examination must also address emergency contraception, sexually transmitted infection prophylaxis and psychological support, because treatment of the survivor takes priority over evidence collection.
M ~ What is meant by the term precipitate labour, and why does it matter in a charge of infanticide? ~ Delivery so rapid and unexpected that the mother could not prepare, offering a defence that injuries to the newborn or failure to care for it were unintentional ~ A prolonged labour requiring intervention ~ Delivery of a macerated stillborn fetus ~ Induced labour ~ A ~ Head injury from a fall onto a hard surface, and an untied torn cord, may then be genuinely accidental, which is why the scene and the mother account are as important as the autopsy findings.
M ~ Which autopsy findings indicate that a newborn breathed after birth? ~ Aerated buoyant lungs reaching beyond the pericardium with a mottled marbled appearance, an expanded chest and air in the stomach ~ Collapsed dark red lungs ~ A closed foramen ovale ~ An unossified lower femoral epiphysis ~ A ~ The hydrostatic test is corroborative rather than conclusive because putrefaction gas and artificial respiration can both make an unbreathed lung float, so the findings are interpreted together.
E ~ Which finding suggests that a fetus was viable at the time of delivery? ~ A gestational age of about 28 weeks or more with a crown-heel length around 35 cm and an ossification centre in the lower end of the femur ~ Any gestational age beyond 12 weeks ~ Presence of vernix alone ~ Presence of lanugo alone ~ A ~ Viability concerns the capacity for independent existence and is separate from the question of whether the child actually breathed, and both questions must be answered separately at autopsy.
H ~ Why is the absence of injury in a survivor of sexual assault not evidence that assault did not occur? ~ Consent is a legal question, injuries are frequently absent particularly where there was submission through fear or where examination is delayed, and the hymen may stretch without tearing ~ Injuries are always present in genuine assault ~ Absence of injury proves consent ~ The examination is unreliable in all cases ~ A ~ This is why the report must describe findings factually without offering an opinion on consent, and why the two-finger test has been prohibited as both unscientific and degrading.
M ~ Which offence is committed when a doctor performs a termination of pregnancy at an unapproved place even with the woman consent? ~ An offence under the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, because the procedure must be done at a government hospital or an approved place by a registered practitioner ~ No offence if consent was given ~ An offence only if complications occur ~ An offence under the Transplantation Act ~ A ~ The Act protects the practitioner only when its conditions on indication, gestation, place and practitioner qualification are all satisfied, and otherwise the general law on causing miscarriage applies.
H ~ Which principle governs the confidentiality of a sexual assault survivor identity, and what is the exception? ~ Disclosure of the identity is prohibited by law, with the exception of communication to the investigating authority and the court as required for the case ~ Identity may be shared with the media if the case is serious ~ There is no restriction ~ Identity may be disclosed to relatives freely ~ A ~ The same protection applies to the medical record, so requests from anyone other than the survivor, the investigating officer or the court should be refused and referred to the court.
