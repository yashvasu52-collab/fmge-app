@ Medicine ~ Glomerular disease & nephrotic syndrome ~ MED_GN ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 314 (Glomerular Diseases)
E ~ A child with generalised oedema, proteinuria of 4 g per day, albumin 1.8 g/dL and normal complement most likely has: ~ Minimal change disease ~ Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis ~ IgA nephropathy ~ Membranous nephropathy ~ A ~ Minimal change disease accounts for most childhood nephrotic syndrome, shows only podocyte foot process effacement on electron microscopy and responds to corticosteroids without needing a biopsy first.
E ~ The commonest cause of nephrotic syndrome in a non-diabetic adult in India is: ~ Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis ~ Minimal change disease ~ Post-infectious glomerulonephritis ~ Alport syndrome ~ A ~ Its incidence has risen in adults worldwide, it responds poorly to steroids and it recurs in transplanted kidneys, which distinguishes it from minimal change disease.
E ~ Low C3 with normal C4 two weeks after a sore throat in a child with cola-coloured urine indicates: ~ Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis ~ Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis ~ Lupus nephritis ~ IgA nephropathy ~ A ~ Alternative pathway activation lowers C3 selectively, and complement normalises within eight weeks, which is a useful confirmatory follow-up test.
M ~ Frank haematuria occurring within 1 to 2 days of an upper respiratory infection suggests: ~ IgA nephropathy ~ Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis ~ Goodpasture syndrome ~ Thin basement membrane disease ~ A ~ Synpharyngitic haematuria distinguishes IgA nephropathy from the 1 to 3 week latency of post-streptococcal disease, and mesangial IgA deposits confirm it.
M ~ Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis with linear IgG deposition along the basement membrane and haemoptysis indicates: ~ Anti-glomerular basement membrane disease ~ Granulomatosis with polyangiitis ~ Lupus nephritis ~ Henoch-Schonlein purpura ~ A ~ Antibody against the alpha-3 chain of type IV collagen binds continuously along the membrane, and plasma exchange with cyclophosphamide and steroids is the treatment.
M ~ Adult membranous nephropathy is most often associated with antibodies against: ~ Phospholipase A2 receptor ~ Double-stranded DNA ~ Myeloperoxidase ~ Glomerular basement membrane ~ A ~ The antibody is present in about 70 percent of primary cases, and its absence should prompt a search for malignancy, hepatitis B or drugs as secondary causes.
M ~ The principal reason nephrotic patients are hypercoagulable is: ~ Urinary loss of antithrombin III with increased hepatic fibrinogen synthesis ~ Thrombocytopenia ~ Excess protein C ~ Reduced factor VIII ~ A ~ Loss of small anticoagulant proteins in the urine and compensatory hepatic synthesis of large procoagulants together favour thrombosis, classically renal vein thrombosis in membranous nephropathy.
H ~ A patient with hepatitis C, palpable purpura, arthralgia, low C4 and nephritic urine most likely has: ~ Cryoglobulinaemic membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis ~ Minimal change disease ~ Amyloidosis ~ Diabetic nephropathy ~ A ~ Cryoglobulins activate the classical pathway so C4 falls disproportionately, and treating the hepatitis C with direct-acting antivirals is the primary therapy.
H ~ Nephrotic-range proteinuria with sensorineural deafness and haematuria in a young man suggests: ~ Alport syndrome ~ Fabry disease ~ Thin basement membrane nephropathy ~ Nail-patella syndrome ~ A ~ X-linked mutations in the alpha-5 chain of type IV collagen affect the cochlea, lens and glomerular basement membrane, which shows lamellated splitting on electron microscopy.
@ Medicine ~ HIV/AIDS: staging, ART & opportunistic infections ~ MED_HIV ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 202 (Human Immunodeficiency Virus Disease)
E ~ Under current national guidelines antiretroviral therapy in an HIV-positive adult is started: ~ At diagnosis regardless of CD4 count ~ Only when CD4 falls below 350 ~ Only when CD4 falls below 200 ~ Only when an opportunistic infection occurs ~ A ~ The START and TEMPRANO trials showed benefit at every CD4 stratum, and treatment as prevention makes an undetectable viral load non-transmissible.
E ~ The preferred first-line antiretroviral regimen for adults in India is: ~ Tenofovir with lamivudine and dolutegravir ~ Zidovudine with lamivudine and nevirapine ~ Stavudine with lamivudine and efavirenz ~ Tenofovir with emtricitabine alone ~ A ~ Dolutegravir has a high genetic barrier to resistance, rapid viral suppression and few interactions, which is why it replaced efavirenz as the anchor drug.
E ~ Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis in HIV is started when the CD4 count falls below: ~ 200 cells per microlitre ~ 500 cells per microlitre ~ 350 cells per microlitre ~ 50 cells per microlitre ~ A ~ Below 200 the risk of Pneumocystis pneumonia rises sharply, and the same prophylaxis also covers toxoplasmosis and isosporiasis.
M ~ An HIV patient with CD4 of 40 develops progressive visual loss with perivascular haemorrhages and exudates on fundoscopy. The likely cause is: ~ Cytomegalovirus retinitis ~ Toxoplasma chorioretinitis ~ HIV retinopathy ~ Candida endophthalmitis ~ A ~ The pizza-pie appearance of haemorrhage and necrosis at CD4 below 50 is characteristic, and treatment is ganciclovir or valganciclovir alongside antiretroviral therapy.
M ~ Multiple ring-enhancing lesions in the basal ganglia of an HIV patient with CD4 of 80 are most likely due to: ~ Toxoplasma encephalitis ~ Primary CNS lymphoma ~ Tuberculoma ~ Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy ~ A ~ Empirical pyrimethamine with sulfadiazine is given and response assessed at two weeks; a solitary periventricular lesion positive for Epstein-Barr virus DNA favours lymphoma instead.
M ~ Paradoxical worsening of tuberculosis a few weeks after starting antiretroviral therapy is called: ~ Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome ~ Drug-resistant tuberculosis ~ Antiretroviral failure ~ Drug-induced hepatitis ~ A ~ Restored immunity mounts an inflammatory response to residual antigen; antiretrovirals are continued and steroids used for severe cases, which is why tuberculosis treatment is started first.
M ~ Prevention of mother-to-child transmission is most effective when: ~ Maternal viral suppression is achieved with lifelong antiretroviral therapy ~ Only intrapartum nevirapine is given ~ Only infant prophylaxis is given ~ Caesarean section is done in all cases ~ A ~ Transmission risk falls below 2 percent with sustained maternal suppression, which is the basis of the lifelong therapy approach for all pregnant women.
H ~ A CD4 count of 150 with dysphagia and white plaques not responding to topical therapy indicates: ~ Oesophageal candidiasis, an AIDS-defining illness ~ Oral hairy leukoplakia ~ Herpes oesophagitis ~ Reflux oesophagitis ~ A ~ Candidiasis extending below the oropharynx is stage 4 disease and warrants systemic fluconazole with initiation of antiretroviral therapy.
H ~ Abacavir hypersensitivity is predicted by the presence of: ~ HLA-B*5701 ~ HLA-B27 ~ HLA-DR3 ~ HLA-B*1502 ~ A ~ Screening before prescription has almost eliminated the reaction, which is a systemic multi-organ hypersensitivity that must never be rechallenged.
@ Medicine ~ Heart failure & valvular disease ~ MED_HFV ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 252 (Heart Failure)
E ~ A plasma NT-proBNP within the normal range in a breathless patient is most useful because it: ~ Effectively excludes heart failure as the cause ~ Confirms heart failure ~ Grades severity ~ Indicates ischaemia ~ A ~ The peptide has a high negative predictive value, so a normal level in an untreated patient redirects the diagnosis towards lung disease or anaemia.
E ~ Four drug classes proven to reduce mortality in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction are: ~ Beta blocker, renin-angiotensin blocker or ARNI, mineralocorticoid antagonist and SGLT2 inhibitor ~ Digoxin, furosemide, nitrate and aspirin ~ Amlodipine, statin, aspirin and diuretic ~ Ivabradine, digoxin, nitrate and hydralazine ~ A ~ Loop diuretics relieve congestion but do not change survival, whereas the four pillars each independently reduce mortality and should be started early at low dose.
M ~ A mid-diastolic rumbling murmur at the apex with a loud first sound and an opening snap indicates: ~ Mitral stenosis ~ Mitral regurgitation ~ Aortic stenosis ~ Tricuspid regurgitation ~ A ~ A pliable stenosed valve closes forcefully and snaps open, and the shorter the interval from the second sound to the snap the tighter the valve.
M ~ An asymptomatic patient with severe aortic stenosis and an ejection fraction of 45 percent should be advised: ~ Aortic valve replacement ~ Continued surveillance ~ Balloon valvuloplasty ~ Vasodilator therapy ~ A ~ Left ventricular systolic dysfunction attributable to the valve is a class I indication for intervention even without symptoms, because outcomes deteriorate sharply once the ventricle fails.
M ~ The most common cause of mitral stenosis in India remains: ~ Rheumatic heart disease ~ Degenerative calcification ~ Congenital parachute valve ~ Infective endocarditis ~ A ~ Rheumatic fever remains prevalent, characteristically affects young women and produces commissural fusion amenable to balloon mitral valvotomy.
M ~ A collapsing pulse with a wide pulse pressure and an early diastolic murmur at the left sternal edge indicates: ~ Aortic regurgitation ~ Aortic stenosis ~ Mitral stenosis ~ Pulmonary regurgitation ~ A ~ Diastolic runoff into the ventricle lowers diastolic pressure and raises stroke volume, producing the water-hammer pulse and the peripheral signs of Corrigan and de Musset.
H ~ Sacubitril-valsartan should not be started within 36 hours of an ACE inhibitor because: ~ Combined neprilysin and ACE inhibition causes bradykinin accumulation and angioedema ~ It causes hyperkalaemia ~ It causes hypoglycaemia ~ It causes hepatotoxicity ~ A ~ Both drugs impair bradykinin degradation, so a washout period is mandatory, and this risk is also why the combination is avoided in patients with prior angioedema.
@ Medicine ~ Hypertension: evaluation, secondary causes, management ~ MED_HTN ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 271 (Hypertensive Vascular Disease)
E ~ The blood pressure threshold for stage 1 hypertension by the 2017 ACC/AHA definition is: ~ 130/80 mmHg ~ 140/90 mmHg ~ 120/80 mmHg ~ 160/100 mmHg ~ A ~ The reclassification followed SPRINT, which showed benefit from intensive lowering, although Indian and European guidelines still use 140/90 for the drug-treatment threshold.
E ~ Hypertension in a young woman with an abdominal bruit and asymmetric renal size suggests: ~ Fibromuscular dysplasia of the renal artery ~ Atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis ~ Primary hyperaldosteronism ~ Coarctation of the aorta ~ A ~ Fibromuscular dysplasia gives a string-of-beads appearance on angiography in young women, whereas atherosclerotic disease affects older patients with other vascular risk factors.
E ~ Radiofemoral delay with upper limb hypertension in a young adult indicates: ~ Coarctation of the aorta ~ Takayasu arteritis ~ Renal artery stenosis ~ Essential hypertension ~ A ~ The obstruction is distal to the left subclavian, so the femoral pulse is delayed and weak, and rib notching from collateral intercostal arteries may be visible on chest radiograph.
M ~ Episodic headache, palpitations and sweating with paroxysmal hypertension should be evaluated by: ~ Plasma free metanephrines ~ Serum cortisol ~ Renal Doppler ~ Thyroid function tests ~ A ~ Metanephrines are produced continuously within the tumour so they are more sensitive than catecholamines, which are released episodically and may be normal between attacks.
M ~ The most appropriate first-line antihypertensive in a 35-year-old with proteinuric chronic kidney disease is: ~ An ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker ~ A thiazide diuretic ~ A calcium channel blocker ~ A beta blocker ~ A ~ Efferent arteriolar dilatation lowers intraglomerular pressure and reduces proteinuria, slowing progression beyond the effect of blood pressure lowering alone.
M ~ In a hypertensive emergency with encephalopathy the mean arterial pressure should be lowered: ~ By no more than 25 percent in the first hour ~ To normal within 15 minutes ~ By 50 percent in the first hour ~ Only after 24 hours ~ A ~ Chronic hypertension shifts cerebral autoregulation rightward, so precipitous reduction causes watershed cerebral, renal and coronary ischaemia.
M ~ Resistant hypertension is defined as blood pressure above target despite: ~ Three drugs at optimal dose including a diuretic ~ Two drugs ~ Any single drug ~ Four drugs ~ A ~ Once adherence and white-coat effect are excluded, the commonest identifiable secondary cause in this group is primary hyperaldosteronism, and spironolactone is the usual fourth agent.
H ~ A hypertensive patient with hypokalaemia, low renin and low aldosterone and a family history suggests: ~ Liddle syndrome ~ Conn syndrome ~ Renal artery stenosis ~ Phaeochromocytoma ~ A ~ A gain-of-function mutation of the epithelial sodium channel causes sodium retention independent of aldosterone, so it responds to amiloride or triamterene and not to spironolactone.
H ~ The preferred antihypertensive combination in a hypertensive patient with acute aortic dissection is: ~ Intravenous beta blocker followed by a vasodilator ~ Vasodilator alone ~ Calcium channel blocker alone ~ Diuretic alone ~ A ~ Reducing the rate of rise of aortic pressure matters as much as the pressure itself, and giving a vasodilator first causes reflex tachycardia that increases shear stress and extends the dissection.
@ Medicine ~ IHD / acute MI: diagnosis & management ~ MED_IAM ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 269 (ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction)
E ~ ST elevation in leads II, III and aVF indicates infarction of the: ~ Inferior wall, usually right coronary artery territory ~ Anterior wall ~ Lateral wall ~ Posterior wall only ~ A ~ Right coronary occlusion also risks right ventricular infarction and heart block, so right-sided leads should be recorded before nitrates are given.
E ~ The preferred reperfusion strategy for ST-elevation myocardial infarction presenting within 12 hours to a PCI-capable centre is: ~ Primary percutaneous coronary intervention within 90 minutes ~ Thrombolysis in all cases ~ Elective angiography at 7 days ~ Medical therapy alone ~ A ~ Primary angioplasty gives better vessel patency, fewer reinfarctions and less intracranial haemorrhage than fibrinolysis when door-to-balloon time can be kept short.
M ~ Hypotension after sublingual nitrate in an inferior myocardial infarction should raise suspicion of: ~ Right ventricular infarction ~ Cardiac tamponade ~ Aortic dissection ~ Pulmonary embolism ~ A ~ The right ventricle is preload dependent, so venodilatation collapses filling; treatment is fluid loading and avoidance of nitrates and diuretics.
M ~ The most specific biomarker for myocardial necrosis is: ~ Cardiac troponin I or T ~ Creatine kinase MB ~ Myoglobin ~ Lactate dehydrogenase ~ A ~ Troponins are cardiac-isoform specific and rise within 3 hours and remain elevated for up to two weeks, whereas CK-MB is used mainly to detect reinfarction because it clears faster.
M ~ A new pansystolic murmur with acute pulmonary oedema three days after myocardial infarction most likely indicates: ~ Papillary muscle rupture with acute mitral regurgitation ~ Ventricular aneurysm ~ Pericarditis ~ Reinfarction ~ A ~ The posteromedial papillary muscle has a single blood supply and is vulnerable in inferior infarction; echocardiography confirms it and surgery is urgent.
H ~ Persistent ST elevation with a bulging apical impulse six weeks after anterior myocardial infarction indicates: ~ Left ventricular aneurysm ~ Dressler syndrome ~ Reinfarction ~ Free wall rupture ~ A ~ The thinned scarred wall neither depolarises nor contracts normally, and mural thrombus within the aneurysm is an indication for anticoagulation.
H ~ Fever, pleuritic chest pain and a pericardial rub two to six weeks after myocardial infarction describes: ~ Dressler syndrome ~ Acute pericarditis of infarction ~ Pulmonary embolism ~ Reinfarction ~ A ~ It is an autoimmune post-cardiac injury syndrome treated with aspirin or colchicine; other NSAIDs and steroids are avoided early because they impair infarct healing.
@ Medicine ~ Infective endocarditis, rheumatic fever & pericardial disease ~ MED_IE ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 128 (Infective Endocarditis)
E ~ The commonest organism causing acute native valve infective endocarditis in intravenous drug users is: ~ Staphylococcus aureus ~ Streptococcus viridans ~ Enterococcus faecalis ~ Coxiella burnetii ~ A ~ Skin flora inoculated directly into the bloodstream attacks the tricuspid valve, producing right-sided endocarditis with septic pulmonary emboli.
E ~ The modified Duke criteria major criteria are: ~ Positive blood cultures with typical organisms and evidence of endocardial involvement ~ Fever and splinter haemorrhages ~ Predisposing heart condition and vascular phenomena ~ Raised ESR and anaemia ~ A ~ Two major, or one major with three minor, or five minor criteria establish a definite diagnosis, and echocardiography supplies the endocardial evidence.
E ~ The Jones criteria major manifestation that occurs latest after streptococcal infection is: ~ Sydenham chorea ~ Carditis ~ Migratory polyarthritis ~ Erythema marginatum ~ A ~ Chorea may appear months later when other features and even the antistreptolysin titre have resolved, so it is accepted alone as sufficient for the diagnosis.
M ~ Secondary prophylaxis after rheumatic fever with carditis and residual valve disease should continue: ~ For at least 10 years or until age 40, sometimes lifelong ~ For 5 years only ~ For 1 year ~ Until symptoms resolve ~ A ~ Benzathine penicillin every three weeks prevents recurrent attacks that cause cumulative valve damage, and duration is graded by the presence and severity of carditis.
M ~ A patient with subacute endocarditis develops painless erythematous macules on the palms and soles. These are: ~ Janeway lesions from septic emboli ~ Osler nodes ~ Roth spots ~ Splinter haemorrhages ~ A ~ Janeway lesions are non-tender and embolic, whereas Osler nodes are painful pulp nodules attributed to immune complex deposition.
M ~ Blood cultures in suspected infective endocarditis should be taken as: ~ Three sets from separate sites before antibiotics ~ A single set after antibiotics ~ One set from a central line ~ Two sets only after fever settles ~ A ~ Bacteraemia is continuous, so separated sets increase yield and distinguish true infection from contamination, and antibiotics before sampling are the commonest cause of culture-negative disease.
M ~ Pulsus paradoxus greater than 10 mmHg with muffled heart sounds and raised jugular venous pressure indicates: ~ Cardiac tamponade ~ Constrictive pericarditis ~ Tension pneumothorax only ~ Restrictive cardiomyopathy ~ A ~ Inspiratory ventricular interdependence within a fixed pericardial volume exaggerates the normal inspiratory fall in systolic pressure, and urgent pericardiocentesis is required.
H ~ Prominent x and y descents with a pericardial knock and calcification on chest radiograph indicate: ~ Constrictive pericarditis ~ Cardiac tamponade ~ Tricuspid stenosis ~ Superior vena caval obstruction ~ A ~ Rapid early diastolic filling into a rigid pericardium gives a steep y descent and an early third sound, in contrast to tamponade where the y descent is blunted.
H ~ Culture-negative endocarditis with a history of contact with farm animals and parturient cats suggests: ~ Coxiella burnetii ~ Bartonella henselae ~ Tropheryma whipplei ~ Brucella melitensis ~ A ~ Q fever endocarditis is diagnosed serologically by phase I IgG titres and needs prolonged doxycycline with hydroxychloroquine rather than standard regimens.
