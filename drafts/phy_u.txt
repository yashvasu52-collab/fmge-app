@ Physiology ~ GIT secretions, motility & absorption ~ PY_GIT ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. The Gastrointestinal Tract
E ~ Which cell secretes intrinsic factor, and what is its function? ~ The gastric parietal cell, binding vitamin B12 for absorption in the terminal ileum ~ The chief cell, activating pepsinogen ~ The G cell, stimulating acid ~ The enterochromaffin-like cell, releasing histamine ~ A ~ Loss of parietal cells in autoimmune gastritis therefore causes both achlorhydria and B12 deficiency, and this is why gastrectomy demands lifelong B12 replacement.
E ~ Which hormone is released by the duodenum in response to acid and stimulates pancreatic bicarbonate secretion? ~ Secretin ~ Cholecystokinin ~ Gastrin ~ Motilin ~ A ~ Cholecystokinin responds mainly to fat and protein and drives enzyme secretion and gallbladder contraction, so the two hormones divide the aqueous and enzymatic components of the pancreatic response.
E ~ Which phase accounts for the largest proportion of gastric acid secretion in response to a meal? ~ The gastric phase, mediated by distension and by peptides acting on G cells ~ The cephalic phase ~ The intestinal phase ~ The interdigestive phase ~ A ~ The cephalic phase contributes about 30 percent through the vagus, the gastric phase about 60 percent, and the intestinal phase only a small amount, which is why vagotomy reduces but does not abolish acid output.
M ~ Which contraction pattern clears the stomach and small intestine of residue between meals and is driven by motilin? ~ The migrating motor complex ~ Segmentation contractions ~ Mass movements ~ Tonic contraction of the pylorus ~ A ~ Loss of this housekeeping activity contributes to bacterial overgrowth, and erythromycin, a motilin agonist, is used clinically to promote gastric emptying.
M ~ Which mechanism absorbs the great majority of dietary iron, and where? ~ Divalent metal transporter 1 on the duodenal enterocyte brush border, with ferroportin exporting iron basolaterally ~ Passive diffusion in the ileum ~ Active transport in the colon ~ Endocytosis of transferrin in the jejunum ~ A ~ Hepcidin degrades ferroportin, so inflammation reduces iron export from the enterocyte and causes the functional iron deficiency of anaemia of chronic disease.
M ~ Which nutrient is absorbed by a mechanism that depends on bile salt micelle formation? ~ Long-chain fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins ~ Glucose ~ Amino acids ~ Medium-chain triglycerides ~ A ~ Medium-chain triglycerides are absorbed directly into the portal blood without micelles, which is why they are useful in cholestasis and lymphatic obstruction where long-chain fat absorption fails.
M ~ Which reflex explains the propulsive wave ahead of a bolus in the intestine? ~ The peristaltic reflex with relaxation distally and contraction proximally, mediated by the enteric nervous system ~ The gastrocolic reflex ~ The enterogastric reflex ~ The rectosphincteric reflex ~ A ~ Nitric oxide and vasoactive intestinal peptide mediate descending relaxation while acetylcholine and substance P mediate ascending contraction, and loss of the inhibitory neurons causes achalasia and Hirschsprung disease.
H ~ Why does the pancreas not normally digest itself despite secreting powerful proteases? ~ Enzymes are secreted as inactive zymogens, packaged with a trypsin inhibitor, and activated only in the duodenum by enterokinase ~ The pancreas secretes no proteases ~ Bicarbonate inactivates them ~ They are activated only by bile ~ A ~ Failure of these safeguards, as with a PRSS1 or SPINK1 mutation or ductal obstruction, permits intrapancreatic trypsin activation and initiates pancreatitis.
H ~ Why does resection of the terminal ileum cause both fat malabsorption and gallstones? ~ Loss of bile salt reabsorption depletes the bile salt pool, reducing micelle formation and raising the cholesterol saturation of bile ~ It removes the gallbladder ~ It increases pancreatic enzyme output ~ It causes bacterial overgrowth only ~ A ~ Unabsorbed bile salts also enter the colon where they cause a secretory diarrhoea and bind calcium, increasing oxalate absorption and the risk of oxalate renal stones.
@ Physiology ~ Hormone actions & feedback ~ PY_HAF ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Introduction to Endocrinology
E ~ Which class of hormone acts through an intracellular receptor to alter gene transcription, giving a slow onset and long duration? ~ Steroid and thyroid hormones ~ Peptide hormones ~ Catecholamines ~ Eicosanoids ~ A ~ Because they must reach the nucleus and induce protein synthesis, their effects take hours and persist, whereas peptide hormones acting on surface receptors act within seconds to minutes.
E ~ Which second messenger mediates the action of parathyroid hormone on its target cells? ~ Cyclic AMP through a Gs-coupled receptor ~ Inositol trisphosphate ~ Cyclic GMP ~ Calcium alone without a nucleotide ~ A ~ Loss of this signalling downstream of the receptor causes pseudohypoparathyroidism, where hormone levels are high but the target tissue does not respond.
M ~ Which pattern of feedback describes the effect of cortisol on the hypothalamus and pituitary? ~ Long-loop negative feedback on both CRH and ACTH secretion ~ Positive feedback on ACTH ~ Short-loop feedback only ~ Ultra-short-loop feedback only ~ A ~ Chronic exogenous glucocorticoid suppresses the whole axis and causes adrenal atrophy, which is why abrupt withdrawal after prolonged therapy precipitates adrenal crisis.
M ~ Which hormonal relationship is an example of physiological positive feedback? ~ Oestrogen triggering the mid-cycle luteinising hormone surge ~ Thyroxine on TSH ~ Cortisol on ACTH ~ Insulin on glucose ~ A ~ Positive feedback is inherently unstable so it is used where an explosive all-or-none event is needed, as in ovulation, parturition with oxytocin, and the clotting cascade.
M ~ Why is measuring a stimulating hormone often more sensitive than measuring the target hormone in detecting early gland failure? ~ Negative feedback amplifies the change, so the trophic hormone moves outside its range before the target hormone does ~ Target hormones are unstable in serum ~ Trophic hormones have longer half-lives ~ Target hormones are protein bound ~ A ~ This is why a raised TSH with normal free thyroxine defines subclinical hypothyroidism, and why TSH is the screening test of choice for thyroid dysfunction.
H ~ Why does a hormone bound to plasma protein have a longer half-life and a delayed onset of action? ~ Only the free fraction is biologically active and available for clearance, so binding creates a reservoir ~ Bound hormone is more potent ~ Binding proteins metabolise the hormone ~ Bound hormone enters cells directly ~ A ~ Changes in binding protein alter total but not free hormone, which is why pregnancy raises total thyroxine without causing thyrotoxicosis and why free hormone assays are preferred.
H ~ Why does the same hormone produce different effects in different tissues? ~ Receptor subtype expression and post-receptor signalling differ between tissues ~ The hormone changes structure in transit ~ Hormone concentration differs between tissues ~ Only one tissue has receptors ~ A ~ Adrenaline causing vasoconstriction through alpha-1 receptors in skin and vasodilatation through beta-2 receptors in muscle is the classic illustration, and it is the basis of selective drug design.
@ Physiology ~ Lung volumes, compliance, V/Q & mechanics of breathing ~ PY_LNG ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Pulmonary Ventilation
E ~ Which lung volume cannot be measured by spirometry and requires helium dilution or body plethysmography? ~ Residual volume, and therefore functional residual capacity and total lung capacity ~ Tidal volume ~ Inspiratory reserve volume ~ Vital capacity ~ A ~ Because the residual volume cannot be exhaled it cannot be measured by a volume-displacement method, which is why restrictive disease is confirmed by a reduced total lung capacity rather than by a reduced vital capacity alone.
E ~ At functional residual capacity, which two forces are in equilibrium? ~ The inward elastic recoil of the lung and the outward recoil of the chest wall ~ Inspiratory and expiratory muscle activity ~ Surface tension and airway resistance ~ Pleural and alveolar pressure ~ A ~ Because it is a passive balance point, functional residual capacity falls when lung recoil increases as in fibrosis and rises when it decreases as in emphysema.
E ~ Which structure produces surfactant, and what is its principal effect? ~ Type II alveolar cells, lowering surface tension and increasing compliance ~ Type I alveolar cells, forming the diffusion barrier ~ Alveolar macrophages, clearing debris ~ Club cells, secreting mucus ~ A ~ By reducing surface tension more in small alveoli than large, surfactant also stabilises alveoli of different sizes against collapse according to the Laplace relationship.
M ~ In an upright lung, which region has the highest ventilation-perfusion ratio and why? ~ The apex, because perfusion falls more than ventilation with height owing to gravity ~ The base, because ventilation is greatest there ~ The mid-zone, where both are equal ~ The apex, because ventilation is greatest there ~ A ~ Both ventilation and perfusion are greater at the base, but the gradient in perfusion is steeper, so the apex behaves as relative dead space and the base as relative shunt.
M ~ Which change distinguishes an obstructive from a restrictive pattern on spirometry? ~ A reduced FEV1 to FVC ratio in obstruction, with a normal or raised ratio and a reduced total lung capacity in restriction ~ A reduced FVC in obstruction only ~ A raised residual volume in restriction ~ A normal flow-volume loop in obstruction ~ A ~ Because both patterns can reduce the absolute FEV1, it is the ratio and the lung volumes rather than the FEV1 alone that separate them.
M ~ Which physiological dead space measurement uses the Bohr equation with expired and arterial carbon dioxide? ~ Physiological dead space, which includes both anatomical and alveolar dead space ~ Anatomical dead space alone ~ Alveolar ventilation ~ Closing volume ~ A ~ Fowler's nitrogen washout measures anatomical dead space, and the difference between the two indicates alveolar dead space from areas ventilated but not perfused, as in pulmonary embolism.
M ~ Which factor most increases the work of breathing in a patient with severe airflow obstruction? ~ Increased airway resistance with dynamic hyperinflation flattening the diaphragm ~ Reduced compliance ~ Reduced dead space ~ Increased surfactant ~ A ~ Air trapping places the inspiratory muscles at a mechanical disadvantage and imposes an intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure that must be overcome before flow begins.
H ~ Why does breathing 100 percent oxygen correct hypoxaemia due to ventilation-perfusion mismatch but not that due to a true shunt? ~ Oxygen cannot reach alveoli that are not ventilated at all, whereas poorly ventilated units can still be enriched ~ Shunted blood carries no haemoglobin ~ Oxygen increases pulmonary shunting ~ Diffusion limits oxygen uptake in shunt ~ A ~ This difference is the physiological basis of the shunt fraction calculation and of the clinical distinction between the two mechanisms at the bedside.
H ~ Why does the closing volume rise with age and in the supine position, and what is the consequence? ~ Reduced elastic recoil and increased pleural pressure at the base allow small airways to close during tidal breathing, causing atelectasis and hypoxaemia ~ Closing volume falls with age ~ It has no clinical consequence ~ It reflects increased compliance of the chest wall ~ A ~ When closing volume exceeds functional residual capacity, dependent airways close during normal breathing, which is why postoperative hypoxaemia is commoner in older and supine patients.
@ Physiology ~ Muscle physiology: skeletal, smooth & cardiac contraction ~ PY_MUS ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Contraction of Skeletal Muscle
E ~ Which event initiates cross-bridge cycling after calcium is released in skeletal muscle? ~ Calcium binds troponin C, moving tropomyosin to expose the actin binding site ~ Calcium binds myosin directly ~ ATP binds actin ~ Calmodulin activates myosin light chain kinase ~ A ~ Smooth muscle lacks troponin, so calcium there acts through calmodulin and myosin light chain kinase, which is a key difference in the regulation of the two muscle types.
E ~ Which structure in skeletal muscle couples membrane depolarisation to calcium release? ~ The triad of a T-tubule with two terminal cisternae, where the dihydropyridine receptor gates the ryanodine receptor ~ The neuromuscular junction ~ The sarcolemma alone ~ The M line ~ A ~ Skeletal muscle uses mechanical coupling between the two receptors so it does not need extracellular calcium, whereas cardiac muscle requires calcium entry to trigger release.
E ~ Which band of the sarcomere shortens during contraction? ~ The I band and the H zone, while the A band remains constant ~ The A band only ~ The thick filaments themselves ~ The Z line width ~ A ~ Because the A band corresponds to the length of the thick filament, its constancy during shortening is direct evidence for the sliding filament mechanism.
M ~ Which difference explains why cardiac muscle cannot be tetanised? ~ Its long action potential plateau makes the refractory period nearly as long as the contraction ~ It lacks calcium channels ~ It has no sarcoplasmic reticulum ~ It is not electrically coupled ~ A ~ The calcium-dependent plateau protects the heart from sustained contraction, which would abolish filling, whereas skeletal muscle has a short action potential relative to its twitch and so summates readily.
M ~ Which mechanism explains calcium-induced calcium release in cardiac muscle? ~ Calcium entering through L-type channels triggers further release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through ryanodine receptors ~ Direct mechanical coupling of the two receptors ~ Sodium-calcium exchange only ~ Calmodulin-dependent release ~ A ~ Because the trigger comes from outside the cell, cardiac contractility is sensitive to extracellular calcium and to anything that alters L-type channel function, such as calcium channel blockers.
M ~ Which feature of smooth muscle allows it to maintain tension at low energy cost? ~ The latch state, in which dephosphorylated cross-bridges remain attached with slow cycling ~ A larger number of mitochondria ~ Troponin-based regulation ~ A well-developed sarcoplasmic reticulum ~ A ~ This is why sphincters and vascular tone can be sustained for hours without fatigue, and it explains the slow, sustained nature of smooth muscle contraction.
M ~ Which type of skeletal muscle fibre is recruited first during a graded voluntary contraction? ~ Slow oxidative type I fibres, because their motor neurons are smallest ~ Fast glycolytic type IIb fibres ~ Both simultaneously ~ Fast oxidative type IIa fibres ~ A ~ Henneman's size principle means smaller motor neurons with lower thresholds fire first, giving fine control at low force and recruiting powerful fatigable units only as demand rises.
H ~ Why does rigor mortis develop after death? ~ ATP depletion prevents detachment of myosin from actin, so cross-bridges remain locked ~ Calcium is extruded from the cytoplasm ~ Tropomyosin covers the actin site ~ Acidosis dissolves the filaments ~ A ~ ATP is required not for the power stroke but for cross-bridge release, and rigor resolves only when autolytic enzymes degrade the proteins.
H ~ Why does eccentric exercise cause more muscle damage and delayed soreness than concentric exercise at the same load? ~ Fewer active fibres bear a higher force per fibre while lengthening, causing sarcomere disruption ~ It uses more ATP ~ It produces more lactate ~ It recruits only slow fibres ~ A ~ The mechanical rather than metabolic nature of the injury explains why soreness peaks at 24 to 48 hours and why a repeated bout confers protection.
@ Physiology ~ O2/CO2 transport, hypoxia ~ PY_OCT ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Transport of Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide
E ~ Which change shifts the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve to the right? ~ A rise in temperature, carbon dioxide, hydrogen ion or 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate ~ A fall in temperature ~ A fall in 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate ~ Alkalosis ~ A ~ A right shift lowers affinity and favours unloading in metabolically active tissue, and the Bohr effect describes the specific contribution of carbon dioxide and hydrogen ion.
E ~ Which form of hypoxia is characterised by a normal arterial oxygen tension with reduced oxygen-carrying capacity? ~ Anaemic hypoxia ~ Hypoxic hypoxia ~ Stagnant hypoxia ~ Histotoxic hypoxia ~ A ~ Because dissolved oxygen tension is normal, pulse oximetry and blood gas analysis can be normal while delivery is severely reduced, which is also true of carbon monoxide poisoning.
E ~ Which type of hypoxia occurs in cyanide poisoning? ~ Histotoxic hypoxia, from inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase ~ Anaemic hypoxia ~ Hypoxic hypoxia ~ Stagnant hypoxia ~ A ~ Because tissues cannot use the oxygen delivered, venous oxygen content rises and the arteriovenous difference narrows, with lactic acidosis despite adequate delivery.
M ~ Why does carbon monoxide cause tissue hypoxia out of proportion to the reduction in oxygen-carrying capacity? ~ It also shifts the dissociation curve to the left, impairing unloading of the oxygen that remains bound ~ It raises 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate ~ It reduces cardiac output only ~ It inhibits cytochrome oxidase exclusively ~ A ~ Pulse oximetry cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin, so saturation reads falsely normal and co-oximetry is needed.
M ~ Which mechanism accounts for the majority of carbon dioxide transport in blood? ~ Conversion to bicarbonate within the red cell by carbonic anhydrase with the chloride shift ~ Dissolved carbon dioxide in plasma ~ Carbaminohaemoglobin ~ Carbonic acid in plasma ~ A ~ About 70 percent travels as bicarbonate, and the Haldane effect describes how deoxygenation of haemoglobin increases its capacity to carry carbon dioxide.
M ~ Which physiological adaptation to high altitude appears within hours and which takes weeks? ~ Hyperventilation with respiratory alkalosis within hours, and increased haematocrit and 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate over weeks ~ Increased haematocrit within hours ~ Increased cardiac output over weeks ~ Reduced ventilation over weeks ~ A ~ Renal bicarbonate excretion over days removes the alkalotic brake on ventilation, which is the mechanism acetazolamide accelerates in the prevention of acute mountain sickness.
M ~ Which measurement best reflects the adequacy of tissue oxygen delivery in a critically ill patient? ~ Central venous or mixed venous oxygen saturation together with lactate ~ Arterial oxygen tension alone ~ Haemoglobin concentration alone ~ Respiratory rate ~ A ~ Because delivery is the product of cardiac output and oxygen content, venous saturation integrates both with consumption, and a rising lactate indicates that delivery has become inadequate.
H ~ Why does fetal haemoglobin have a higher oxygen affinity than adult haemoglobin? ~ Gamma chains bind 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate poorly, so the curve lies to the left ~ It has more haem groups ~ It has a higher iron content ~ It binds carbon dioxide better ~ A ~ The leftward shift allows the fetus to extract oxygen across the placenta from maternal blood at low tension, and the double Bohr effect further assists transfer.
H ~ Why does supplemental oxygen relieve hypoxaemia at altitude but not correct the underlying problem of a low inspired partial pressure? ~ Barometric pressure and therefore inspired oxygen tension remain low, so oxygen raises the fraction rather than restoring the ambient pressure ~ Oxygen cannot diffuse at altitude ~ Haemoglobin is saturated already ~ Ventilation cannot increase further ~ A ~ Descent remains the definitive treatment for severe altitude illness, because it restores the driving pressure rather than compensating for its loss.
