@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Thermal, electrical & lightning injuries; burns ~ FM_THR ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Thermal Deaths
E ~ Which method estimates the burnt surface area in an adult for prognosis and medico-legal reporting? ~ The rule of nines, allocating 9 percent to each upper limb and head, 18 percent to each lower limb and to each of the front and back of the trunk ~ The rule of fives ~ Measurement of blister number ~ The Gustafson score ~ A ~ A child needs the Lund and Browder chart because the head accounts for a much larger proportion, and mortality rises with the area burnt, the depth and the presence of inhalational injury.
M ~ Which gastrointestinal complication characteristically follows extensive burns, and when does it appear? ~ Curling ulcer of the duodenum, appearing in the first days to weeks ~ Cushing ulcer of the oesophagus ~ Mallory-Weiss tear ~ Boerhaave perforation ~ A ~ Splanchnic hypoperfusion and stress underlie it, so it belongs to the group of remote complications along with septicaemia, acute kidney injury and adult respiratory distress that determine late mortality.
M ~ What is the usual immediate cause of death in a lightning strike? ~ Cardiac arrest from asystole with respiratory arrest from medullary stunning ~ Extensive burns ~ Hypovolaemia from plasma loss ~ Delayed renal failure ~ A ~ Because the arrest is often primarily respiratory and the heart may restart spontaneously, prolonged ventilation of an apparently dead victim is justified, and this is one of the situations where resuscitation is continued longer than usual.
M ~ Which findings indicate that a burn was inflicted during life rather than after death? ~ A line of redness at the margin, blisters containing protein-rich fluid, and evidence of an inflammatory reaction with repair ~ Blisters containing only air, with no marginal redness ~ Charring of the skin ~ Splitting of the skin over joints ~ A ~ Vital reaction requires circulation, so its presence establishes that the victim was alive, and it is assessed together with soot in the airway and raised carboxyhaemoglobin.
H ~ How is a heat rupture in a charred body distinguished from an antemortem incised wound? ~ A heat rupture has irregular margins, does not extend into deeper viscera along a plane, spares vessels and nerves, and shows no vital reaction ~ A heat rupture bleeds freely ~ A heat rupture has clean-cut margins ~ A heat rupture always involves the abdominal cavity ~ A ~ The distinction matters because heat ruptures over flexures and the burst appearance of the skull can be mistaken for injury, and vital reaction in the surrounding tissue is the deciding feature.
H ~ Which artefact in a burnt body may be mistaken for an antemortem extradural haemorrhage? ~ A heat haematoma, a friable chocolate-brown collection produced by heat-driven exudation from the venous sinuses ~ A subdural haematoma ~ Subarachnoid bleeding ~ A contrecoup contusion ~ A ~ It is typically bilateral or unrelated to any external injury and lies beneath charred bone with heat-driven skull splitting, whereas a true extradural bleed accompanies a fracture and localised scalp injury.
M ~ Which autopsy finding supports death from hypothermia? ~ Wischnewsky spots, multiple small brown-black gastric mucosal erosions, with pink patches of lividity and pancreatic haemorrhage ~ Cherry-pink lividity with soot in the airway ~ Rigor developing unusually early ~ Pugilistic attitude ~ A ~ The pink lividity results from persistence of oxyhaemoglobin in the cold, and paradoxical undressing before death is a behavioural feature that may otherwise raise unwarranted suspicion of assault.
@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Wounds, firearm & mechanical injuries ~ FM_WND ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Mechanical Injuries
E ~ Which type of abrasion retains the pattern of the object that caused it and is therefore most valuable in identifying the weapon? ~ An imprint or pressure abrasion, such as a tyre tread or ligature mark ~ A graze ~ A scratch ~ A brush burn ~ A ~ Graze abrasions show heaped-up epithelium at the far end which indicates the direction of force, so both patterned and grazed abrasions carry information beyond the mere fact of contact.
M ~ A contusion is bluish-black on day 1 and greenish by the end of the first week. Which pigment change accounts for the sequence? ~ Haemoglobin is converted through biliverdin to bilirubin and then to haemosiderin, giving blue, green, yellow and finally brown ~ Melanin is deposited progressively ~ Iron is oxidised to a red pigment ~ Lipofuscin accumulates ~ A ~ The sequence gives only an approximate age because depth, site, age of the victim and treatment all affect it, so the opinion is expressed as a range rather than an exact day.
E ~ Which feature distinguishes an incised wound from a laceration? ~ An incised wound has clean-cut margins with the length greater than the depth and no tissue bridging, whereas a laceration has ragged margins with bridging strands ~ An incised wound has ragged margins ~ A laceration has clean margins ~ Both show tissue bridging ~ A ~ Bridging of vessels and nerves across the wound indicates tearing rather than cutting, which is why it is the single most useful sign at the bedside and at autopsy.
M ~ How does the length of a stab wound on the skin relate to the breadth of the blade? ~ It may be equal to, longer than or shorter than the blade breadth, so it gives only an approximate indication ~ It is always exactly equal to the blade breadth ~ It is always shorter than the blade breadth ~ It bears no relation at all ~ A ~ Movement of the blade or of the victim lengthens the wound while elastic recoil of the skin can shorten it, and the depth may exceed the blade length where the chest or abdominal wall is compressed.
M ~ Which of the following situations satisfies the legal definition of grievous hurt in Indian law? ~ Fracture or dislocation of a bone, permanent privation of the sight of an eye, or any hurt endangering life or causing the sufferer to be in severe bodily pain for 20 days ~ Any wound requiring sutures ~ Any wound causing bleeding ~ Any wound requiring hospital admission ~ A ~ The enumerated categories, now carried into the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, are what the doctor is asked to comment on, and the doctor states the medical facts and lets the court apply the definition.
M ~ Which features distinguish the entrance from the exit wound of a bullet? ~ The entrance is usually smaller with inverted margins and an abrasion collar, while the exit is larger with everted margins and no collar or soot ~ The entrance is larger with everted margins ~ Both are identical in appearance ~ The exit always shows tattooing ~ A ~ These rules can fail when the bullet is deformed, has yawed, or exits through tight clothing which can invert the margins, so the findings are interpreted as a whole rather than by any single sign.
H ~ Which findings indicate a contact shot as opposed to a close or distant discharge? ~ A muzzle imprint with singeing, dense blackening and soot inside the wound track, with cherry-pink discoloration of the tissues ~ Tattooing without blackening ~ Neither soot nor tattooing ~ A wide zone of tattooing only ~ A ~ Blackening appears roughly within about 30 cm and tattooing within about a metre for a handgun, so the sequence of disappearing signs allows the range to be estimated but never the exact distance.
M ~ How does the wound from a rifled weapon differ from that of a shotgun fired at a distance? ~ A rifled weapon gives a single circular wound, whereas a shotgun at a distance gives multiple separate pellet wounds spread over an area ~ A shotgun always gives one large wound at any range ~ A rifled weapon gives multiple wounds ~ Neither can be distinguished ~ A ~ At contact and very close range shotgun pellets travel as a mass and produce a single large ragged wound with a cuff, and the spread of the pellet pattern is used to estimate range.
M ~ Which features suggest that wounds on a forearm are defence wounds rather than self-inflicted? ~ Wounds on the ulnar borders, palms and backs of the hands of varying depth and direction, inflicted while warding off a weapon ~ Multiple parallel superficial cuts of even depth on the accessible flexor forearm ~ A single deep incision over a bony prominence ~ Wounds only over the abdomen ~ A ~ Self-inflicted wounds are typically multiple, parallel, superficial and confined to areas the person can reach with the dominant hand, with tentative hesitation cuts beside the deeper one.
H ~ Which features characterise a fabricated or fictitious wound alleged to have been inflicted by another? ~ Multiple superficial wounds on accessible parts, of uniform depth, sparing sensitive and vital areas, with clothing undamaged and an account inconsistent with the findings ~ A single deep wound over the back ~ Wounds with tissue bridging over the scalp ~ Wounds accompanied by defence injuries ~ A ~ The undamaged clothing over an alleged stab is often the decisive point, and the doctor should record the findings and the inconsistency objectively rather than accusing the person of fabrication.
@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Common poisons (OPC, corrosives, snakebite) ~ FM_POI ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Toxicology
M ~ A young man is confused with dilated pupils, dry flushed skin, retention of urine and a raised temperature after eating seeds mixed in sweets. Which poison is responsible and what is the antidote? ~ Datura, an anticholinergic, treated with physostigmine in severe central toxicity along with supportive care ~ Organophosphate, treated with atropine ~ Opium, treated with naloxone ~ Oleander, treated with digoxin antibody ~ A ~ Datura is the classic road poison used by highway robbers to stupefy travellers, and the picture is the mirror image of organophosphate poisoning, which is why the antidote of one is the toxic syndrome of the other.
M ~ Why is gastric lavage contraindicated after ingestion of a strong corrosive acid? ~ The weakened oesophageal and gastric wall may perforate on passing the tube, and neutralisation generates heat and gas ~ It causes no harm but is ineffective ~ The acid is already absorbed ~ Lavage fluid dilutes the antidote ~ A ~ Management is dilution with water or milk if seen immediately, analgesia, endoscopy to grade the injury and later dilatation for strictures, and an emetic is equally contraindicated for the same reason.
M ~ A woman collapses with vomiting, bradycardia and hyperkalaemia after chewing the seeds of a plant used for suicide in coastal India. Which poison is this and what is the mechanism? ~ Yellow oleander, whose cardiac glycosides inhibit the sodium-potassium ATPase ~ Aconite, which opens sodium channels ~ Nux vomica, which blocks glycine receptors ~ Abrus, which inhibits protein synthesis ~ A ~ The electrocardiogram shows varying degrees of atrioventricular block, treatment is atropine with temporary pacing and correction of potassium, and digoxin-specific antibody fragments are used where available.
M ~ A worker in a battery factory has colicky abdominal pain, wrist drop and a blue line at the gum margin. Which haematological finding supports the diagnosis? ~ Basophilic stippling of red cells with a hypochromic microcytic anaemia and raised free erythrocyte protoporphyrin ~ Macrocytosis with hypersegmented neutrophils ~ Target cells with Howell-Jolly bodies ~ Spherocytes with a positive Coombs test ~ A ~ The metal inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase in haem synthesis, and chelation with calcium disodium edetate, dimercaprol or oral succimer is used according to the blood level and symptoms.
E ~ Why is gastric lavage avoided after kerosene ingestion in a child? ~ Aspiration of the hydrocarbon during lavage or vomiting causes a severe chemical pneumonitis, which is the main danger ~ Kerosene is not absorbed ~ Lavage causes gastric perforation ~ Kerosene is neutralised by gastric acid ~ A ~ Systemic toxicity is minor compared with the pulmonary injury, so management is oxygen, observation and a chest radiograph, and antibiotics and steroids are not given routinely.
M ~ Which finding indicates that antivenom is required after a snakebite rather than observation alone? ~ Evidence of systemic envenomation such as a prolonged 20-minute whole blood clotting time, spontaneous bleeding, ptosis or respiratory weakness, or rapidly progressing local swelling ~ Presence of fang marks alone ~ Any pain at the site ~ A history of a snake being seen ~ A ~ Polyvalent antivenom in India covers the cobra, krait, Russell viper and saw-scaled viper, neostigmine with atropine helps in cobra bites where the block is postsynaptic, and a tourniquet is avoided in favour of immobilisation.
@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Forensic psychiatry, general toxicology & autopsy technique ~ FM_PSY ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Forensic Psychiatry and General Toxicology
E ~ Which term describes a false unshakeable belief that is out of keeping with the person background and cannot be corrected by reasoning? ~ A delusion ~ An illusion ~ A hallucination ~ An obsession ~ A ~ An illusion is a misinterpretation of a real stimulus and a hallucination is a perception without any stimulus, and it is delusion in particular that most often underlies criminal acts by the mentally ill.
E ~ Which disorder is characterised by a recurrent irresistible impulse to steal articles of little value that the person does not need? ~ Kleptomania ~ Pyromania ~ Mythomania ~ Trichotillomania ~ A ~ These impulse control disorders are relevant in court because the act is admitted but the capacity to resist is impaired, which is a question of degree rather than of knowing right from wrong.
M ~ Which features suggest that insanity is being feigned by a person facing trial? ~ Abrupt onset related to the charge, over-acting with exaggerated and inconsistent symptoms, a rare combination of features, and behaviour that stops when the person believes he is unobserved ~ Gradual onset with a family history ~ Symptoms that persist unobserved ~ Consistent symptoms over months ~ A ~ Prolonged observation in a hospital setting, ideally with staff unknown to the person, is the standard method, because a feigned illness is difficult to sustain consistently over time.
M ~ How does the law treat an act committed during an episode of somnambulism? ~ It is treated as an act done without volition, a form of automatism, so criminal responsibility may not attach ~ It is treated as voluntary intoxication ~ It is always punishable ~ It requires a plea of insanity ~ A ~ The claim must be supported by a documented history of sleepwalking and the absence of motive or concealment, because the plea is easy to make and difficult to verify.
M ~ Which provision of the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 allows a person to record in advance how they wish to be treated during a future episode of illness? ~ An advance directive, together with the appointment of a nominated representative ~ A power of attorney only ~ A reception order ~ A voluntary boarder application ~ A ~ The Act also decriminalised attempted suicide and made the right to mental healthcare enforceable, shifting the framework from custody towards capacity and consent.
M ~ Which preservative is used for viscera in a suspected poisoning autopsy, and when must it be changed? ~ Saturated saline for most poisons, replaced by rectified spirit when phosphorus or ethanol is not in question, and no preservative for a blood sample for alcohol other than fluoride ~ Formalin for all viscera ~ Glycerine for all viscera ~ Phenol for all viscera ~ A ~ Formalin is never used because it interferes with the chemical analysis, and rectified spirit is avoided when alcohol or phosphorus is suspected because it would confound the very finding being sought.
M ~ Who may order an exhumation in India, and is there a time limit? ~ A magistrate may order it, and there is no time limit in Indian law ~ Only the police, within one year ~ Only the treating doctor, within 6 months ~ Only a civil court, within 10 years ~ A ~ Identification of the grave, presence of the magistrate and the doctor, and collection of soil samples from above, below and beside the coffin to exclude contamination are the essential steps.
H ~ Which chemical tests were classically used to detect arsenic in viscera? ~ The Reinsch test as a screening test and the Marsh test as a confirmatory test ~ The Gettler test and the Kastle-Meyer test ~ The Benzidine test and the Takayama test ~ The precipitin test and the Barberio test ~ A ~ Modern laboratories use atomic absorption spectrophotometry instead, and hair and nail analysis remains useful because the metal is deposited there and gives an indication of the timing and chronicity of exposure.
@ Forensic Medicine (FMT) ~ Legal procedure, consent, key IPC sections ~ FM_LEG ~ Reddy The Essentials of Forensic Medicine and Toxicology 34e, Ch. Legal Procedure
M ~ What is a hostile witness, and what is the consequence for the doctor giving evidence? ~ A witness who gives evidence contrary to the side that called them, whereupon the party calling them may with the court permission cross-examine their own witness ~ A witness who refuses to attend court ~ A witness who is aggressive towards counsel ~ A witness who is paid by the opposing side ~ A ~ A doctor who has recorded findings carefully and states them without embellishment does not become hostile merely by giving evidence that does not suit the prosecution, which is the practical protection against this situation.
E ~ What is conduct money in the context of a doctor summoned to give evidence? ~ The fee and travelling expenses tendered to a witness in a civil case at the time of service of the summons ~ A fee paid by the patient for the medical report ~ Money deposited with the court by the accused ~ The fee paid to the doctor for performing an autopsy ~ A ~ It is not payable in a criminal case, where the witness must attend regardless, and in a civil case a witness may decline to attend if it has not been tendered.
M ~ What constitutes perjury by a medical witness, and what is its consequence? ~ Wilfully giving false evidence under oath, which is a punishable offence ~ Giving an opinion that the court disagrees with ~ Failing to remember a detail ~ Refusing to answer a hypothetical question ~ A ~ An honest opinion later shown to be wrong is not perjury, so the doctor should confine evidence to what the record supports and say plainly when a question is beyond the available findings.
H ~ In which circumstance is a doctor legally required to inform the police even without the patient consent? ~ Where the case involves a cognizable offence such as an assault, poisoning, sexual offence against a child, or a suspicious or unnatural death ~ In every case of injury however trivial ~ Only when the patient dies ~ Never, because confidentiality is absolute ~ A ~ Treatment of the patient always takes precedence over reporting, and a doctor who refuses emergency treatment while awaiting police formalities is the one who commits the more serious breach of duty.
