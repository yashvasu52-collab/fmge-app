@ Microbiology ~ Arboviruses: dengue, chikungunya, JE, Zika ~ MIC_ARB ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Arboviruses
E ~ Dengue virus belongs to which family and has how many serotypes? ~ Flaviviridae, with four serotypes ~ Togaviridae, with two serotypes ~ Bunyaviridae, with three serotypes ~ Reoviridae, with five serotypes ~ A ~ Infection with one of the four serotypes gives lifelong homotypic immunity but only transient heterotypic protection, which is the basis of severe disease on secondary infection with a different serotype.
E ~ Which test is most appropriate to diagnose dengue on day 2 of fever? ~ NS1 antigen detection or RT-PCR ~ IgM capture ELISA ~ IgG ELISA ~ Haemagglutination inhibition ~ A ~ NS1 antigen and viral RNA are detectable from day 1 to about day 5, whereas IgM appears only after day 4 to 5, so the choice of test depends entirely on the day of illness.
E ~ Chikungunya virus is transmitted by the same vector as dengue but is distinguished clinically by: ~ Severe incapacitating polyarthralgia that may persist for months ~ Plasma leakage with shock ~ Encephalitis in most cases ~ Congenital microcephaly ~ A ~ Chikungunya is an alphavirus of the Togaviridae whose hallmark is prolonged symmetrical arthralgia, whereas thrombocytopenia and plasma leakage characterise dengue.
M ~ The mechanism proposed for severe dengue on secondary infection with a different serotype is: ~ Antibody-dependent enhancement of viral entry into monocytes ~ Direct cytolysis of endothelium ~ Molecular mimicry of platelet antigen ~ Superantigen-mediated T cell activation ~ A ~ Non-neutralising cross-reactive antibody opsonises the new serotype and delivers it into Fc receptor-bearing cells, amplifying viral load and cytokine release, which underlies the plasma leakage of severe dengue.
M ~ Which arbovirus has an amplifying host cycle involving pigs and ardeid birds with Culex tritaeniorhynchus as vector? ~ Japanese encephalitis virus ~ Dengue virus ~ Chikungunya virus ~ West Nile virus ~ A ~ Humans are dead-end hosts in this zoonotic cycle, which is why control focuses on vaccination and on separating pig rearing from human habitation rather than on treating human cases.
M ~ Which feature of Zika virus infection distinguishes it from other arboviral fevers? ~ Vertical transmission causing fetal microcephaly and sexual transmission between adults ~ Haemorrhagic shock in adults ~ Chronic arthritis lasting years ~ Ascending flaccid paralysis in all cases ~ A ~ Zika is neurotropic for fetal neural progenitor cells and is the only arbovirus with established sexual transmission, and it is also associated with Guillain-Barre syndrome in adults.
H ~ Why does the tourniquet test have limited value in the current classification of dengue? ~ It is neither sensitive nor specific, and the revised classification uses warning signs and evidence of plasma leakage ~ It cannot be performed in children ~ It requires laboratory support ~ It is positive only after day 7 ~ A ~ The 2009 WHO revision replaced the older dengue fever and dengue haemorrhagic fever grading with dengue with or without warning signs and severe dengue, because the older haemorrhagic criteria missed severe cases.
H ~ Which serological finding best distinguishes a primary from a secondary dengue infection? ~ A high IgM to IgG ratio in primary and a low ratio in secondary infection ~ Presence of IgM alone in secondary infection ~ Absence of IgG in secondary infection ~ NS1 positivity only in primary infection ~ A ~ Secondary infection produces a rapid, high-titre IgG response from memory cells with relatively less IgM, and the ratio is used epidemiologically because it correlates with the risk of severe disease.
@ Microbiology ~ Bacterial structure, growth, genetics & antimicrobial resistance ~ MIC_STR ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Morphology and Physiology of Bacteria
E ~ Which structure gives Gram-negative bacteria their endotoxin activity? ~ Lipid A of the lipopolysaccharide outer membrane ~ Teichoic acid ~ Peptidoglycan ~ Capsular polysaccharide ~ A ~ Lipid A signals through Toll-like receptor 4 to trigger the cytokine cascade of septic shock, and unlike exotoxin it is heat stable, poorly immunogenic and cannot be converted to a toxoid.
E ~ Bacterial spores resist autoclaving at lower temperatures chiefly because of: ~ Dipicolinic acid with calcium and a dehydrated core ~ A thick capsule ~ A waxy mycolic acid coat ~ An outer membrane ~ A ~ Calcium dipicolinate and the very low water content of the spore core protect proteins from heat denaturation, which is why 121 degrees at 15 pounds for 15 minutes is required and why spore strips are used as biological indicators.
E ~ Which genetic mechanism transfers antibiotic resistance by direct cell-to-cell contact through a conjugation bridge? ~ Conjugation mediated by a sex pilus and a plasmid ~ Transformation of naked DNA ~ Transduction by a bacteriophage ~ Transposition within the genome ~ A ~ Conjugative plasmids carry the tra genes for pilus formation and spread resistance widely and rapidly, including across species, which explains outbreaks of multidrug resistance in Gram-negative organisms.
M ~ Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase production in Escherichia coli confers resistance to which group and is detected by which test? ~ Third-generation cephalosporins, detected by inhibition with clavulanate ~ Carbapenems, detected by the modified Hodge test ~ Aminoglycosides, detected by a synergy test ~ Vancomycin, detected by an agar screen ~ A ~ These plasmid-encoded enzymes hydrolyse oxyimino-cephalosporins but remain inhibited by clavulanic acid, which is the principle of the phenotypic confirmatory test; carbapenems remain the treatment of choice.
M ~ Methicillin resistance in Staphylococcus aureus results from: ~ Acquisition of the mecA gene encoding an altered penicillin-binding protein PBP2a ~ Production of an extended-spectrum beta-lactamase ~ Efflux pump overexpression ~ Porin loss in the outer membrane ~ A ~ PBP2a has low affinity for all beta-lactams, so the whole class fails regardless of beta-lactamase inhibitors, and cefoxitin disc testing or mecA detection identifies it.
M ~ Which growth phase is most relevant when testing the bactericidal activity of a beta-lactam antibiotic? ~ Logarithmic phase, when the cell wall is being actively synthesised ~ Lag phase ~ Stationary phase ~ Decline phase ~ A ~ Beta-lactams inhibit transpeptidation during wall assembly, so they act only on dividing organisms; this is also why they are relatively ineffective against dormant persisters within biofilm.
M ~ Which resistance mechanism explains vancomycin resistance in enterococci? ~ Replacement of the terminal D-alanine with D-lactate in the peptidoglycan precursor ~ Beta-lactamase production ~ Ribosomal methylation ~ Reduced porin permeability ~ A ~ The vanA and vanB gene clusters remodel the precursor so vancomycin can no longer bind, and because the change is enzymatic rather than structural it can be transferred on a plasmid.
H ~ Why is a biofilm on an indwelling catheter so difficult to eradicate with antibiotics alone? ~ The extracellular matrix limits drug penetration and the sessile cells are metabolically dormant with altered gene expression ~ Biofilm bacteria all carry beta-lactamase ~ Antibiotics are chemically destroyed by the polymer ~ The organisms are intracellular ~ A ~ Biofilm cells can be a thousandfold less susceptible than their planktonic counterparts, which is why removal or exchange of the device is usually essential for cure.
@ Microbiology ~ Clostridia & anaerobes (tetanus, gas gangrene, botulism) ~ MIC_CLO ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Clostridium
E ~ Tetanospasmin causes rigidity and spasm by: ~ Blocking release of glycine and GABA from inhibitory spinal interneurons ~ Blocking acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction ~ Increasing acetylcholine release ~ Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase ~ A ~ The toxin is a zinc-dependent protease that cleaves synaptobrevin in inhibitory neurons, so motor neurons fire unopposed; botulinum toxin cleaves the same family of proteins but in cholinergic motor terminals, giving flaccid paralysis.
E ~ Which clinical feature best distinguishes botulism from Guillain-Barre syndrome? ~ Descending symmetrical paralysis with early bulbar and pupillary involvement and normal sensation ~ Ascending paralysis with areflexia ~ Sensory loss in a glove-and-stocking pattern ~ Raised cerebrospinal fluid protein with normal cells ~ A ~ Botulinum toxin blocks all cholinergic transmission, so pupils are dilated and secretions dry, and the paralysis descends from the cranial nerves, unlike the ascending pattern of Guillain-Barre syndrome.
E ~ Which organism most commonly causes gas gangrene and produces an alpha toxin that is a lecithinase? ~ Clostridium perfringens ~ Clostridium tetani ~ Clostridium botulinum ~ Clostridium difficile ~ A ~ The alpha toxin is a phospholipase C that hydrolyses membrane lecithin, causing haemolysis and tissue necrosis, and its activity is demonstrated by the Nagler reaction on egg yolk agar.
M ~ A patient with a deep contaminated wound and no previous immunisation requires which tetanus prophylaxis? ~ Tetanus toxoid plus human tetanus immunoglobulin at separate sites ~ Toxoid alone ~ Immunoglobulin alone ~ Antibiotics alone ~ A ~ Passive immunisation neutralises circulating toxin at once while the toxoid initiates active immunity, and thorough wound debridement remains the most important measure of all.
M ~ Which feature of Clostridioides difficile explains why alcohol hand rub is inadequate for infection control? ~ Its spores are not killed by alcohol, so soap and water washing is required ~ It has a waxy cell wall ~ It is an obligate intracellular organism ~ It produces a capsule ~ A ~ Spore persistence in the environment also explains the need for chlorine-based disinfection of surfaces and for contact isolation of affected patients.
M ~ Which toxin of Clostridioides difficile is principally responsible for the cytotoxic effect used in the cell culture assay? ~ Toxin B, a glucosyltransferase inactivating Rho GTPases ~ Toxin A alone ~ Alpha toxin ~ Binary toxin CDT ~ A ~ Both toxins glucosylate Rho, disrupting the actin cytoskeleton, but toxin B is far more potent in cell culture, and modern diagnosis combines glutamate dehydrogenase screening with toxin detection or PCR.
M ~ Botulism in an infant differs from the adult foodborne form in that it results from: ~ Intestinal colonisation by spores, classically from honey, with in vivo toxin production ~ Ingestion of preformed toxin in canned food ~ Wound contamination ~ Inhalation of aerosolised toxin ~ A ~ The immature infant gut flora permits germination and colonisation, which is why honey is avoided under 1 year, and treatment uses human-derived botulism immune globulin rather than equine antitoxin.
H ~ Why is antitoxin of limited value once tetanus is clinically established? ~ It cannot neutralise toxin already internalised and bound within neurons ~ It causes anaphylaxis in most patients ~ It is inactivated by the wound ~ It only works if given orally ~ A ~ Once the toxin has undergone retrograde axonal transport and cleaved synaptobrevin, recovery requires regeneration of new nerve terminals over weeks, so treatment becomes supportive with sedation, muscle relaxation and ventilation.
@ Microbiology ~ Corynebacterium, Bacillus, Listeria, Nocardia, Actinomyces ~ MIC_COR ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Corynebacterium and Bacillus
E ~ Diphtheria toxin causes cell death by: ~ ADP-ribosylating elongation factor 2 and halting protein synthesis ~ Cleaving synaptobrevin ~ Increasing cyclic AMP ~ Forming membrane pores ~ A ~ The toxin is encoded by a lysogenic beta-phage, so only tox-positive strains are pathogenic, and the Elek gel precipitation test demonstrates toxigenicity.
E ~ Which medium containing potassium tellurite is used for the selective isolation of Corynebacterium diphtheriae? ~ Tinsdale or McLeod medium ~ Lowenstein-Jensen medium ~ Thayer-Martin medium ~ MacConkey agar ~ A ~ Tellurite inhibits most other throat flora and is reduced by the organism to metallic tellurium, producing black colonies, while Loeffler serum slope is used for rapid growth and demonstration of granules.
E ~ Which stain demonstrates the metachromatic granules of Corynebacterium diphtheriae? ~ Albert stain ~ Ziehl-Neelsen stain ~ India ink ~ Giemsa stain ~ A ~ Albert or Neisser staining shows the polymetaphosphate volutin granules, and the organism arranges itself in Chinese letter or cuneiform patterns because of snapping division.
M ~ A pregnant woman develops fever after eating unpasteurised soft cheese, and blood culture grows a Gram-positive rod with tumbling motility at 22 degrees. The organism and the neonatal risk are: ~ Listeria monocytogenes, causing granulomatosis infantiseptica or late-onset meningitis ~ Corynebacterium diphtheriae, causing airway obstruction ~ Bacillus cereus, causing vomiting ~ Nocardia asteroides, causing lung abscess ~ A ~ Listeria grows at refrigeration temperature, shows characteristic tumbling motility and cold enrichment, and crosses the placenta; ampicillin with gentamicin is the treatment of choice because cephalosporins are ineffective.
M ~ Which organism causes an ulcer with a black eschar surrounded by non-pitting oedema after handling animal hides? ~ Bacillus anthracis ~ Bacillus cereus ~ Actinomyces israelii ~ Nocardia brasiliensis ~ A ~ The malignant pustule is painless because the oedema toxin and lethal toxin suppress inflammation, the capsule prevents phagocytosis, and McFadyean stain of a blood film shows the capsulated bacilli.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes Nocardia from Actinomyces on laboratory examination? ~ Nocardia is aerobic and partially acid-fast, Actinomyces is anaerobic and not acid-fast ~ Nocardia is anaerobic and acid-fast ~ Both are strict anaerobes ~ Both are fully acid-fast ~ A ~ The distinction matters therapeutically because nocardiosis is treated with cotrimoxazole for months whereas actinomycosis responds to prolonged high-dose penicillin, and both form branching filaments with sulphur granules or grains.
M ~ Bacillus cereus food poisoning presenting with vomiting within 2 hours of eating reheated rice is caused by: ~ A preformed heat-stable emetic toxin, cereulide ~ A heat-labile enterotoxin raising cyclic AMP ~ Invasion of the intestinal mucosa ~ Endotoxin release ~ A ~ Spores survive boiling and germinate in rice left at room temperature; the emetic syndrome is toxin-mediated and short-lived, whereas the diarrhoeal syndrome from the heat-labile toxin has a longer incubation.
H ~ Why does the Schick test have little role in modern diphtheria control? ~ It measures individual immunity but is superseded by antibody assays and by population coverage monitoring ~ It cannot be interpreted in children ~ It requires toxigenic organisms ~ It causes anaphylaxis ~ A ~ The intradermal test relied on a toxin and a control toxoid, gave frequent hypersensitivity reactions, and has been replaced by serological measurement of antitoxin levels for seroprevalence surveys.
@ Microbiology ~ Gram-negative: E.coli, Salmonella, Vibrio, Pseudomonas ~ MIC_GNE ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Enterobacteriaceae and Vibrio
E ~ Which Escherichia coli pathotype causes bloody diarrhoea without fever and is associated with haemolytic uraemic syndrome? ~ Enterohaemorrhagic E coli O157:H7 producing Shiga-like verotoxin ~ Enterotoxigenic E coli ~ Enteropathogenic E coli ~ Enteroaggregative E coli ~ A ~ Verotoxin inhibits the 60S ribosomal subunit and damages endothelium, so antibiotics are avoided because bacterial lysis releases more toxin and increases the risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome.
E ~ Which selective medium is used for the isolation of Vibrio cholerae from stool? ~ Thiosulphate citrate bile salt sucrose agar ~ Deoxycholate citrate agar ~ Thayer-Martin agar ~ Lowenstein-Jensen medium ~ A ~ The alkaline high-salt medium suits Vibrio and sucrose fermentation gives yellow colonies, while alkaline peptone water serves as the enrichment medium for transport and primary culture.
M ~ Cholera toxin causes secretory diarrhoea by: ~ ADP-ribosylating the Gs alpha subunit, locking adenylate cyclase on and raising cyclic AMP ~ Inhibiting protein synthesis ~ Forming membrane pores ~ Raising cyclic GMP through guanylate cyclase ~ A ~ The persistent rise in cyclic AMP drives chloride secretion through CFTR with obligatory water loss, which is why rehydration alone is life-saving and the heat-labile toxin of E coli acts identically.
M ~ Which serological reaction distinguishes a current typhoid infection from previous vaccination in the Widal test? ~ A rising titre of O agglutinins on paired sera, since H agglutinins persist after vaccination ~ A single high H titre ~ Presence of Vi antibody ~ Absence of O antibody ~ A ~ Somatic O antibody rises early and falls fast, so a fourfold rise is meaningful, whereas flagellar H antibody persists for years after infection or vaccination and is a poor discriminator.
M ~ Which characteristic makes Pseudomonas aeruginosa a particular problem in burns and ventilator care? ~ Minimal nutritional needs with intrinsic multidrug resistance and biofilm formation ~ Spore formation ~ Obligate intracellular growth ~ Acid-fastness ~ A ~ It survives in disinfectants and moist equipment, produces pyocyanin and elastase, and its efflux pumps and porin structure confer resistance, so anti-pseudomonal cover and equipment hygiene are both required.
H ~ Why does the classical Widal test have limited diagnostic value in an endemic area? ~ High background seroprevalence and prior vaccination reduce specificity, and antibody appears only in the second week ~ It requires a live organism ~ It cross-reacts with Brucella only ~ It is positive only after treatment ~ A ~ Blood culture in the first week remains the definitive test, and newer rapid antibody tests still perform poorly, which is why culture capacity is central to typhoid diagnosis.
@ Microbiology ~ Gram-positive: Staph, Strep, toxins ~ MIC_GPS ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Staphylococcus and Streptococcus
E ~ Which staphylococcal enzyme is used to differentiate Staphylococcus aureus from other staphylococci? ~ Coagulase ~ Catalase ~ Oxidase ~ Urease ~ A ~ All staphylococci are catalase positive and oxidase negative, so coagulase, along with mannitol fermentation and DNase, identifies Staphylococcus aureus.
M ~ Toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 causes hypotension and multiorgan failure because it acts as: ~ A superantigen cross-linking MHC class II with the T cell receptor to cause massive cytokine release ~ A protein synthesis inhibitor ~ A membrane pore-forming toxin ~ A protease cleaving complement ~ A ~ Bypassing normal antigen processing activates up to 20 percent of T cells at once, and the same mechanism underlies streptococcal toxic shock from pyrogenic exotoxins.
M ~ Which streptococcal antigen is the basis of Lancefield grouping? ~ The cell wall carbohydrate ~ The M protein ~ The capsular polysaccharide ~ Lipoteichoic acid ~ A ~ Lancefield grouping uses the group-specific cell wall carbohydrate, whereas Griffith typing of group A streptococci uses the M protein, which is also the main virulence factor and the target of type-specific immunity.
H ~ Why does rheumatic fever follow pharyngeal but not skin infection with group A streptococcus, whereas glomerulonephritis follows both? ~ Rheumatogenic M types colonise the throat and cross-react with cardiac myosin, while nephritogenic strains differ ~ Skin strains lack M protein ~ The throat lacks complement ~ Skin infection produces no antibody ~ A ~ Strain-specific tropism and molecular mimicry between M protein and cardiac and neuronal antigens explain the site dependence of rheumatic fever, whereas immune complex deposition in nephritis is not site restricted.
@ Microbiology ~ Immunoglobulins, complement, hypersensitivity ~ MIC_ICH ~ Ananthanarayan and Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology 11e, Ch. Immunoglobulins and Hypersensitivity
E ~ Which immunoglobulin class is the principal antibody of mucosal secretions and exists as a dimer with a secretory component? ~ IgA ~ IgG ~ IgM ~ IgE ~ A ~ The secretory component derives from the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor and protects the dimer from proteolysis in the gut lumen, which is why selective IgA deficiency predisposes to mucosal and respiratory infection.
M ~ Which complement pathway is activated by antigen-antibody complexes containing IgM or IgG and begins with C1q binding? ~ Classical pathway ~ Alternative pathway ~ Lectin pathway ~ Amplification loop ~ A ~ The alternative pathway is triggered by microbial surfaces without antibody, the lectin pathway by mannose-binding lectin recognising sugars, and all three converge on C3 to form the membrane attack complex.
H ~ Which hypersensitivity mechanism underlies the Arthus reaction and serum sickness? ~ Type III immune complex deposition with complement activation and neutrophil influx ~ Type I IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation ~ Type II antibody-mediated cytotoxicity ~ Type IV delayed cell-mediated response ~ A ~ Complexes formed in antibody excess lodge in vessel walls and glomeruli, fixing complement and recruiting neutrophils, which is why the reaction peaks at several hours and shows vasculitis rather than the wheal of type I disease.
