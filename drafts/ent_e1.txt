@ ENT ~ Anatomy & physiology of the ear; audiological tests ~ ENT_AUD ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Anatomy and Physiology of Ear
E ~ Which ossicle is attached to the oval window and transmits vibration to the cochlear fluid? ~ Stapes ~ Malleus ~ Incus ~ Tensor tympani ~ A ~ The ossicular lever with the areal ratio between the tympanic membrane and the oval window gives an impedance transformer effect of about 22 to 1, which is what is lost in ossicular discontinuity.
E ~ Which structure is responsible for transduction of sound into neural impulses? ~ The organ of Corti with its outer and inner hair cells on the basilar membrane ~ The stria vascularis ~ The tectorial membrane alone ~ The round window membrane ~ A ~ Inner hair cells are the true afferent transducers while outer hair cells act as a cochlear amplifier, and it is their function that otoacoustic emission testing measures.
E ~ Which part of the cochlea responds to high-frequency sound? ~ The base, because the basilar membrane is narrow and stiff there ~ The apex, because it is widest ~ The middle turn only ~ The whole cochlea equally ~ A ~ This tonotopic organisation explains why noise and ototoxic damage first affect high frequencies and why the 4 kHz notch is the earliest audiometric sign of noise injury.
M ~ Which test uses a 512 Hz tuning fork to compare air with bone conduction in the same ear? ~ The Rinne test ~ The Weber test ~ The absolute bone conduction test ~ The Schwabach test ~ A ~ A negative Rinne with bone conduction exceeding air conduction indicates conductive loss of at least 20 to 25 dB, and 512 Hz is used because lower frequencies can be felt as vibration and higher ones decay too fast.
M ~ Which audiometric finding characterises otosclerosis? ~ A conductive loss with a dip in bone conduction at 2 kHz, the Carhart notch ~ A high-frequency sensorineural loss ~ A flat sensorineural loss ~ A low-frequency conductive loss with no notch ~ A ~ The notch is an artefact of the loss of the ossicular resonance rather than true cochlear damage, and it disappears after successful stapedotomy.
M ~ Which test measures middle ear compliance and identifies a middle ear effusion? ~ Tympanometry, giving a flat type B curve in effusion ~ Pure tone audiometry ~ Otoacoustic emissions ~ Brainstem evoked response audiometry ~ A ~ A type C curve with negative pressure indicates eustachian dysfunction and a type As curve reduced compliance as in otosclerosis, while type Ad indicates a hypermobile or discontinuous ossicular chain.
M ~ Which test is used for universal newborn hearing screening because it is objective and requires no response from the infant? ~ Otoacoustic emissions, with automated auditory brainstem response for confirmation ~ Pure tone audiometry ~ Play audiometry ~ Speech discrimination testing ~ A ~ Emissions test outer hair cell function so they miss auditory neuropathy, which is why high-risk infants including those in neonatal intensive care are screened with brainstem response.
H ~ Which finding on audiometry suggests a retrocochlear lesion such as a vestibular schwannoma? ~ Asymmetric sensorineural loss with poor speech discrimination out of proportion to the pure tone loss and tone decay ~ Symmetrical high-frequency loss ~ A conductive loss with a notch ~ A flat conductive loss ~ A ~ Absent stapedial reflex decay and abnormal brainstem responses support it, and magnetic resonance imaging of the internal auditory meatus is the definitive investigation.
H ~ Why does a patient with a large conductive loss never have a total hearing loss on audiometry? ~ Bone conduction bypasses the middle ear so hearing cannot fall below the bone conduction threshold, giving a maximum air-bone gap of about 60 dB ~ Air conduction is unaffected ~ The cochlea compensates ~ Masking prevents measurement ~ A ~ Therefore an apparent total loss implies a sensorineural component, and failure to mask the non-test ear produces the shadow curve that can mislead the unwary.
H ~ Why is the eustachian tube more prone to dysfunction in a child than in an adult? ~ It is shorter, wider and more horizontal, so nasopharyngeal secretions reflux more easily and drainage is poorer ~ It is longer and narrower ~ It lacks cartilage ~ It is closed permanently ~ A ~ Together with adenoid hypertrophy this explains the peak incidence of otitis media in early childhood and the improvement as the skull base grows and the tube becomes more vertical.
@ ENT ~ Complications of ear disease & CSF leak ~ ENT_CMP ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Complications of Suppurative Otitis Media
E ~ Which complication of chronic suppurative otitis media presents with a postauricular swelling that pushes the pinna forward? ~ Mastoid abscess, which has broken through the cortex ~ Bezold abscess ~ Luc abscess ~ Citelli abscess ~ A ~ A Bezold abscess tracks into the neck through the mastoid tip, a Citelli abscess into the digastric triangle and a Luc abscess into the temporal region beneath the zygoma.
E ~ Which type of chronic suppurative otitis media is unsafe and requires surgery? ~ The atticoantral type with cholesteatoma and bone erosion ~ The tubotympanic type ~ Serous otitis media ~ Otitis externa ~ A ~ Cholesteatoma expands by enzymatic bone resorption, so it threatens the facial nerve, labyrinth and intracranial contents, which is why mastoidectomy is required rather than medical treatment alone.
E ~ Which sign indicates labyrinthine fistula in a patient with cholesteatoma? ~ A positive fistula test, with vertigo and nystagmus on applying pressure to the external canal ~ A positive Rinne test ~ A negative Weber test ~ Facial weakness ~ A ~ It indicates erosion of the labyrinth, most often the lateral semicircular canal, and it makes urgent surgery necessary because progression causes total sensorineural loss.
M ~ Which intracranial complication of otitis media presents with fever, headache and papilloedema with a picket-fence temperature chart? ~ Lateral sinus thrombophlebitis ~ Otitic hydrocephalus ~ Extradural abscess ~ Meningitis ~ A ~ Griesinger sign of tenderness over the mastoid emissary vein and a positive Tobey-Ayer test support it, and treatment combines antibiotics, mastoid surgery and anticoagulation in selected cases.
M ~ Which complication should be suspected when a patient with chronic ear disease develops persistent headache with a normal neurological examination and raised intracranial pressure? ~ Otitic hydrocephalus, from impaired cerebrospinal fluid absorption after sinus thrombosis ~ Cerebellar abscess ~ Temporal lobe abscess ~ Petrositis ~ A ~ Despite the name there is no ventricular dilatation, and treatment is directed at reducing the pressure to protect vision alongside management of the ear disease.
M ~ Which triad indicates petrositis complicating middle ear disease? ~ Gradenigo triad of persistent otorrhoea, retro-orbital pain from trigeminal involvement and lateral rectus palsy ~ Charcot triad ~ Reynolds triad ~ Hutchinson triad ~ A ~ The abducens nerve is affected in Dorello canal at the petrous apex, so the sixth nerve palsy is the key localising sign in a patient with a discharging ear.
M ~ Which investigation confirms that clear rhinorrhoea after temporal bone trauma is cerebrospinal fluid? ~ Beta-2 transferrin assay of the fluid ~ Glucose oxidase dipstick ~ Protein estimation ~ Culture ~ A ~ Beta-2 transferrin is unique to cerebrospinal fluid, whereas the older glucose test is unreliable because nasal secretions contain glucose and blood contamination confounds it.
H ~ Which finding distinguishes an extradural abscess from a temporal lobe abscess in a patient with cholesteatoma? ~ An extradural abscess often gives only headache and pulsatile discharge, whereas a temporal lobe abscess causes nominal aphasia, contralateral hemianopia and seizures ~ Both cause identical signs ~ Extradural abscess causes aphasia ~ Temporal lobe abscess causes only fever ~ A ~ Cerebellar abscess instead produces ipsilateral ataxia, past-pointing and spontaneous nystagmus, so the neurological pattern localises the collection.
H ~ Why does facial nerve palsy occur more readily in acute otitis media in a child than in an adult? ~ A dehiscent fallopian canal is commoner and the bone is thinner, so infection reaches the nerve directly ~ The nerve is longer in children ~ Children have no fallopian canal ~ The nerve is unmyelinated ~ A ~ In acute otitis media the palsy usually recovers with myringotomy and antibiotics, whereas in chronic disease with cholesteatoma it indicates erosion and needs surgical exploration.
H ~ Why must a cerebrospinal fluid leak after temporal bone fracture be managed with attention to meningitis risk rather than to the leak alone? ~ Most leaks close spontaneously within a week, but the communication carries a continuing risk of ascending meningitis while it persists ~ Leaks never close ~ Meningitis cannot occur ~ Surgery is always immediate ~ A ~ Prophylactic antibiotics are of unproven benefit and may select resistant organisms, so pneumococcal vaccination, avoidance of nose blowing and prompt treatment of any febrile illness are emphasised.
@ ENT ~ External & middle ear disease: otitis externa, otosclerosis ~ ENT_EAR ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Diseases of External and Middle Ear
E ~ Which condition presents with severe ear pain, granulation tissue at the bony-cartilaginous junction and a raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate in an elderly diabetic? ~ Necrotising (malignant) otitis externa, usually due to Pseudomonas aeruginosa ~ Otomycosis ~ Furunculosis ~ Keratosis obturans ~ A ~ It is an osteomyelitis of the skull base requiring prolonged systemic antipseudomonal therapy, and cranial nerve palsy, especially of the facial nerve at the stylomastoid foramen, indicates advanced disease.
E ~ Which organism most often causes otomycosis, and what is its appearance? ~ Aspergillus niger, giving black spores on a wet-newspaper mass of debris ~ Candida albicans, giving white plaques only ~ Pseudomonas, giving green discharge ~ Staphylococcus, giving a furuncle ~ A ~ Meticulous aural toilet is more important than the topical antifungal, and predisposing moisture, instrumentation and topical antibiotic use should be corrected.
E ~ Which surgical procedure treats otosclerosis, and what is its principal risk? ~ Stapedotomy with prosthesis placement, risking sensorineural hearing loss ~ Myringotomy, risking perforation ~ Mastoidectomy, risking facial palsy ~ Tympanoplasty, risking graft failure ~ A ~ Because the only hearing ear must never be operated on, and because a hearing aid is a safe alternative, careful counselling precedes surgery.
M ~ Which finding on otoscopy suggests an active otosclerotic focus? ~ Schwartze sign, a reddish blush over the promontory through the tympanic membrane ~ A retracted drum with fluid ~ A perforation with granulation ~ Tympanosclerosis ~ A ~ It reflects increased vascularity of otospongiotic bone, and the condition characteristically worsens during pregnancy which supports the hormonal influence on its progression.
M ~ Which condition presents with conductive hearing loss and a retracted tympanic membrane with fluid but no pain, commonly in a child? ~ Otitis media with effusion, or glue ear ~ Acute suppurative otitis media ~ Cholesteatoma ~ Otosclerosis ~ A ~ Watchful waiting for 3 months is appropriate because most resolve, and grommet insertion with or without adenoidectomy is considered for persistent bilateral loss affecting speech and learning.
M ~ Which type of tympanic membrane perforation is more dangerous, and why? ~ A marginal or attic perforation, because squamous epithelium can migrate in to form a cholesteatoma ~ A central perforation ~ A subtotal central perforation ~ A traumatic perforation ~ A ~ Central perforations with the annulus intact belong to safe tubotympanic disease, whereas the loss of the annular barrier in a marginal perforation permits keratinising epithelium to enter the middle ear.
M ~ Which condition should be suspected in an adult with unilateral persistent otitis media with effusion? ~ A nasopharyngeal carcinoma obstructing the eustachian tube, requiring nasopharyngeal examination ~ Otosclerosis ~ Cholesteatoma ~ Otomycosis ~ A ~ Missing this diagnosis is a classic and serious error, so every adult with unexplained unilateral effusion requires endoscopy of the nasopharynx and, if suspicious, biopsy.
H ~ Which mechanism explains paracusis Willisii in otosclerosis? ~ Background noise is attenuated by the conductive loss while the speaker raises their voice, so speech becomes relatively clearer in noise ~ The cochlea is stimulated directly ~ Bone conduction improves in noise ~ The stapes becomes mobile in noise ~ A ~ Together with a soft monotonous voice, because the patient hears their own voice loudly by bone conduction, it forms a classic clinical picture.
H ~ Why is a hearing aid a reasonable alternative to surgery in bilateral otosclerosis? ~ The cochlea is normal so amplification is highly effective, and it avoids the small but real risk of a dead ear ~ Surgery never works ~ Hearing aids cure the disease ~ Surgery is contraindicated in all cases ~ A ~ The decision balances the excellent surgical results in experienced hands against a roughly 1 percent risk of profound sensorineural loss, which is why the better-hearing ear is not operated first.
H ~ Why does keratosis obturans differ in management from external canal cholesteatoma? ~ Keratosis obturans is a bilateral accumulation of desquamated keratin in a widened canal without bone erosion, treated by removal, whereas canal cholesteatoma erodes bone and may need surgery ~ Both erode bone equally ~ Keratosis obturans is always unilateral ~ Neither requires treatment ~ A ~ Keratosis obturans is associated with bronchiectasis and sinusitis and causes pain with conductive loss, whereas canal cholesteatoma is usually unilateral in older patients with a dull ache.
@ ENT ~ Larynx: hoarseness, stridor, vocal cord palsy, tracheostomy ~ ENT_LAR ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Diseases of Larynx
E ~ Which nerve injury causes hoarseness with a paramedian cord, and where is it most often damaged? ~ The recurrent laryngeal nerve, most often during thyroid surgery or by a mediastinal lesion on the left ~ The external laryngeal nerve at the upper pole ~ The internal laryngeal nerve ~ The glossopharyngeal nerve ~ A ~ The left nerve has a longer intrathoracic course looping under the aortic arch, which is why aortic aneurysm, left atrial enlargement and bronchial carcinoma cause left cord palsy.
E ~ Which finding indicates the need for urgent tracheostomy rather than intubation in upper airway obstruction? ~ Obstruction at or above the larynx that cannot be bypassed by an endotracheal tube, or prolonged ventilation requirement ~ Any stridor ~ Hoarseness alone ~ A foreign body in the bronchus ~ A ~ Tracheostomy also reduces dead space and eases secretion clearance, and cricothyroidotomy is the emergency alternative when the larynx cannot be intubated.
E ~ Which vocal cord lesion is a bilateral symmetrical swelling at the junction of the anterior and middle thirds in a professional voice user? ~ Vocal nodules, treated primarily with voice therapy ~ Vocal polyp, treated surgically ~ Reinke oedema, treated by stopping smoking ~ Papilloma, treated by excision ~ A ~ The site corresponds to the point of maximum vibratory contact, and nodules in children often resolve with voice rest and speech therapy without surgery.
M ~ Which position does the vocal cord adopt in a complete recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, and why is bilateral palsy dangerous? ~ Paramedian, so bilateral palsy leaves a narrow glottis with stridor requiring airway intervention ~ Fully abducted, causing aphonia only ~ Fully adducted with no airway compromise ~ Midline with normal voice ~ A ~ In unilateral palsy the opposite cord compensates over months so the voice improves, whereas bilateral palsy gives a reasonable voice but a critical airway.
M ~ Which condition presents in a child with inspiratory stridor that worsens when supine and improves with prone positioning, resolving by 2 years? ~ Laryngomalacia ~ Subglottic stenosis ~ Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis ~ Vocal cord palsy ~ A ~ It is the commonest cause of congenital stridor, diagnosed by flexible laryngoscopy showing an omega-shaped epiglottis with indrawing of the supraglottic structures, and severe cases need supraglottoplasty.
M ~ Which malignancy of the larynx presents earliest with hoarseness and carries the best prognosis? ~ Glottic carcinoma, because the cords have sparse lymphatic drainage ~ Supraglottic carcinoma ~ Subglottic carcinoma ~ Postcricoid carcinoma ~ A ~ Supraglottic tumours have rich lymphatics so they present with nodes and a worse outlook, and subglottic tumours present late with stridor.
M ~ Which level of the trachea is used for a standard tracheostomy, and which structure must be avoided? ~ Between the second and fourth tracheal rings, avoiding the first ring to prevent subglottic stenosis and the isthmus of the thyroid ~ Above the first ring ~ Below the sixth ring ~ Through the cricoid cartilage ~ A ~ Cutting the first ring or the cricoid risks permanent subglottic stenosis, and a low tracheostomy risks injury to the great vessels and a tracheo-innominate fistula.
H ~ Why does a patient with a long-standing tracheostomy develop difficulty on decannulation? ~ Tracheal stenosis, granulation at the stoma and loss of the normal respiratory pattern all contribute, so decannulation is staged ~ The trachea heals instantly ~ Stenosis never occurs ~ Only psychological factors are involved ~ A ~ Progressive downsizing, capping trials and endoscopic assessment identify stenosis before the tube is removed, and cuff pressure control during ventilation is the main preventive measure.
H ~ Why is a distinction between stridor and stertor important in a child with noisy breathing? ~ Stridor arises from the larynx or trachea and indicates a narrowed airway needing urgent assessment, whereas stertor arises above the larynx from the nose or pharynx ~ Both arise from the larynx ~ Stertor is always more dangerous ~ The distinction has no clinical value ~ A ~ The phase of the noise localises the level further, with inspiratory stridor indicating supraglottic and expiratory or biphasic stridor indicating tracheal or subglottic narrowing.
@ ENT ~ Nasal polyps, granulomatous disease & tumours of nose and PNS ~ ENT_NAS ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Diseases of Nose and Paranasal Sinuses
E ~ Which feature distinguishes an antrochoanal polyp from ethmoidal polyps? ~ It is usually unilateral, single, arises from the maxillary antrum in a young person and extends into the choana ~ It is bilateral and multiple ~ It arises from the ethmoids ~ It is associated with aspirin sensitivity ~ A ~ Ethmoidal polyps are bilateral, multiple and allergic in origin, and any unilateral polyp in an adult must be regarded with suspicion until histology excludes malignancy.
E ~ Which triad comprises Samter syndrome? ~ Nasal polyposis, asthma and aspirin sensitivity ~ Nasal polyps, deafness and situs inversus ~ Sinusitis, bronchiectasis and infertility ~ Epistaxis, telangiectasia and family history ~ A ~ Cyclooxygenase inhibition diverts arachidonate to the leukotriene pathway, which is why leukotriene receptor antagonists help and why aspirin desensitisation is used in selected patients.
E ~ Which malignancy of the nose and paranasal sinuses is associated with woodworking, and where does it arise? ~ Adenocarcinoma, arising in the ethmoid sinus ~ Squamous carcinoma of the maxilla ~ Adenoid cystic carcinoma of the sphenoid ~ Melanoma of the septum ~ A ~ Squamous carcinoma remains the commonest sinonasal malignancy overall and arises most often in the maxillary antrum, presenting with unilateral obstruction, bleeding, facial swelling or a loose tooth.
M ~ Which vascular tumour of the nasopharynx occurs almost exclusively in adolescent males and must not be biopsied in the clinic? ~ Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma ~ Antrochoanal polyp ~ Inverted papilloma ~ Nasopharyngeal carcinoma ~ A ~ Contrast imaging shows a highly vascular mass widening the sphenopalatine foramen, and preoperative embolisation with surgical excision is the treatment because biopsy can cause torrential haemorrhage.
M ~ Which condition presents with a destructive midline nasal lesion with saddle deformity, positive c-ANCA and renal involvement? ~ Granulomatosis with polyangiitis ~ Rhinoscleroma ~ Sarcoidosis ~ Lethal midline granuloma from NK T cell lymphoma ~ A ~ Antibody testing with biopsy showing necrotising granulomatous vasculitis distinguishes it, and immunosuppression with cyclophosphamide or rituximab has transformed a once-fatal disease.
M ~ Which benign sinonasal tumour has a recognised risk of recurrence and of malignant transformation, requiring complete excision? ~ Inverted papilloma ~ Antrochoanal polyp ~ Rhinophyma ~ Nasal glioma ~ A ~ It arises from the lateral nasal wall, is associated with human papillomavirus and shows endophytic growth into the stroma, and about 5 to 10 percent harbour or develop squamous carcinoma.
M ~ Which chronic granulomatous infection produces a hard nodular deformity of the nose with Mikulicz cells on histology? ~ Rhinoscleroma due to Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis ~ Rhinosporidiosis ~ Tuberculosis ~ Leprosy ~ A ~ It progresses through catarrhal, granulomatous and cicatricial stages, and prolonged treatment with a tetracycline or fluoroquinolone with surgical correction of deformity is required.
H ~ Why is a unilateral nasal polyp in an adult always biopsied before or during removal? ~ It may be an inverted papilloma, a malignancy or an encephalocele rather than an inflammatory polyp ~ All polyps are benign ~ Biopsy is never needed ~ Unilateral polyps are commoner than bilateral ~ A ~ Imaging before any intranasal biopsy is essential because removing an encephalocele or a highly vascular tumour without preparation can be catastrophic.
H ~ Why does invasive fungal sinusitis in a diabetic in ketoacidosis progress so rapidly? ~ Mucorales are angioinvasive and thrive on the free iron and acidic conditions of ketoacidosis, causing thrombosis and tissue necrosis ~ The fungus produces a toxin ~ The immune system is unaffected ~ It spreads only along nerves ~ A ~ Because vascular thrombosis prevents antifungal penetration, urgent surgical debridement with amphotericin B and correction of the acidosis together determine survival.
