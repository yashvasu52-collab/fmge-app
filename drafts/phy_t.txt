@ Physiology ~ Action potential & neuromuscular junction ~ PY_APN ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Membrane Physiology
E ~ Which ionic event is responsible for the rapid upstroke of a nerve action potential? ~ Opening of voltage-gated sodium channels with sodium influx ~ Opening of voltage-gated potassium channels ~ Closure of chloride channels ~ Activation of the sodium-potassium pump ~ A ~ Depolarisation to threshold opens sodium channels in a positive feedback loop, and their inactivation together with delayed potassium opening produces repolarisation.
E ~ The absolute refractory period of a nerve corresponds to which channel state? ~ Sodium channels in the inactivated state, which cannot be reopened until the membrane repolarises ~ Potassium channels closed ~ Calcium channels open ~ Chloride channels inactivated ~ A ~ This state sets the maximum firing frequency and ensures the action potential travels in one direction only, because the segment just traversed cannot be re-excited.
E ~ Which factor increases the conduction velocity of an axon? ~ Myelination with a larger fibre diameter ~ A smaller diameter ~ Reduced internodal distance ~ Loss of nodes of Ranvier ~ A ~ Myelin raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance so current jumps between nodes, and velocity varies with the square root of diameter in unmyelinated fibres and roughly linearly in myelinated ones.
M ~ Which event immediately follows the arrival of an action potential at the motor nerve terminal? ~ Opening of voltage-gated calcium channels with calcium entry triggering vesicle fusion ~ Release of acetylcholinesterase ~ Opening of postsynaptic sodium channels ~ Hydrolysis of acetylcholine ~ A ~ Calcium binding to synaptotagmin drives SNARE-mediated fusion, which is why botulinum toxin cleaving SNARE proteins and hypocalcaemia both impair transmission.
M ~ Which feature of the end-plate potential explains the safety factor of neuromuscular transmission? ~ Each nerve impulse releases far more acetylcholine than the minimum needed to reach threshold ~ The end-plate potential is all-or-none ~ Acetylcholine is not degraded ~ There is one vesicle per impulse ~ A ~ This reserve is why myasthenia gravis becomes clinically apparent only after substantial receptor loss, and why fatigue appears with repeated activity as the reserve is consumed.
M ~ Curare-like non-depolarising blockers produce paralysis by: ~ Competitive antagonism at the postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptor ~ Depolarising the end plate persistently ~ Blocking presynaptic calcium channels ~ Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase ~ A ~ Because the block is competitive it is reversed by raising acetylcholine with neostigmine, whereas the depolarising blocker suxamethonium is made worse by anticholinesterase.
H ~ Why does hyperkalaemia reduce the excitability of a nerve despite bringing the membrane potential closer to threshold? ~ Sustained partial depolarisation inactivates sodium channels, so fewer are available to open ~ It hyperpolarises the membrane ~ It closes potassium channels ~ It raises the threshold potential ~ A ~ The same mechanism underlies the flaccid weakness of severe hyperkalaemia and the cardiac conduction changes, and it explains why depolarising the membrane can paradoxically make it inexcitable.
H ~ Why does local anaesthetic block small unmyelinated pain fibres before large motor fibres? ~ Smaller fibres have a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio and shorter internodal distance, so fewer channels must be blocked ~ Pain fibres lack sodium channels ~ Motor fibres are unmyelinated ~ Large fibres bind the drug less avidly ~ A ~ This differential sensitivity gives the clinically useful sequence of autonomic, then pain and temperature, then touch, and finally motor block during spinal anaesthesia.
@ Physiology ~ Blood: haemopoiesis, haemostasis, blood groups ~ PY_BLD ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Blood Cells and Haemostasis
E ~ Which cytokine is the principal physiological regulator of red cell production, and where is it made? ~ Erythropoietin, produced by peritubular interstitial cells of the renal cortex in response to hypoxia ~ Thrombopoietin, from the liver ~ Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, from marrow stroma ~ Interleukin-3, from lymphocytes ~ A ~ Hypoxia-inducible factor stabilisation drives transcription, which is why chronic kidney disease causes anaemia and why HIF stabilisers are now used to treat it.
E ~ Which sequence correctly describes primary haemostasis after vessel injury? ~ Platelet adhesion via von Willebrand factor, activation and shape change, then aggregation through fibrinogen bridging GpIIb-IIIa ~ Fibrin formation then platelet adhesion ~ Vasodilatation then platelet aggregation ~ Thrombin generation before platelet adhesion ~ A ~ Adhesion uses GpIb binding to von Willebrand factor on exposed collagen, and aggregation uses GpIIb-IIIa, which is why Bernard-Soulier and Glanzmann diseases affect different steps.
E ~ Which clotting factor is common to both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways at the point where they converge? ~ Factor X ~ Factor VII ~ Factor IX ~ Factor XI ~ A ~ Factor Xa with factor Va forms the prothrombinase complex, so the prothrombin time tests the extrinsic and common pathways and the activated partial thromboplastin time tests the intrinsic and common pathways.
M ~ Which anticoagulant mechanism is provided by the intact endothelium itself? ~ Surface heparan sulphate with thrombomodulin, prostacyclin and nitric oxide, plus tissue factor pathway inhibitor ~ Secretion of plasmin directly ~ Release of factor VIII ~ Expression of tissue factor ~ A ~ Thrombomodulin switches thrombin from a procoagulant to an activator of protein C, so a healthy endothelium actively restrains clotting rather than being merely passive.
M ~ A person with blood group O is described as a universal donor of packed red cells because: ~ Their red cells carry neither A nor B antigen so they are not agglutinated by recipient anti-A or anti-B ~ Their plasma contains no antibodies ~ They carry both A and B antigens ~ They lack the Rh antigen ~ A ~ Their plasma does contain anti-A and anti-B, which is why the concept applies to red cells rather than whole blood and why group AB plasma is the universal plasma donor.
M ~ Why is Rh incompatibility a problem in the second and later pregnancies rather than the first? ~ Primary sensitisation produces IgM which does not cross the placenta, while the secondary response produces placenta-crossing IgG ~ The first fetus is always Rh negative ~ The placenta becomes permeable only later ~ Anti-D is naturally occurring ~ A ~ Anti-D immunoglobulin works by preventing that primary sensitisation, which is why it must be given prophylactically and after any potentially sensitising event.
H ~ Why does a deficiency of factor XII prolong the activated partial thromboplastin time without causing bleeding? ~ The contact pathway is not required for physiological haemostasis in vivo, which is initiated by tissue factor and factor VIIa ~ Factor XII is not part of the cascade ~ The test is invalid ~ Factor XII deficiency is always mild ~ A ~ The cell-based model of coagulation replaced the strict waterfall model precisely because it explains this discrepancy and why haemophilia bleeds despite an intact extrinsic pathway.
H ~ Why is fibrinolysis normally confined to the site of a clot? ~ Tissue plasminogen activator is most efficient when bound to fibrin, and circulating plasmin is rapidly inhibited by alpha-2 antiplasmin ~ Plasminogen circulates only in injured vessels ~ Plasmin cannot leave the clot ~ Fibrin activates antiplasmin ~ A ~ This localisation is why systemic thrombolytic therapy, which overwhelms these controls, carries a bleeding risk that endogenous fibrinolysis does not.
@ Physiology ~ Cardiac cycle, ECG basis, BP regulation ~ PY_CCE ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. The Heart
E ~ Which phase of the cardiac cycle accounts for the largest part of ventricular filling? ~ Rapid passive filling immediately after mitral valve opening ~ Atrial systole ~ Isovolumetric relaxation ~ Reduced filling (diastasis) ~ A ~ Atrial contraction contributes only about 20 percent at rest, which is why atrial fibrillation is tolerated at rest but limits filling when tachycardia shortens diastole.
E ~ The first heart sound is produced by: ~ Closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves at the onset of ventricular systole ~ Closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves ~ Opening of the mitral valve ~ Rapid ventricular filling ~ A ~ The second sound comes from semilunar valve closure with physiological splitting on inspiration, and a third sound reflects rapid filling while a fourth reflects atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle.
E ~ Which ECG interval represents atrioventricular conduction time and is prolonged in first degree heart block? ~ PR interval ~ QT interval ~ QRS duration ~ ST segment ~ A ~ The interval includes atrial depolarisation and the nodal delay that allows atrial emptying before ventricular contraction, and it shortens with sympathetic stimulation.
M ~ Which receptor and reflex is responsible for the immediate fall in heart rate when arterial pressure rises? ~ Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch acting through the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves ~ Chemoreceptors in the carotid body ~ Atrial volume receptors ~ Ventricular mechanoreceptors ~ A ~ Increased stretch raises afferent firing, which inhibits the sympathetic and stimulates the vagal outflow, and the reflex resets within days so it buffers acute rather than chronic changes.
M ~ Which mechanism explains the Frank-Starling relationship at the sarcomere level? ~ Increased end-diastolic sarcomere length improves myofilament overlap and raises troponin C calcium sensitivity ~ Increased heart rate ~ Increased sympathetic drive ~ Reduced afterload ~ A ~ Because the relationship is intrinsic to the muscle it operates in the denervated and transplanted heart, and it matches output between the two ventricles beat by beat.
M ~ Which change would shift the pressure-volume loop to indicate increased contractility? ~ A steeper end-systolic pressure-volume relationship with a smaller end-systolic volume at the same afterload ~ A rightward shift of the end-diastolic relationship ~ A larger end-diastolic volume alone ~ A higher afterload ~ A ~ The slope of the end-systolic relationship is a load-independent index of contractility, which is why it is preferred to ejection fraction in physiological studies.
H ~ Why does the QT interval need to be corrected for heart rate? ~ Repolarisation duration shortens as heart rate rises, so an uncorrected value cannot be compared across rates ~ The QT interval is unaffected by rate ~ Correction accounts for electrode placement ~ It adjusts for age ~ A ~ Bazett and Fridericia formulae are used, and a prolonged corrected interval matters because it predicts torsades de pointes, whether congenital or drug-induced.
H ~ Why does severe aortic stenosis eventually cause angina even with normal coronary arteries? ~ Concentric hypertrophy raises oxygen demand while high intraventricular pressure and shortened diastole reduce coronary perfusion ~ The coronary arteries become occluded ~ Oxygen carriage falls ~ Cardiac output rises excessively ~ A ~ Coronary flow occurs mainly in diastole, so the combination of increased muscle mass, raised wall stress and reduced diastolic perfusion time produces demand ischaemia.
@ Physiology ~ Cardiac output, regional circulations & exercise ~ PY_CO ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. Cardiac Output and Circulation
E ~ Which formula expresses the Fick principle for measuring cardiac output? ~ Oxygen consumption divided by the arteriovenous oxygen difference ~ Stroke volume multiplied by heart rate ~ Mean arterial pressure divided by resistance ~ Blood volume divided by circulation time ~ A ~ Both expressions are valid but the Fick method allows measurement without knowing stroke volume, and the arteriovenous difference widens when output falls, which is the basis of its clinical use.
E ~ Which vascular bed shows the greatest capacity for autoregulation of blood flow across a wide pressure range? ~ Cerebral and renal circulations ~ Cutaneous circulation ~ Splanchnic circulation during digestion ~ Pulmonary circulation ~ A ~ Myogenic and metabolic mechanisms keep cerebral flow near constant between mean pressures of about 60 and 160 mmHg, and the range is shifted upward in chronic hypertension.
E ~ During moderate exercise, which change accounts for most of the increase in cardiac output in a trained individual? ~ An increase in stroke volume together with heart rate, with stroke volume contributing relatively more in the trained ~ Heart rate alone ~ A fall in systemic vascular resistance alone ~ Increased blood volume alone ~ A ~ Training increases end-diastolic volume and contractility so resting bradycardia and a larger stroke volume develop, allowing a higher maximal output at any given heart rate.
M ~ Which factor most powerfully increases coronary blood flow during exercise? ~ Local metabolic vasodilatation, chiefly through adenosine and hypoxia ~ Sympathetic alpha stimulation ~ Circulating catecholamines acting on alpha receptors ~ Increased blood viscosity ~ A ~ Because oxygen extraction is already near maximal at rest, increased demand must be met almost entirely by increased flow, which makes coronary reserve the limiting factor in ischaemic heart disease.
M ~ Which unique response distinguishes the pulmonary from the systemic circulation? ~ Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, which diverts blood from poorly ventilated regions ~ Hypoxic vasodilatation ~ Autoregulation across a wide pressure range ~ High vascular resistance ~ A ~ Matching perfusion to ventilation improves gas exchange locally, but when hypoxia is global as at altitude or in chronic lung disease it produces pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale.
M ~ Which change in the cutaneous circulation occurs during heavy exercise in a hot environment, and what is the consequence? ~ Marked vasodilatation for heat loss competes with muscle for cardiac output, so blood pressure may fall ~ Vasoconstriction to preserve pressure ~ No change in skin flow ~ Reduced sweating ~ A ~ This competition explains exercise-induced heat exhaustion and why hydration and acclimatisation, which expand plasma volume and improve sweating efficiency, improve performance and safety.
M ~ Which measurement defines the ejection fraction, and what is its normal value? ~ Stroke volume divided by end-diastolic volume, normally about 55 to 70 percent ~ Stroke volume divided by end-systolic volume ~ Cardiac output divided by body surface area ~ End-diastolic minus end-systolic pressure ~ A ~ Cardiac output divided by body surface area gives the cardiac index, and ejection fraction is load-dependent, which is why it can be normal in heart failure with a stiff ventricle.
H ~ Why does the central venous pressure fall when cardiac contractility increases, and rise when venous return increases? ~ The cardiac function and venous return curves intersect at the operating point, so a change in either shifts the equilibrium ~ Central venous pressure is independent of the heart ~ Contractility raises venous pressure ~ Venous return does not affect pressure ~ A ~ Guyton's analysis of the two curves explains why the same pressure reading can reflect a failing heart or an overfilled circulation, and why the number must be interpreted alongside output.
H ~ Why does prolonged bed rest reduce exercise capacity and cause orthostatic intolerance? ~ Plasma volume falls with reduced stroke volume and blunted baroreflex and muscle pump function ~ Haemoglobin rises ~ Maximal heart rate falls ~ Arterial compliance increases ~ A ~ The same deconditioning occurs in spaceflight, and it is why early mobilisation after illness or surgery is emphasised in recovery protocols.
@ Physiology ~ Cell membrane transport & body fluid compartments ~ PY_CMT ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. The Cell and Body Fluid Compartments
E ~ Which transport process moves sodium out of the cell against its gradient using ATP directly? ~ Primary active transport by the sodium-potassium ATPase ~ Secondary active transport ~ Facilitated diffusion ~ Osmosis ~ A ~ The pump exports three sodium for every two potassium imported, so it is electrogenic and consumes a large share of basal metabolic rate, and its gradient powers most secondary transport.
E ~ Which substance is used to measure total body water, and which to measure extracellular fluid volume? ~ Deuterium oxide or tritiated water for total body water, and inulin or mannitol for extracellular fluid ~ Evans blue for total body water ~ Radioiodinated albumin for total body water ~ Inulin for plasma volume ~ A ~ Intracellular volume cannot be measured directly and is obtained by subtraction, and plasma volume is measured with a marker confined to the vascular space such as labelled albumin.
E ~ Approximately what proportion of body weight is total body water in a young adult male? ~ About 60 percent, of which two-thirds is intracellular ~ About 40 percent ~ About 80 percent ~ About 20 percent ~ A ~ The proportion is higher in the newborn and lower in women and the elderly because of differences in fat content, which is why fluid and drug dosing differ across these groups.
M ~ Infusion of isotonic saline produces which change in body fluid compartments? ~ Expansion of the extracellular compartment with no change in intracellular volume or osmolality ~ Expansion of both compartments equally ~ Contraction of the extracellular compartment ~ Expansion of the intracellular compartment only ~ A ~ Because the infusate is iso-osmotic there is no water shift across cell membranes, whereas 5 percent dextrose distributes through total body water once the glucose is metabolised.
M ~ Which channel type accounts for the high water permeability of the renal collecting duct under vasopressin? ~ Aquaporin-2 inserted into the apical membrane ~ Aquaporin-1 in the descending limb ~ Epithelial sodium channel ~ Urea transporter ~ A ~ Vasopressin acting on V2 receptors raises cyclic AMP which traffics aquaporin-2 vesicles to the apical membrane, and mutations in either the receptor or the channel cause nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.
M ~ Which mechanism explains the resting membrane potential being close to the potassium equilibrium potential? ~ The membrane at rest is far more permeable to potassium than to sodium ~ Sodium permeability exceeds potassium permeability ~ Chloride sets the potential ~ The sodium pump alone generates the potential ~ A ~ The Goldman equation weights each ion by its permeability, so the potential lies near the equilibrium potential of the most permeant ion, with the electrogenic pump contributing a few millivolts.
M ~ Which force in the Starling equation opposes filtration at the capillary and is generated mainly by albumin? ~ Plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure ~ Capillary hydrostatic pressure ~ Interstitial hydrostatic pressure ~ Interstitial oncotic pressure ~ A ~ Hypoalbuminaemia lowers this force and causes oedema, and the revised Starling model recognises the endothelial glycocalyx and lymphatic return as central to steady-state fluid balance.
H ~ Why does hyponatraemia cause cerebral oedema while hypernatraemia causes cell shrinkage? ~ Water moves across the freely permeable cell membrane down the osmotic gradient set by effective solutes ~ Sodium enters cells directly ~ Brain cells lack aquaporins ~ Chloride follows sodium into cells ~ A ~ The brain adapts by extruding or accumulating organic osmolytes, which is why chronic dysnatraemias must be corrected slowly to avoid osmotic demyelination or rebound oedema.
H ~ Why does a rise in plasma glucose in uncontrolled diabetes lower measured sodium without causing hypotonicity? ~ Glucose is an effective osmole that draws water from cells, diluting sodium while total osmolality is maintained or raised ~ Glucose displaces sodium in the assay ~ Sodium is lost in the urine only ~ Glucose enters cells freely ~ A ~ Correcting the measured sodium for glucose reveals the true free water deficit, and this is why translocational hyponatraemia is not treated with hypertonic saline.
@ Physiology ~ GFR, clearance, urine concentration ~ PY_GCU ~ Guyton and Hall Textbook of Medical Physiology 14e, Ch. The Kidney
E ~ Which substance is the ideal marker for measuring glomerular filtration rate, and why? ~ Inulin, because it is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed, secreted nor metabolised ~ Para-aminohippuric acid, because it is completely cleared ~ Glucose, because it is freely filtered ~ Urea, because it is filtered and reabsorbed ~ A ~ Creatinine approximates it clinically but is slightly secreted, and para-aminohippuric acid clearance estimates renal plasma flow rather than filtration.
E ~ Which change in the afferent arteriole would reduce glomerular filtration rate? ~ Afferent arteriolar constriction, which lowers glomerular capillary pressure ~ Afferent arteriolar dilatation ~ Efferent arteriolar constriction ~ A fall in plasma oncotic pressure ~ A ~ Efferent constriction raises glomerular pressure and filtration, which is why angiotensin II preserves filtration in hypovolaemia and why blocking it can precipitate a fall in filtration in renal artery stenosis.
E ~ What is the normal filtration fraction, and how is it calculated? ~ About 20 percent, being glomerular filtration rate divided by renal plasma flow ~ About 50 percent, being filtration divided by cardiac output ~ About 10 percent, being filtration divided by blood flow ~ About 60 percent, being clearance divided by filtration ~ A ~ It rises when efferent tone increases, which raises peritubular oncotic pressure and enhances proximal sodium reabsorption, linking glomerular and tubular function.
M ~ Which mechanism underlies tubuloglomerular feedback? ~ Macula densa cells sense distal sodium chloride delivery and signal to adjust afferent arteriolar tone via adenosine ~ Baroreceptors in the renal artery ~ Direct sympathetic sensing of urine flow ~ Antidiuretic hormone release ~ A ~ High distal delivery causes afferent constriction to reduce filtration, protecting against salt wasting, and loop diuretics interrupt this signal by blocking the sodium-potassium-chloride cotransporter in the macula densa.
M ~ Which segment of the nephron generates the medullary osmotic gradient necessary for concentrating urine? ~ The thick ascending limb, which reabsorbs solute without water, creating countercurrent multiplication ~ The proximal convoluted tubule ~ The collecting duct ~ The descending thin limb ~ A ~ Urea recycling and the vasa recta countercurrent exchanger maintain the gradient, and this is why loop diuretics abolish both concentrating and diluting ability.
M ~ Free water clearance is negative in which situation? ~ When concentrated urine is being produced under the action of vasopressin ~ When dilute urine is produced ~ When urine osmolality equals plasma osmolality ~ During a water diuresis ~ A ~ Negative free water clearance means water is being retained relative to solute, which is the definition of urinary concentration, and calculating it clarifies whether the kidney is contributing to a dysnatraemia.
M ~ Which finding indicates that glucosuria is due to exceeding the tubular transport maximum rather than a tubular defect? ~ Plasma glucose above about 180 mg per dL with normal tubular function ~ Glucosuria with a normal plasma glucose ~ Aminoaciduria with glucosuria ~ Phosphaturia with glucosuria ~ A ~ Glucosuria at a normal plasma glucose indicates a lowered threshold as in renal glycosuria or Fanconi syndrome, where several proximal transport systems fail together.
H ~ Why does creatinine clearance overestimate glomerular filtration rate, and why does the estimated rate become unreliable in acute kidney injury? ~ Creatinine is slightly secreted by the tubule, and in acute injury the serum level lags behind the falling filtration rate so it is not in steady state ~ Creatinine is reabsorbed ~ Creatinine production is variable only in chronic disease ~ Secretion is blocked in acute injury ~ A ~ Estimating equations assume a steady state, so in evolving injury the serum creatinine understates the severity, which is why urine output and biomarkers are used alongside it.
H ~ Why does protein restriction reduce the progression of chronic kidney disease in physiological terms? ~ It lowers single-nephron hyperfiltration and intraglomerular pressure, reducing glomerular injury ~ It reduces creatinine production only ~ It lowers plasma oncotic pressure ~ It reduces renal blood flow to zero ~ A ~ The same haemodynamic logic underlies the renoprotective effect of renin-angiotensin blockade and of SGLT2 inhibition, which restores tubuloglomerular feedback and lowers intraglomerular pressure.
