@ Pediatrics ~ Paediatric emergencies, poisoning & IMNCI ~ PED_EMG ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Paediatric Emergencies
E ~ In paediatric basic life support by two rescuers, the compression to ventilation ratio and compression rate are: ~ 15 to 2 at 100 to 120 compressions per minute ~ 30 to 2 at 100 per minute ~ 3 to 1 at 120 per minute ~ 5 to 1 at 80 per minute ~ A ~ A lone rescuer uses 30 to 2, two healthcare rescuers use 15 to 2 because paediatric arrest is usually hypoxic, and 3 to 1 is the neonatal ratio.
E ~ The correct adrenaline dose for paediatric cardiac arrest is: ~ 0.01 mg per kg of the 1 in 10000 solution intravenously every 3 to 5 minutes ~ 0.1 mg per kg of 1 in 1000 ~ 1 mg regardless of weight ~ 0.5 mg per kg subcutaneously ~ A ~ Using the wrong concentration is a recurrent and dangerous error, and the intramuscular 1 in 1000 preparation at 0.01 mL per kg is used for anaphylaxis rather than for arrest.
E ~ In IMNCI, a child aged 2 months to 5 years with any general danger sign requires: ~ Urgent pre-referral treatment and referral to hospital ~ Home treatment with oral antibiotics ~ Reassessment in 2 days ~ Counselling on feeding only ~ A ~ The general danger signs are inability to drink or breastfeed, vomiting everything, convulsions and lethargy or unconsciousness, and any one of them defines severe illness needing referral.
M ~ A 3-year-old with status epilepticus has had two doses of benzodiazepine without effect. The next step is: ~ A second-line agent such as intravenous levetiracetam, fosphenytoin or valproate ~ A third dose of the same benzodiazepine ~ Intravenous mannitol ~ Immediate thiopentone without airway control ~ A ~ Repeating benzodiazepines beyond two doses adds respiratory depression without improving seizure control, and the ESETT trial showed levetiracetam, fosphenytoin and valproate to be equally effective second-line agents.
M ~ A child ingests an unknown number of iron tablets and has vomiting with haematemesis. The specific antidote and monitoring are: ~ Desferrioxamine with serial serum iron and abdominal radiography for radiopaque tablets ~ N-acetylcysteine ~ Activated charcoal, which binds iron well ~ Sodium bicarbonate ~ A ~ Iron is not adsorbed by activated charcoal, the illness has a characteristic quiescent phase before hepatic and cardiovascular collapse, and chelation is guided by clinical severity and iron levels.
M ~ Which measure is contraindicated after ingestion of a corrosive substance such as an acid or alkali? ~ Gastric lavage or induced emesis, because of the risk of re-exposure and perforation ~ Early endoscopy to grade injury ~ Nil by mouth with analgesia ~ Airway assessment ~ A ~ Neutralising agents are also avoided because the exothermic reaction worsens injury; management is airway protection, analgesia and endoscopic grading to predict stricture formation.
M ~ A child presents with drowsiness, tachypnoea, tinnitus and metabolic acidosis with a respiratory alkalosis after ingesting a large number of tablets. The likely agent is: ~ Salicylate ~ Paracetamol ~ Iron ~ Organophosphate ~ A ~ Salicylate directly stimulates the respiratory centre while uncoupling oxidative phosphorylation, giving the mixed picture, and urinary alkalinisation with dialysis for severe cases is the treatment.
M ~ A 2-year-old is brought after a witnessed kerosene ingestion and is coughing. The management principle is: ~ Do not induce emesis or lavage; observe for chemical pneumonitis with supportive care ~ Immediate gastric lavage ~ Induce vomiting with syrup of ipecac ~ Give milk to coat the stomach ~ A ~ Hydrocarbons have low viscosity and high volatility, so the main hazard is aspiration pneumonitis, and any attempt to empty the stomach greatly increases that risk.
H ~ Which finding in a child with suspected raised intracranial pressure contraindicates immediate lumbar puncture? ~ Focal neurological signs, papilloedema, a Glasgow Coma Scale below 9 or abnormal posturing ~ Fever above 39 degrees ~ Neck stiffness ~ A petechial rash ~ A ~ Withdrawing cerebrospinal fluid in the presence of a pressure gradient risks cerebral herniation, so antibiotics are given first and imaging obtained, with the puncture deferred until it is safe.
@ Pediatrics ~ Paediatric endocrinology: short stature, hypothyroidism, diabetes ~ PED_END ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Endocrine Disorders
E ~ Which feature distinguishes familial short stature from growth hormone deficiency? ~ Normal growth velocity with bone age equal to chronological age and short parents ~ Growth velocity below the third centile ~ Delayed bone age with a normal predicted height ~ Truncal obesity with a doll-like face ~ A ~ In familial short stature the child is short but growing normally along a low centile, whereas pathological causes show a falling growth velocity, which is why serial measurement matters more than a single height.
E ~ Which feature suggests constitutional delay of growth and puberty rather than a pathological cause? ~ Delayed bone age with a family history of late puberty and a normal predicted adult height ~ Bone age advanced beyond chronological age ~ Growth velocity above the 97th centile ~ Raised gonadotropins ~ A ~ Because epiphyses remain open longer, final height is preserved, so reassurance with monitoring is usually sufficient, and a short course of low-dose testosterone may be offered for psychological distress.
E ~ Which is the commonest cause of acquired hypothyroidism in a child in an iodine-sufficient area? ~ Autoimmune (Hashimoto) thyroiditis ~ Iodine deficiency ~ Thyroid dysgenesis ~ Pituitary tumour ~ A ~ Anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies with a firm goitre are typical, and the presentation may be growth failure with delayed bone age rather than the classical adult symptoms.
M ~ A 6-year-old with type 1 diabetes has HbA1c of 9.5 percent and frequent nocturnal hypoglycaemia. The most appropriate change is: ~ Switch to a basal-bolus regimen with an analogue basal insulin and structured education ~ Increase the single daily dose of premixed insulin ~ Add metformin ~ Reduce total daily calories ~ A ~ Basal-bolus or pump therapy with carbohydrate counting gives better control with less nocturnal hypoglycaemia than fixed premixed regimens, and education of the child and family is the largest determinant of outcome.
M ~ Which finding indicates that a child presenting with hyperglycaemia has diabetic ketoacidosis rather than simple hyperglycaemia? ~ Blood glucose above 200 mg per dL with venous pH below 7.3 or bicarbonate below 15 and ketonaemia ~ Glycosuria alone ~ Random glucose above 140 mg per dL ~ Polyuria with a normal blood gas ~ A ~ These three components define ketoacidosis, and children are managed with careful rehydration over 48 hours because of the risk of cerebral oedema, which is the leading cause of death.
M ~ Why is fluid replacement in paediatric diabetic ketoacidosis given more cautiously than in adults? ~ Rapid osmolar shifts increase the risk of cerebral oedema, the commonest cause of death in children ~ Children have less total body water ~ Insulin resistance is greater ~ Renal function is immature ~ A ~ Deficits are replaced evenly over 24 to 48 hours with a modest initial bolus, insulin is started after fluids and bicarbonate is avoided, and any deterioration in consciousness prompts treatment for cerebral oedema.
M ~ A newborn girl has ambiguous genitalia with clitoromegaly, and on day 5 develops vomiting, hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia. The diagnosis and immediate treatment are: ~ Congenital adrenal hyperplasia from 21-hydroxylase deficiency, treated with hydrocortisone, fludrocortisone, saline and glucose ~ Androgen insensitivity syndrome, treated with gonadectomy ~ Turner syndrome, treated with growth hormone ~ Ovotesticular disorder, treated surgically ~ A ~ Impaired cortisol and aldosterone synthesis with accumulation of androgenic precursors gives virilisation with salt-wasting crisis, and 17-hydroxyprogesterone is markedly raised.
M ~ Which finding differentiates precocious puberty of central origin from a peripheral cause? ~ A pubertal rise in luteinising hormone after gonadotropin-releasing hormone stimulation ~ Advanced bone age ~ Raised oestradiol ~ Presence of pubic hair ~ A ~ Central causes activate the axis so gonadotropins rise and respond to stimulation, whereas peripheral sources such as an adrenal or gonadal tumour or McCune-Albright syndrome suppress gonadotropins.
H ~ Which growth pattern suggests hypothyroidism rather than growth hormone deficiency in a child with short stature? ~ Markedly delayed bone age out of proportion to the height deficit with a low growth velocity and normal or increased weight for height ~ Bone age equal to chronological age ~ Proportionate delay in height and bone age with truncal obesity ~ Advanced bone age ~ A ~ Thyroid hormone is essential for epiphyseal maturation, so bone age lags disproportionately, and thyroid function must always be checked before growth hormone testing.
@ Pediatrics ~ Paediatric haematology-oncology: anaemias, thalassaemia, leukaemia ~ PED_HEM ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Haematological and Oncological Disorders
E ~ Which is the commonest cause of anaemia in Indian children, and which index changes earliest? ~ Iron deficiency, with serum ferritin falling before haemoglobin ~ Folate deficiency, with a raised mean corpuscular volume ~ Thalassaemia trait, with a raised HbA2 ~ Vitamin B12 deficiency, with hypersegmented neutrophils ~ A ~ Storage iron is exhausted first, then transport iron falls, and only later does erythropoiesis become iron-restricted enough to lower haemoglobin and cell size.
E ~ A child has severe anaemia from 6 months of age requiring regular transfusion, with hepatosplenomegaly and frontal bossing. Electrophoresis shows almost no HbA with raised HbF. The diagnosis is: ~ Beta-thalassaemia major ~ Sickle cell anaemia ~ Hereditary spherocytosis ~ Iron deficiency anaemia ~ A ~ Symptoms begin as the gamma to beta switch fails, marrow expansion produces the skeletal changes, and management is regular transfusion with iron chelation, with transplantation as the curative option.
E ~ Which chelating agent is given orally and is used in transfusion-dependent thalassaemia? ~ Deferasirox ~ Desferrioxamine ~ Penicillamine ~ Dimercaprol ~ A ~ Desferrioxamine requires prolonged subcutaneous infusion, so oral deferasirox and deferiprone improved adherence, with deferiprone carrying a risk of agranulocytosis needing blood count monitoring.
M ~ Which is the commonest childhood malignancy, and which finding on peripheral smear supports it? ~ Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, with lymphoblasts and cytopenias ~ Wilms tumour, with haematuria ~ Neuroblastoma, with catecholamine metabolites ~ Retinoblastoma, with leucocoria ~ A ~ Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia accounts for about a quarter of childhood cancers, presenting with pallor, fever, bruising, bone pain and lymphadenopathy, and marrow examination with immunophenotyping confirms it.
M ~ Which factor indicates a favourable prognosis in childhood acute lymphoblastic leukaemia? ~ Age between 1 and 10 years with a white cell count below 50000 and hyperdiploidy or the ETV6-RUNX1 fusion ~ Age under 1 year ~ Presenting white cell count above 100000 ~ Presence of the BCR-ABL1 fusion ~ A ~ Risk stratification by age, count, cytogenetics and early response to therapy determines treatment intensity, and infants and those with Philadelphia-positive disease do worst.
M ~ A 3-year-old has a large abdominal mass that does not cross the midline, with haematuria and hypertension. The likely diagnosis and a key management principle are: ~ Wilms tumour, where biopsy is avoided to prevent tumour spillage and upstaging ~ Neuroblastoma, treated by biopsy first ~ Hepatoblastoma, treated by transplantation ~ Lymphoma, treated by radiotherapy ~ A ~ Imaging and staging precede nephrectomy or preoperative chemotherapy according to protocol, and the tumour is associated with WT1 mutations in WAGR and Denys-Drash syndromes.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes neuroblastoma from Wilms tumour on presentation? ~ A mass that crosses the midline with calcification, raised urinary vanillylmandelic acid, periorbital bruising and opsoclonus-myoclonus ~ A smooth mass confined to one flank ~ Haematuria as the presenting feature ~ Hypertension from renin secretion ~ A ~ Neuroblastoma arises from sympathetic tissue so it encases vessels, metastasises to bone and orbit, and MYCN amplification is the key adverse prognostic marker.
M ~ Which prophylaxis is essential when starting induction chemotherapy in a child with a high leucocyte count? ~ Hyperhydration with allopurinol or rasburicase to prevent tumour lysis syndrome ~ Prophylactic platelet transfusion ~ Corticosteroid taper ~ Restriction of fluids ~ A ~ Rapid cell lysis releases potassium, phosphate and urate causing acute kidney injury with hypocalcaemia, so anticipating and preventing it is safer than treating established tumour lysis.
H ~ Why does a child with sickle cell disease require pneumococcal vaccination and penicillin prophylaxis? ~ Repeated splenic infarction causes functional asplenia with impaired clearance of encapsulated organisms ~ Sickled cells are directly bactericidal ~ Penicillin prevents vaso-occlusive crises ~ Vaccination reduces haemolysis ~ A ~ Overwhelming pneumococcal sepsis is a leading cause of early death, so prophylactic penicillin from infancy with conjugate and polysaccharide vaccination, plus parental education about fever, is standard care.
@ Pediatrics ~ Paediatric nephrology: nephrotic syndrome, UTI, AGN ~ PED_NEP ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Renal Disorders
E ~ A 4-year-old has generalised oedema, urine protein 4 plus, serum albumin 1.8 g per dL and cholesterol 320 mg per dL with normal blood pressure and no haematuria. The management is: ~ Start oral prednisolone without a renal biopsy, as minimal change disease is most likely ~ Perform a renal biopsy first in all cases ~ Start an ACE inhibitor alone ~ Give albumin infusion as definitive treatment ~ A ~ Over 90 percent of childhood nephrotic syndrome in this age group is steroid-responsive minimal change disease, so biopsy is reserved for atypical features or steroid resistance.
E ~ Which feature in a child with nephrotic syndrome would prompt renal biopsy before starting steroids? ~ Age under 1 year or over 12 years, hypertension, persistent haematuria, renal impairment or low complement ~ Periorbital oedema on waking ~ Proteinuria of 3 plus ~ Hypercholesterolaemia ~ A ~ These atypical features raise the likelihood of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, membranoproliferative disease or a secondary cause, where the treatment and prognosis differ.
E ~ Which infection is a child with nephrotic syndrome in relapse at particular risk of, and which organism? ~ Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis due to Streptococcus pneumoniae ~ Urinary tract infection due to Proteus ~ Osteomyelitis due to Salmonella ~ Meningitis due to Haemophilus ~ A ~ Loss of immunoglobulin and complement factors in the urine impairs opsonisation, so pneumococcal vaccination and a high index of suspicion for peritonitis in a child with abdominal pain and fever are important.
M ~ A 6-year-old has cola-coloured urine, periorbital puffiness and hypertension 2 weeks after a sore throat, with a low C3. The expected course is: ~ Spontaneous recovery with C3 normalising by about 8 weeks and an excellent long-term prognosis ~ Progression to end-stage renal disease in most children ~ Persistent low C3 beyond 3 months ~ Requirement for immunosuppression ~ A ~ Management is supportive with salt and fluid restriction, diuretics and antihypertensives, and a C3 that stays low beyond 3 months suggests membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis instead.
M ~ Which investigation is indicated after a first febrile urinary tract infection in an infant under 2 years? ~ Ultrasonography of the kidneys and urinary tract, with further imaging guided by findings and recurrence ~ Micturating cystourethrogram in every child ~ DMSA scan in every child at presentation ~ No imaging is needed ~ A ~ Current guidance has moved away from routine invasive imaging, reserving cystourethrography for abnormal ultrasound, atypical organisms, recurrence or a family history of reflux.
M ~ Which method of urine collection is acceptable for diagnosing urinary tract infection in a non-toilet-trained infant when treatment will be started? ~ Suprapubic aspiration or urethral catheterisation ~ Adhesive bag specimen ~ Nappy pad squeeze ~ Perineal swab ~ A ~ Bag and pad specimens have unacceptably high contamination rates, so a positive result from them must be confirmed by a reliable method before committing an infant to treatment and imaging.
M ~ Which finding on a DMSA scan indicates renal scarring rather than acute infection, and when should it be performed? ~ Persistent photopenic defects on a scan performed at least 4 to 6 months after the infection ~ Photopenia during the acute illness ~ Hydronephrosis ~ Vesicoureteric reflux ~ A ~ Acute pyelonephritis itself causes reversible defects, so scanning too early cannot distinguish inflammation from permanent scarring, which is what predicts hypertension and later renal impairment.
M ~ Which complication of steroid therapy must be monitored in a child with frequently relapsing nephrotic syndrome? ~ Growth suppression, cataract, hypertension, obesity and bone demineralisation ~ Hepatotoxicity ~ Nephrotoxicity ~ Ototoxicity ~ A ~ Cumulative steroid toxicity is the reason steroid-sparing agents such as levamisole, mycophenolate, calcineurin inhibitors or rituximab are introduced in frequently relapsing and steroid-dependent disease.
H ~ Why does a child with nephrotic syndrome develop a hypercoagulable state? ~ Urinary loss of antithrombin III with increased hepatic synthesis of fibrinogen and haemoconcentration from diuresis ~ Thrombocytopenia ~ Excess protein C ~ Reduced factor VIII ~ A ~ Renal vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism can occur, so aggressive diuresis and immobility are avoided and any sudden flank pain with worsening proteinuria is investigated urgently.
@ Pediatrics ~ Paediatric neurology: seizures, cerebral palsy, meningitis ~ PED_NEU ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Neurological Disorders
E ~ Which features define a simple febrile seizure? ~ A generalised seizure lasting under 15 minutes, not recurring within 24 hours, in a child of 6 months to 5 years with fever and no neurological deficit ~ A focal seizure lasting 20 minutes ~ Two seizures in 24 hours ~ A seizure in a child of 8 years ~ A ~ Simple febrile seizures carry an excellent prognosis with only a slightly increased risk of later epilepsy, so investigation is directed at the cause of the fever rather than at the brain.
E ~ The parents of a child with a first simple febrile seizure should be advised that: ~ Recurrence occurs in about a third, antipyretics do not prevent recurrence, and long-term antiepileptics are not indicated ~ Daily phenobarbitone should be started ~ Antipyretics reliably prevent recurrence ~ An electroencephalogram is required ~ A ~ First aid, positioning and when to seek help are the practical teaching points, and rescue benzodiazepine is provided only for prolonged or clustering seizures.
E ~ Which type of cerebral palsy is commonest, and which perinatal event is most associated with it? ~ Spastic diplegia, associated with prematurity and periventricular leukomalacia ~ Dyskinetic cerebral palsy, associated with hyperbilirubinaemia ~ Ataxic cerebral palsy, associated with cerebellar malformation ~ Spastic hemiplegia, associated with term asphyxia ~ A ~ Periventricular white matter injury affects the descending motor fibres to the legs, which lie medially, producing lower limb predominant spasticity with relatively preserved cognition.
M ~ Which cerebrospinal fluid pattern indicates tuberculous rather than pyogenic meningitis in a child? ~ Lymphocytic pleocytosis with a very high protein, low glucose and a cobweb clot ~ Neutrophilic pleocytosis with high protein and low glucose ~ Lymphocytosis with normal protein and glucose ~ Normal cell count with high protein ~ A ~ The slow onset with cranial nerve palsies and basal exudate on imaging supports it, molecular testing on an adequate volume of fluid helps, and steroids are added to antitubercular therapy.
M ~ Which organism is the commonest cause of bacterial meningitis in a child over 3 months in India, and what has changed its epidemiology? ~ Streptococcus pneumoniae, with Haemophilus influenzae type b greatly reduced by conjugate vaccination ~ Escherichia coli, reduced by hygiene ~ Listeria monocytogenes, reduced by food safety ~ Group B streptococcus, reduced by intrapartum prophylaxis ~ A ~ Group B streptococcus, Escherichia coli and Listeria dominate in neonates, whereas beyond 3 months pneumococcus and meningococcus predominate, with Haemophilus now uncommon where vaccination coverage is good.
M ~ Which adjunctive treatment reduces hearing loss in childhood bacterial meningitis, and when must it be given? ~ Dexamethasone, started with or just before the first dose of antibiotic ~ Mannitol, after 24 hours ~ Phenytoin, at diagnosis ~ Immunoglobulin, on day 3 ~ A ~ The benefit depends on suppressing the inflammatory burst that follows bacterial lysis, so steroid given after antibiotics have already been running loses its effect; the evidence is strongest for Haemophilus influenzae.
M ~ A 5-year-old has brief episodes of staring with eyelid fluttering many times a day, provoked by hyperventilation, with 3 hertz generalised spike-and-wave on electroencephalogram. The treatment of choice is: ~ Ethosuximide or valproate ~ Carbamazepine ~ Phenytoin ~ Gabapentin ~ A ~ Childhood absence epilepsy responds to ethosuximide, which has fewer adverse effects than valproate, and sodium channel blockers such as carbamazepine and phenytoin characteristically aggravate absence seizures.
M ~ An infant of 6 months has clusters of sudden flexion spasms with developmental regression and hypsarrhythmia on electroencephalogram. First-line treatment is: ~ ACTH or oral corticosteroids, with vigabatrin if tuberous sclerosis is present ~ Carbamazepine ~ Phenytoin ~ Ethosuximide ~ A ~ West syndrome demands prompt treatment because seizure control correlates with developmental outcome, and the skin should be examined under ultraviolet light for hypopigmented macules of tuberous sclerosis.
H ~ Why is a child with cerebral palsy assessed for hip surveillance and scoliosis even when the motor deficit appears stable? ~ Spasticity and muscle imbalance progressively displace the hip and deform the spine as the child grows, and early detection allows preventive intervention ~ Cerebral palsy is progressive at the brain level ~ Bone is intrinsically abnormal ~ Seizures cause skeletal change ~ A ~ The brain lesion is static but its musculoskeletal consequences are not, so serial radiographs guided by the gross motor function level, together with tone management and posture care, prevent painful dislocation.
@ Pediatrics ~ Respiratory distress in the newborn & neonatal seizures ~ PED_RDS ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Neonatal Respiratory Disorders
E ~ A preterm infant of 30 weeks develops grunting, retractions and cyanosis within an hour of birth, with a reticulogranular pattern and air bronchograms on radiograph. The pathophysiology is: ~ Surfactant deficiency causing alveolar collapse with reduced compliance and functional residual capacity ~ Retained lung fluid ~ Meconium obstruction ~ Congenital pneumonia ~ A ~ Surfactant lowers surface tension so its absence causes progressive atelectasis; antenatal steroids and early continuous positive airway pressure with surfactant when required are the mainstays.
E ~ A term infant born by elective caesarean develops mild tachypnoea at 2 hours which resolves by 24 hours, with prominent perihilar streaking and fluid in the fissures. The diagnosis is: ~ Transient tachypnoea of the newborn from delayed clearance of lung fluid ~ Respiratory distress syndrome ~ Meconium aspiration syndrome ~ Congenital diaphragmatic hernia ~ A ~ Labour and vaginal delivery promote fluid clearance through catecholamine-driven sodium channels, so elective caesarean without labour is the classic risk factor, and treatment is supportive.
E ~ Which score is used to grade the severity of respiratory distress in a newborn? ~ Silverman-Andersen or Downes score ~ Apgar score ~ New Ballard score ~ Bhutani nomogram ~ A ~ Grading grunting, retraction, nasal flaring, cyanosis and air entry allows objective monitoring of progression and response, whereas the Apgar assesses transition and Ballard estimates gestation.
M ~ A term infant born through thick meconium-stained fluid is vigorous at birth. The correct approach is: ~ Routine care with observation, as intrapartum suctioning and routine tracheal suction of a vigorous infant are not recommended ~ Immediate intubation and tracheal suction ~ Amnioinfusion ~ Gastric lavage ~ A ~ Evidence showed no benefit from routine suctioning, so resuscitation follows the standard algorithm and tracheal suction is considered only if the airway is obstructed in a non-vigorous infant.
M ~ Which complication should be anticipated in an infant with severe meconium aspiration and persistent hypoxaemia despite high oxygen? ~ Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn with right-to-left shunting ~ Bronchopulmonary dysplasia ~ Pneumothorax only ~ Congenital heart block ~ A ~ Meconium and hypoxia raise pulmonary vascular resistance, and a difference between pre-ductal and post-ductal saturations suggests ductal shunting, treated with oxygenation, inhaled nitric oxide and sometimes extracorporeal support.
M ~ Which is the commonest cause of seizures in the first 3 days of life? ~ Hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy ~ Meningitis ~ Hypocalcaemia ~ Inborn error of metabolism ~ A ~ Metabolic causes such as hypoglycaemia and hypocalcaemia must be excluded immediately because they are rapidly correctable, and hypocalcaemic seizures characteristically appear towards the end of the first week.
M ~ Which feature suggests a neonatal seizure rather than jitteriness? ~ Abnormal eye movements or autonomic change, not stimulus-provoked and not abolished by gentle restraint ~ Provoked by handling ~ Stops when the limb is held ~ Symmetrical fine tremor ~ A ~ Jitteriness is stimulus-sensitive, suppressible and never accompanied by eye deviation or autonomic change, and amplitude-integrated electroencephalography helps where the distinction is uncertain.
M ~ Which antiepileptic remains first line for neonatal seizures? ~ Phenobarbitone ~ Carbamazepine ~ Valproate ~ Topiramate ~ A ~ Despite modest efficacy, phenobarbitone has the best-established pharmacokinetics and safety data in the newborn, with levetiracetam and fosphenytoin as alternatives, and pyridoxine is trialled in refractory cases.
H ~ Why does a neonate with hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy benefit from therapeutic hypothermia only if it is started within 6 hours? ~ Cooling attenuates the secondary energy failure phase, which begins after a latent period of about 6 hours ~ Cooling reverses the primary injury ~ Later cooling causes coagulopathy ~ The brain is insensitive to temperature after 6 hours ~ A ~ The therapeutic window corresponds to the latent phase before delayed excitotoxicity, inflammation and apoptosis, which is why identifying eligible infants promptly matters so much.
