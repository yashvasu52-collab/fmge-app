@ Pharmacology ~ Antivirals, antifungals, antimalarials, anticancer ~ PH_AAA2 ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Antiviral, Antifungal and Anticancer Drugs
E ~ Which antifungal binds ergosterol to form membrane pores and is nephrotoxic? ~ Amphotericin B ~ Fluconazole ~ Caspofungin ~ Griseofulvin ~ A ~ Liposomal formulations reduce nephrotoxicity and infusion reactions at higher cost, and potassium and magnesium wasting with a renal tubular acidosis are characteristic accompaniments.
E ~ Which class of antifungal inhibits beta-1,3-glucan synthesis in the fungal cell wall? ~ Echinocandins such as caspofungin ~ Azoles ~ Polyenes ~ Allylamines ~ A ~ Because human cells have no cell wall the class is well tolerated, and it is fungicidal against Candida but only fungistatic against Aspergillus, with no activity against Cryptococcus.
E ~ Which antiviral is used for herpes simplex encephalitis and requires viral thymidine kinase for activation? ~ Aciclovir ~ Ganciclovir ~ Oseltamivir ~ Ribavirin ~ A ~ Resistance arises from thymidine kinase mutation, in which case foscarnet, which needs no activation, is used, and adequate hydration prevents crystal nephropathy.
M ~ Which mechanism explains the action of oseltamivir, and when must it be started to be effective? ~ Neuraminidase inhibition preventing release of progeny virions, started within 48 hours of symptom onset ~ M2 ion channel blockade ~ Polymerase inhibition ~ Haemagglutinin blockade ~ A ~ Benefit falls sharply with delay because the drug limits spread rather than clearing established infection, and it is most valuable in severe disease and high-risk groups.
M ~ Which anticancer drug causes a dose-related irreversible cardiomyopathy, and which agent reduces the risk? ~ Doxorubicin, with dexrazoxane as an iron-chelating cardioprotectant ~ Cisplatin, with amifostine ~ Bleomycin, with steroids ~ Vincristine, with pyridoxine ~ A ~ Free radical generation by the anthracycline-iron complex is the mechanism, cumulative dose is monitored and left ventricular function is assessed before and during treatment.
M ~ Which anticancer drug causes pulmonary fibrosis and is dose-limited by that toxicity rather than by myelosuppression? ~ Bleomycin ~ Cyclophosphamide ~ Methotrexate ~ 5-fluorouracil ~ A ~ Because bleomycin causes little marrow suppression it is valuable in combination regimens, and oxygen exposure during anaesthesia may aggravate the lung injury, which is relevant to later surgery.
H ~ Why is a uroprotective agent given with high-dose cyclophosphamide, and what is it? ~ Mesna binds acrolein in the urine, preventing haemorrhagic cystitis ~ Amifostine binds platinum ~ Leucovorin rescues folate ~ Dexrazoxane chelates iron ~ A ~ Acrolein is the toxic metabolite concentrated in urine, so hydration with mesna is used, and long-term exposure also raises the risk of bladder carcinoma.
@ Pharmacology ~ Asthma & COPD drugs, antitussives & antihistamines ~ PH_AST ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Drugs for Respiratory Disorders
E ~ Which inhaled agent is the cornerstone of long-term asthma control? ~ Inhaled corticosteroid, which suppresses airway inflammation ~ Short-acting beta-2 agonist ~ Ipratropium ~ Theophylline ~ A ~ Current guidance combines inhaled corticosteroid with formoterol even for as-needed use, because reliever monotherapy with a beta agonist increases exacerbation and mortality risk.
E ~ Which drug is a leukotriene receptor antagonist useful in aspirin-sensitive asthma? ~ Montelukast ~ Zileuton ~ Cromolyn ~ Omalizumab ~ A ~ Zileuton inhibits the 5-lipoxygenase enzyme rather than the receptor, and neuropsychiatric adverse effects have led to a warning on montelukast, so it is used selectively.
E ~ Which antihistamine is preferred for allergic rhinitis in a driver, and why? ~ A second-generation agent such as cetirizine or loratadine, because it penetrates the central nervous system poorly ~ Chlorpheniramine, because it is more potent ~ Promethazine, because it is longer acting ~ Diphenhydramine, because it is cheaper ~ A ~ The older agents also have antimuscarinic effects causing dry mouth, retention and confusion in the elderly, and their sedation is used therapeutically in pruritus and as a hypnotic.
M ~ Which biologic is indicated in severe allergic asthma with a raised IgE and sensitisation to a perennial aeroallergen? ~ Omalizumab, an anti-IgE monoclonal antibody ~ Mepolizumab, an anti-interleukin-5 antibody ~ Dupilumab, an anti-interleukin-4 receptor antibody ~ Infliximab ~ A ~ Eosinophilic phenotypes are targeted with anti-interleukin-5 agents instead, so phenotyping by IgE, eosinophil count and exhaled nitric oxide determines which biologic is appropriate.
M ~ Why does theophylline require plasma level monitoring, and which interactions matter most? ~ It has a narrow therapeutic index and is metabolised by CYP1A2, so ciprofloxacin, erythromycin and smoking cessation raise levels ~ It is renally excreted unchanged ~ It is highly protein bound ~ It has zero-order kinetics at all doses ~ A ~ Toxicity causes nausea, arrhythmia and seizures, and because smoking induces CYP1A2, stopping smoking can precipitate toxicity in a previously stable patient.
M ~ Which mechanism explains the benefit of a long-acting muscarinic antagonist in COPD? ~ Blockade of M3-mediated bronchoconstriction and mucus secretion, reducing exacerbations and improving lung function ~ Direct beta-2 stimulation ~ Anti-inflammatory action on eosinophils ~ Leukotriene receptor blockade ~ A ~ Because vagal tone is the main reversible component of airflow obstruction in COPD, antimuscarinic bronchodilatation is often more effective there than in asthma.
@ Pharmacology ~ Cholinergic & anticholinergic drugs ~ PH_CAD ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Cholinergic System
E ~ Which drug is used to reverse non-depolarising neuromuscular blockade, and what must be co-administered? ~ Neostigmine with glycopyrrolate or atropine to block muscarinic effects ~ Suxamethonium with atropine ~ Physostigmine with glycopyrrolate ~ Edrophonium alone ~ A ~ Glycopyrrolate is preferred because it does not cross the blood-brain barrier, and sugammadex offers an alternative that encapsulates the aminosteroid relaxant directly.
E ~ Which anticholinesterase crosses the blood-brain barrier and is used for central antimuscarinic toxicity? ~ Physostigmine, a tertiary amine ~ Neostigmine ~ Pyridostigmine ~ Edrophonium ~ A ~ The quaternary agents act only peripherally, which is why they treat myasthenia and reverse neuromuscular blockade but cannot reverse the delirium of atropine poisoning.
E ~ Which muscarinic agonist is used to treat xerostomia and dry eyes in Sjogren syndrome? ~ Pilocarpine ~ Bethanechol ~ Carbachol ~ Methacholine ~ A ~ Bethanechol is used for urinary retention and gastric atony, carbachol for glaucoma and methacholine as a bronchial provocation agent, so each agonist has a distinct clinical niche.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes the muscarinic from the nicotinic features of organophosphate poisoning, and which does atropine treat? ~ Atropine treats only the muscarinic features, while fasciculations and weakness require oximes and supportive ventilation ~ Atropine treats both equally ~ Oximes treat only muscarinic features ~ Neither is treatable ~ A ~ Pralidoxime reactivates acetylcholinesterase before ageing of the enzyme occurs, which is why it must be given early, and the atropine end point is drying of secretions rather than pupil size.
M ~ Which antimuscarinic drug is used for motion sickness and is available as a transdermal patch? ~ Hyoscine (scopolamine) ~ Atropine ~ Glycopyrrolate ~ Ipratropium ~ A ~ Its central action on vestibular pathways explains both its efficacy and its sedative and amnestic effects, and glycopyrrolate lacks central action which is why it is not used for this purpose.
M ~ Which effect of a systemic antimuscarinic drug is most dangerous in an elderly man with prostatic hyperplasia? ~ Acute urinary retention ~ Dry mouth ~ Blurred vision ~ Constipation ~ A ~ Precipitation of acute angle-closure glaucoma in a susceptible eye is the other serious hazard, and cumulative anticholinergic burden is associated with cognitive decline in older people.
H ~ Why does suxamethonium cause a transient rise in serum potassium, and in which patients is this dangerous? ~ Depolarisation releases intracellular potassium, which is exaggerated where receptors are upregulated as in burns, denervation or prolonged immobility ~ It inhibits the sodium pump ~ It causes rhabdomyolysis in everyone ~ It blocks renal potassium excretion ~ A ~ In these patients the rise can be several millimoles and cause cardiac arrest, so a non-depolarising agent is used instead after about the first 48 hours from the injury.
@ Pharmacology ~ Clinical trials, pharmacovigilance, prescription writing & drug schedules ~ PH_CTR ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Drug Development and Regulations
E ~ Which phase of clinical development first establishes safety and pharmacokinetics in a small number of healthy volunteers? ~ Phase I ~ Phase II ~ Phase III ~ Phase IV ~ A ~ Phase II explores efficacy and dose in a few hundred patients, phase III confirms efficacy against a comparator in large numbers, and phase IV is post-marketing surveillance.
E ~ Which design feature most protects a randomised trial against observer and reporting bias? ~ Blinding of participants, investigators and outcome assessors ~ Randomisation alone ~ A large sample size ~ Use of a crossover design ~ A ~ Randomisation protects against selection bias and confounding at baseline, whereas blinding protects against differential treatment and assessment after allocation, so the two address different threats.
E ~ Which drug schedule under the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules lists substances requiring a prescription and record keeping because of dependence potential? ~ Schedule H1 ~ Schedule G ~ Schedule M ~ Schedule Y ~ A ~ Schedule M covers good manufacturing practice, Schedule Y governs clinical trial and new drug requirements, and Schedule H1 was introduced specifically to control antibiotics and habit-forming drugs.
M ~ Which analysis preserves the benefit of randomisation by analysing participants in the groups to which they were assigned regardless of adherence? ~ Intention-to-treat analysis ~ Per-protocol analysis ~ As-treated analysis ~ Subgroup analysis ~ A ~ Per-protocol analysis reintroduces selection bias because dropout is rarely random, so intention-to-treat gives the more conservative and clinically relevant estimate of a treatment strategy.
M ~ Which regulatory element is required before a clinical trial may begin in India? ~ Approval by an independent ethics committee and registration on the Clinical Trials Registry India, with regulatory permission ~ Ethics approval alone ~ Registration after completion ~ Sponsor approval alone ~ A ~ Prospective registration prevents selective reporting of favourable trials, and the ethics committee also oversees informed consent, compensation for injury and ongoing safety review.
H ~ Why is a non-inferiority design used instead of a superiority design, and what is its principal risk? ~ It is used when a new drug offers other advantages over an effective comparator, and the risk is that a poorly chosen margin allows an inferior drug to pass ~ It is used when no comparator exists ~ It requires fewer participants always ~ It removes the need for blinding ~ A ~ Because it cannot be placebo-controlled ethically where effective treatment exists, the credibility of the conclusion rests entirely on the pre-specified margin and on assay sensitivity.
@ Pharmacology ~ Diuretics & drugs acting on the kidney ~ PH_DIU ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Diuretics
E ~ Which diuretic acts on the thick ascending limb and is the most potent, retaining efficacy in renal impairment? ~ Furosemide, a loop diuretic ~ Hydrochlorothiazide ~ Spironolactone ~ Acetazolamide ~ A ~ Thiazides lose efficacy once the glomerular filtration rate falls below about 30 mL per minute, whereas loop diuretics continue to work because they reach their luminal site by secretion.
E ~ Which diuretic causes hypercalciuria and which causes hypocalciuria? ~ Loop diuretics cause hypercalciuria while thiazides cause hypocalciuria ~ Both cause hypercalciuria ~ Both cause hypocalciuria ~ Thiazides cause hypercalciuria ~ A ~ This is why thiazides are used to reduce recurrent calcium stones and to treat idiopathic hypercalciuria, while loop diuretics are used to treat hypercalcaemia alongside hydration.
E ~ Which diuretic is used to reduce intraocular pressure and to prevent acute mountain sickness? ~ Acetazolamide ~ Mannitol ~ Furosemide ~ Amiloride ~ A ~ Carbonic anhydrase inhibition reduces aqueous formation and produces a bicarbonate diuresis that offsets the respiratory alkalosis of altitude, and metabolic acidosis with paraesthesia and stones are its adverse effects.
M ~ Which electrolyte disturbance is characteristic of thiazide diuretics and is dose-dependent? ~ Hyponatraemia with hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia ~ Hyperkalaemia ~ Hypercalciuria ~ Metabolic acidosis ~ A ~ Thiazide-induced hyponatraemia is commonest in older thin women and appears within weeks of starting, and thiazides also raise urate, glucose and lipids at higher doses.
M ~ Which diuretic combination produces the greatest natriuresis and why? ~ A loop diuretic with a thiazide, because the thiazide blocks the compensatory distal sodium reabsorption ~ Two loop diuretics ~ Two thiazides ~ A loop diuretic with mannitol ~ A ~ This sequential nephron blockade overcomes diuretic braking but requires close monitoring because profound hypokalaemia and volume depletion can follow.
H ~ Why does spironolactone take several days to reach full effect while amiloride acts within hours? ~ Spironolactone acts through nuclear receptor-mediated changes in protein synthesis while amiloride blocks the sodium channel directly ~ Spironolactone is poorly absorbed ~ Amiloride is a prodrug ~ Spironolactone is renally activated ~ A ~ Both spare potassium, but only spironolactone and eplerenone reduce mortality in heart failure, which reflects the wider effects of mineralocorticoid receptor blockade on fibrosis.
@ Pharmacology ~ GIT drugs: antiemetics, antacids/PPIs, laxatives, antidiarrhoeals ~ PH_GIT ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Drugs for Gastrointestinal Disorders
E ~ Which antiemetic class is most effective for chemotherapy-induced vomiting in the acute phase? ~ 5-HT3 receptor antagonists such as ondansetron ~ Antihistamines ~ Antimuscarinics ~ Dopamine antagonists alone ~ A ~ Delayed emesis is better controlled by an NK1 antagonist such as aprepitant with dexamethasone, so triple therapy is used for highly emetogenic regimens.
E ~ Which laxative acts as an osmotic agent and is also used to treat hepatic encephalopathy? ~ Lactulose ~ Bisacodyl ~ Ispaghula ~ Liquid paraffin ~ A ~ Colonic bacterial fermentation acidifies the lumen and traps ammonia as ammonium, so the same drug serves two purposes through different mechanisms.
E ~ Which antiemetic is contraindicated in a child with gastroenteritis because of extrapyramidal reactions? ~ Metoclopramide ~ Ondansetron ~ Domperidone ~ Promethazine ~ A ~ Children and young adults are particularly susceptible to acute dystonia, and domperidone does not cross the blood-brain barrier appreciably though it carries a QT-prolongation caution.
M ~ Which mechanism explains why a proton pump inhibitor is more effective than an H2 receptor antagonist? ~ Irreversible inhibition of the terminal H+/K+ ATPase blocks acid secretion regardless of the stimulus ~ It blocks histamine, gastrin and acetylcholine receptors ~ It neutralises acid directly ~ It coats the ulcer base ~ A ~ Because the pump is the final common step, and because new pump synthesis takes about 24 hours, once-daily dosing before a meal gives sustained suppression.
M ~ Which long-term concern is associated with prolonged proton pump inhibitor use? ~ Hypomagnesaemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, enteric infection and possibly fracture ~ Hepatic fibrosis ~ Optic neuritis ~ Pulmonary fibrosis ~ A ~ These associations justify reviewing the indication periodically and using the lowest effective dose, rather than avoiding a drug class that prevents ulcer bleeding effectively.
H ~ Why is loperamide preferred to diphenoxylate as an antidiarrhoeal, and why are both avoided in dysentery? ~ Loperamide does not cross the blood-brain barrier so it lacks central opioid effects, and both are avoided because slowing transit prolongs invasive infection and risks toxic dilatation ~ Diphenoxylate is not absorbed ~ Loperamide is an antibiotic ~ Both are safe in dysentery ~ A ~ High-dose loperamide misuse has been associated with fatal cardiac arrhythmia through sodium and potassium channel blockade, which is a recent and clinically important addition.
@ Pharmacology ~ Immunosuppressants, biologicals, vitamins & chelators ~ PH_IMS ~ KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology 8e, Ch. Immunosuppressants and Chelating Agents
E ~ Which immunosuppressant inhibits calcineurin and requires level monitoring because of nephrotoxicity? ~ Ciclosporin or tacrolimus ~ Azathioprine ~ Mycophenolate mofetil ~ Sirolimus ~ A ~ Both cause dose-related nephrotoxicity and hypertension, ciclosporin additionally causes gum hypertrophy and hirsutism, and tacrolimus is more diabetogenic and neurotoxic.
E ~ Which chelating agent is used for lead poisoning in a child with encephalopathy? ~ Calcium disodium edetate with dimercaprol ~ Desferrioxamine ~ Penicillamine alone ~ Deferasirox ~ A ~ Dimercaprol is added because edetate alone can redistribute lead into the brain, and succimer is used orally for less severe poisoning.
E ~ Which agent is used to chelate copper in Wilson disease and can cause a pyridoxine deficiency? ~ Penicillamine ~ Deferiprone ~ Dimercaprol ~ Edetate ~ A ~ Trientine and zinc, which blocks intestinal copper absorption, are alternatives, and penicillamine can also cause nephrotic syndrome, marrow suppression and a myasthenic syndrome.
M ~ Which enzyme deficiency causes severe azathioprine toxicity, and what is the consequence? ~ Thiopurine methyltransferase deficiency, causing profound myelosuppression ~ Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency ~ Pseudocholinesterase deficiency ~ N-acetyltransferase deficiency ~ A ~ Testing activity or genotype before treatment identifies at-risk patients, and allopurinol blocks the alternative xanthine oxidase route so the azathioprine dose must be cut by about three quarters if the two are combined.
M ~ Which biologic mechanism is shared by infliximab, etanercept and adalimumab, and what screening is mandatory? ~ Tumour necrosis factor blockade, requiring screening for latent tuberculosis and hepatitis B ~ Interleukin-6 blockade ~ B cell depletion ~ Integrin blockade ~ A ~ Tumour necrosis factor maintains granuloma integrity so its blockade reactivates tuberculosis, and etanercept is a soluble receptor fusion protein while the other two are monoclonal antibodies.
H ~ Why is sirolimus useful in a transplant recipient with calcineurin inhibitor nephrotoxicity, and what limits its use? ~ It inhibits mTOR without nephrotoxicity, but it impairs wound healing and causes hyperlipidaemia and proteinuria ~ It has no adverse effects ~ It is nephrotoxic as well ~ It cannot be combined with steroids ~ A ~ Because of impaired healing it is usually introduced some weeks after surgery, and it has an antiproliferative effect that reduces the risk of some post-transplant malignancies.
