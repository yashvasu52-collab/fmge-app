@ Medicine ~ Cirrhosis, hepatitis, GI bleed, IBD/PUD ~ MED_CHG ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 337 (Cirrhosis and Its Complications)
E ~ A serum-ascites albumin gradient of 1.4 g/dL in a patient with ascites indicates: ~ Portal hypertension as the cause ~ Peritoneal tuberculosis ~ Peritoneal carcinomatosis ~ Nephrotic syndrome ~ A ~ A gradient of 1.1 g/dL or more reflects the Starling forces of raised sinusoidal pressure, and only portal hypertensive causes produce it regardless of the absolute ascitic protein.
E ~ The first-line drug for prevention of variceal rebleeding after band ligation is: ~ Non-selective beta blocker such as propranolol or carvedilol ~ Atenolol ~ Nifedipine ~ Omeprazole ~ A ~ Non-selective blockade reduces cardiac output and allows unopposed alpha-mediated splanchnic vasoconstriction, both of which lower portal pressure; cardioselective agents lack the splanchnic effect.
M ~ An ascitic fluid neutrophil count of 300 per cubic millimetre in a cirrhotic with fever means: ~ Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis needing immediate cefotaxime ~ Sterile ascites ~ Secondary peritonitis needing laparotomy ~ Tuberculous peritonitis ~ A ~ A count of 250 or more is diagnostic without waiting for culture, and albumin infusion alongside antibiotics reduces hepatorenal syndrome and mortality.
M ~ A patient with alcoholic cirrhosis develops confusion and asterixis after a large gastrointestinal bleed. The mainstay of treatment is: ~ Lactulose with treatment of the precipitating bleed ~ Protein restriction alone ~ Intravenous mannitol ~ Haemodialysis ~ A ~ Blood in the gut is a large nitrogen load; lactulose acidifies the colon to trap ammonia as ammonium, and rifaximin is added when lactulose alone fails.
M ~ Hepatitis B surface antigen positive with e antigen negative, anti-HBe positive, normal transaminases and low viral DNA describes: ~ Inactive carrier state ~ Immune tolerant phase ~ Acute hepatitis B ~ Resolved infection ~ A ~ Seroconversion to anti-HBe with a quiescent liver and low replication defines the inactive carrier, who needs surveillance rather than antiviral therapy.
M ~ Non-caseating granulomas, skip lesions and transmural inflammation on colonoscopy indicate: ~ Crohn's disease ~ Ulcerative colitis ~ Ischaemic colitis ~ Amoebic colitis ~ A ~ Ulcerative colitis is continuous, mucosal and starts at the rectum, whereas Crohn's involves any part of the gut with intervening normal mucosa and full-thickness inflammation that gives fistulae and strictures.
H ~ A cirrhotic with a rising creatinine, urine sodium below 10 mmol/L and no improvement after albumin challenge and diuretic withdrawal has: ~ Hepatorenal syndrome ~ Acute tubular necrosis ~ Prerenal azotaemia ~ Glomerulonephritis ~ A ~ Intense renal vasoconstriction with structurally normal kidneys gives avid sodium retention that volume expansion cannot reverse; terlipressin with albumin is the medical treatment and transplantation the definitive one.
H ~ Recurrent duodenal ulceration with diarrhoea and a fasting gastrin above 1000 pg/mL suggests: ~ Zollinger-Ellison syndrome ~ Helicobacter pylori infection ~ Chronic atrophic gastritis ~ NSAID-induced ulceration ~ A ~ A gastrinoma drives unregulated acid output that also inactivates pancreatic lipase and causes steatorrhoea, and it may be part of MEN type 1.
@ Medicine ~ Coma, raised intracranial pressure & brain death ~ MED_COMA ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 300 (Coma)
E ~ A head-injured man opens his eyes to pain, makes incomprehensible sounds and withdraws from pain. His Glasgow Coma Scale score is: ~ 8 ~ 6 ~ 10 ~ 12 ~ A ~ Eye opening to pain scores 2, incomprehensible sounds 2 and withdrawal from pain 4, giving 8, which is the threshold at which the airway is usually secured.
E ~ Unilateral fixed dilated pupil with contralateral hemiparesis in a head-injured patient indicates: ~ Uncal herniation compressing the third nerve ~ Pontine haemorrhage ~ Metabolic coma ~ Cerebellar infarct ~ A ~ The medial temporal lobe herniates over the tentorial edge, catching the oculomotor nerve and pressing the midbrain, and it is a neurosurgical emergency.
E ~ Pinpoint reactive pupils in a comatose patient most suggest: ~ Opioid poisoning or pontine haemorrhage ~ Atropine poisoning ~ Uncal herniation ~ Brainstem death ~ A ~ Loss of descending sympathetic fibres or excess opioid tone leaves parasympathetic constriction unopposed, and naloxone quickly separates the two possibilities.
M ~ The first-line measure for acutely lowering intracranial pressure in a deteriorating patient is: ~ Head elevation to 30 degrees with hyperosmolar therapy ~ Prophylactic hyperventilation to PaCO2 of 25 mmHg ~ High-dose corticosteroids in trauma ~ Aggressive fluid restriction ~ A ~ Mannitol or hypertonic saline with simple positioning buys time; prolonged deep hyperventilation causes ischaemic vasoconstriction and steroids increase mortality in head injury.
M ~ A patient with an expanding extradural haematoma develops a blood pressure of 190/100 mmHg, a pulse of 45 per minute and irregular breathing. This triad signifies: ~ Imminent brainstem compression from critically raised intracranial pressure ~ Neurogenic shock ~ Septic shock ~ Autonomic dysreflexia ~ A ~ As intracranial pressure approaches mean arterial pressure the sympathetic surge raises systemic pressure to preserve perfusion, and baroreceptor reflex bradycardia follows.
M ~ Before declaring brain death, which of the following must be excluded: ~ Hypothermia, sedative drugs and severe metabolic derangement ~ Presence of spinal reflexes ~ Absent corneal reflex ~ Absent gag reflex ~ A ~ Reversible causes must be excluded because they mimic the clinical picture; spinal reflexes may persist in true brain death and do not invalidate the diagnosis.
M ~ In the apnoea test for brain death the criterion for a positive result is: ~ No respiratory effort with PaCO2 rising above 60 mmHg ~ No effort after two minutes regardless of gases ~ Fall in oxygen saturation ~ Rise in blood pressure ~ A ~ The stimulus to the medullary respiratory centre must be maximal before absence of effort proves the centre is dead, so a documented hypercapnic threshold is essential.
H ~ Sudden coma with quadriplegia but preserved vertical eye movement and consciousness describes: ~ Locked-in syndrome from ventral pontine infarction ~ Persistent vegetative state ~ Akinetic mutism ~ Catatonia ~ A ~ Destruction of the ventral pons interrupts corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts while sparing the reticular formation and the midbrain vertical gaze centres, so the patient is aware but can only blink and look up.
H ~ Coma with a wide anion gap metabolic acidosis, high osmolar gap and calcium oxalate crystalluria indicates: ~ Ethylene glycol poisoning ~ Methanol poisoning ~ Diabetic ketoacidosis ~ Salicylate poisoning ~ A ~ Ethylene glycol is metabolised to glycolic and oxalic acid, and treatment is fomepizole or ethanol with haemodialysis; methanol gives visual loss without oxalate crystals.
@ Medicine ~ Demyelinating disease, spinal cord syndromes & headache ~ MED_DEM ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 444 (Multiple Sclerosis)
E ~ Oligoclonal bands in cerebrospinal fluid with normal serum bands support a diagnosis of: ~ Multiple sclerosis ~ Guillain-Barre syndrome ~ Bacterial meningitis ~ Neurosyphilis only ~ A ~ Intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis produces bands present in CSF but absent from serum, which is found in over 90 percent of established multiple sclerosis.
E ~ A unilateral throbbing headache with photophobia, nausea and a preceding visual scintillation is: ~ Migraine with aura ~ Cluster headache ~ Tension-type headache ~ Trigeminal neuralgia ~ A ~ Cortical spreading depression produces the aura and trigeminovascular activation the pain, and the attack characteristically lasts 4 to 72 hours.
E ~ The abortive treatment of choice for an acute cluster headache attack is: ~ High-flow oxygen with subcutaneous sumatriptan ~ Oral paracetamol ~ Oral amitriptyline ~ Carbamazepine ~ A ~ Attacks peak within minutes so oral drugs are too slow; 100 percent oxygen at 12 litres per minute aborts most attacks and verapamil is used for prophylaxis.
M ~ Ipsilateral loss of proprioception with contralateral loss of pain and temperature below a spinal level describes: ~ Brown-Sequard syndrome ~ Anterior spinal artery syndrome ~ Central cord syndrome ~ Cauda equina syndrome ~ A ~ The dorsal columns ascend uncrossed while the spinothalamic tract decussates within a few segments of entry, so a hemisection dissociates the two modalities.
M ~ Bilateral loss of pain and temperature in a cape distribution with preserved touch suggests: ~ Syringomyelia ~ Multiple sclerosis ~ Subacute combined degeneration ~ Tabes dorsalis ~ A ~ An expanding central canal cavity first interrupts the decussating spinothalamic fibres in the anterior commissure, giving a suspended dissociated sensory loss over the shoulders.
M ~ Optic neuritis with longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis and aquaporin-4 antibodies indicates: ~ Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder ~ Multiple sclerosis ~ Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis ~ Sarcoidosis ~ A ~ Lesions spanning three or more vertebral segments and the antibody distinguish it from multiple sclerosis, and this matters because interferon beta worsens neuromyelitis optica.
M ~ A young obese woman with headache, papilloedema, normal imaging and raised opening pressure has: ~ Idiopathic intracranial hypertension ~ Subarachnoid haemorrhage ~ Meningitis ~ Migraine ~ A ~ Diagnosis rests on the modified Dandy criteria, treatment is weight loss with acetazolamide, and the risk to be prevented is progressive visual field loss.
H ~ Subacute combined degeneration of the cord affects which tracts: ~ Dorsal columns and lateral corticospinal tracts ~ Spinothalamic tracts only ~ Anterior horn cells ~ Spinocerebellar tracts only ~ A ~ Vitamin B12 deficiency impairs myelin maintenance in the posterior and lateral columns, giving loss of vibration and position sense with upgoing plantars but absent ankle jerks.
H ~ The single most useful investigation in a patient with sudden severe headache and a normal CT done 8 hours after onset is: ~ Lumbar puncture for xanthochromia ~ Repeat CT at 48 hours ~ MR angiography ~ Electroencephalogram ~ A ~ CT sensitivity for subarachnoid blood falls with time, and xanthochromia from haemoglobin breakdown appears after about 12 hours and persists for up to two weeks.
@ Medicine ~ Dengue, malaria, enteric fever ~ MED_DME ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 204 (Malaria) and Ch. 199 (Salmonellosis)
E ~ The warning sign that best predicts progression to severe dengue is: ~ Persistent vomiting with abdominal pain and rising haematocrit ~ Fever above 39 degrees ~ Retro-orbital pain ~ Positive tourniquet test ~ A ~ Plasma leakage begins as fever settles, and clinical warning signs plus a rising haematocrit with falling platelets identify the critical phase when fluid therapy must begin.
E ~ The drug of choice for severe falciparum malaria is: ~ Intravenous artesunate ~ Intravenous quinine ~ Oral chloroquine ~ Oral primaquine ~ A ~ Artesunate clears parasitaemia faster and reduced mortality against quinine in the large SEAQUAMAT and AQUAMAT trials, and it avoids quinine-induced hypoglycaemia.
E ~ Radical cure of Plasmodium vivax requires: ~ Primaquine for 14 days after G6PD screening ~ Chloroquine alone ~ Artesunate alone ~ Doxycycline ~ A ~ Only 8-aminoquinolines kill the hepatic hypnozoites that cause relapse, and G6PD status must be known because primaquine causes severe haemolysis in deficient patients.
M ~ Relative bradycardia with a fever of 39 degrees, rose spots and leucopenia in the second week of illness suggests: ~ Enteric fever ~ Dengue ~ Falciparum malaria ~ Leptospirosis ~ A ~ Faget sign, the dissociation between temperature and pulse, is classical, and blood culture in the first week or bone marrow culture later confirms it.
M ~ The most appropriate initial fluid in a patient with dengue shock syndrome is: ~ Isotonic crystalloid bolus with reassessment of haematocrit ~ Platelet transfusion ~ Fresh frozen plasma ~ Hypotonic dextrose ~ A ~ Shock is due to plasma leakage, so isotonic volume is the priority, and prophylactic platelets do not prevent bleeding and may cause harm.
M ~ Chloroquine resistance in falciparum malaria is mediated principally by mutation in: ~ The pfcrt transporter gene ~ Dihydrofolate reductase ~ Cytochrome b ~ Kelch 13 ~ A ~ The mutated transporter pumps chloroquine out of the food vacuole; kelch 13 mutations underlie artemisinin partial resistance and dihydrofolate reductase mutations underlie pyrimethamine resistance.
H ~ Blackwater fever in falciparum malaria is characterised by: ~ Massive intravascular haemolysis with haemoglobinuria and acute kidney injury ~ Splenic rupture ~ Cerebral oedema ~ Pulmonary haemorrhage ~ A ~ Sudden severe haemolysis stains the urine dark and precipitates renal failure; it has been associated with quinine exposure and high parasitaemia.
H ~ The commonest cause of relapse of enteric fever after apparently adequate therapy is: ~ Persistence of organisms in the gallbladder and mononuclear phagocyte system ~ Antibiotic resistance alone ~ Reinfection from water ~ Immunodeficiency ~ A ~ Salmonella survives intracellularly and in biliary sludge, which is also why chronic carriers need prolonged therapy and occasionally cholecystectomy.
@ Medicine ~ Diabetes mellitus: diagnosis, complications, mgmt ~ MED_DMD ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 403 (Diabetes Mellitus)
E ~ The diagnostic fasting plasma glucose threshold for diabetes mellitus is: ~ 126 mg/dL or more on two occasions ~ 110 mg/dL ~ 140 mg/dL ~ 100 mg/dL ~ A ~ The cut-off was set where retinopathy risk rises steeply, and the same reasoning gives a 2-hour value of 200 mg/dL and an HbA1c of 6.5 percent.
E ~ The first-line oral agent in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes without contraindication is: ~ Metformin ~ Glibenclamide ~ Pioglitazone ~ Acarbose ~ A ~ It reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis without causing hypoglycaemia or weight gain and has the best long-term cardiovascular evidence of the older agents.
E ~ Annual screening for diabetic nephropathy is best done by: ~ Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio ~ Serum creatinine alone ~ Urine dipstick for protein ~ Renal ultrasound ~ A ~ Moderately increased albuminuria precedes dipstick-detectable proteinuria by years and is the point at which renin-angiotensin blockade changes the natural history.
M ~ The resolution criterion for diabetic ketoacidosis is: ~ Anion gap closure with bicarbonate above 15 and pH above 7.3 ~ Blood glucose below 200 alone ~ Absence of urinary ketones ~ Normal serum sodium ~ A ~ Urine ketone tests detect acetoacetate and can stay positive as beta-hydroxybutyrate is converted, so the anion gap is the reliable endpoint; dextrose is added once glucose falls while insulin continues.
M ~ Potassium must be checked before starting insulin in diabetic ketoacidosis because: ~ Insulin drives potassium intracellularly and can cause fatal hypokalaemia ~ Insulin raises serum potassium ~ Potassium is always high ~ Potassium does not change ~ A ~ Total body potassium is depleted despite a normal or high serum level from acidosis, so replacement starts when potassium is below 5.3 and insulin is withheld below 3.3 mmol/L.
M ~ An SGLT2 inhibitor is particularly indicated in a type 2 diabetic who also has: ~ Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction or chronic kidney disease ~ Recurrent genital infection ~ Type 1 diabetes ~ Severe hepatic failure ~ A ~ Trials such as EMPA-REG, DAPA-HF and CREDENCE showed reductions in heart failure hospitalisation and renal progression independent of glucose lowering.
M ~ Painless loss of vision with neovascularisation on fundoscopy in a diabetic requires: ~ Pan-retinal photocoagulation or anti-VEGF therapy ~ Tighter glycaemic control alone ~ Observation ~ Topical steroids ~ A ~ Proliferative retinopathy threatens vitreous haemorrhage and tractional detachment, and laser ablation of ischaemic retina removes the drive to new vessel formation.
H ~ A diabetic with painless foot ulceration over the metatarsal head, palpable pulses and absent ankle reflexes has ulceration primarily due to: ~ Peripheral sensory neuropathy with abnormal pressure loading ~ Critical limb ischaemia ~ Venous insufficiency ~ Vasculitis ~ A ~ Loss of protective sensation allows repeated unnoticed trauma at pressure points, and management centres on offloading, debridement and infection control rather than revascularisation.
H ~ Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state differs from diabetic ketoacidosis mainly because: ~ Residual insulin suppresses ketogenesis despite severe hyperglycaemia ~ Insulin is completely absent ~ Glucagon is suppressed ~ It occurs only in type 1 diabetes ~ A ~ Enough insulin remains to block lipolysis but not gluconeogenesis, so glucose climbs above 600 mg/dL with profound dehydration and osmolality above 320 but minimal acidosis.
@ Medicine ~ Geriatrics, palliative care & end-of-life issues ~ MED_GER ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 471 (Palliative and End-of-Life Care)
E ~ The World Health Organization analgesic ladder recommends that a patient with severe cancer pain be started on: ~ A strong opioid such as morphine ~ Paracetamol alone ~ A weak opioid only ~ An antidepressant ~ A ~ Severity, not sequence, determines the step chosen, so severe pain begins at step three with a strong opioid plus adjuvants rather than climbing from the bottom.
E ~ The commonest adverse effect of chronic opioid therapy that does not develop tolerance is: ~ Constipation ~ Nausea ~ Sedation ~ Respiratory depression ~ A ~ Tolerance develops to most opioid effects but not to reduced gut motility, so a stimulant laxative is prescribed prophylactically with every strong opioid.
E ~ A validated first-line screening tool for cognitive impairment in an elderly outpatient is: ~ Mini-Mental State Examination or Montreal Cognitive Assessment ~ Glasgow Coma Scale ~ Barthel index ~ Geriatric depression scale ~ A ~ These test multiple cognitive domains, whereas the Barthel index measures function and the depression scale screens mood, both of which are complementary rather than diagnostic of cognitive loss.
M ~ Polypharmacy in the elderly is best addressed using: ~ Explicit criteria such as the Beers or STOPP-START lists ~ Stopping all drugs at once ~ Halving every dose ~ Adding a second specialist ~ A ~ These lists identify drugs whose risk exceeds benefit in older people, such as long-acting benzodiazepines and anticholinergics, and also flag omitted beneficial therapy.
M ~ The commonest cause of acute confusion in a hospitalised elderly patient is: ~ Delirium from an acute medical precipitant ~ Alzheimer disease ~ Depression ~ Schizophrenia ~ A ~ Delirium has an acute onset with fluctuating attention and is usually multifactorial from infection, drugs, pain or retention, so the response is to find and treat the precipitant.
M ~ Falls risk in an older person is most reduced by: ~ Multifactorial assessment with strength and balance training ~ Bed rest ~ Routine hip protectors alone ~ Sedative prescription ~ A ~ Exercise programmes plus medication review, vision correction and home hazard modification have the strongest trial evidence, whereas immobility accelerates deconditioning.
M ~ In the last days of life, excessive respiratory secretions are best managed with: ~ Antimuscarinic agents such as glycopyrrolate with repositioning ~ Deep suctioning ~ Intravenous furosemide ~ Broad-spectrum antibiotics ~ A ~ The sound distresses relatives more than the patient; drying secretions and turning the patient are effective, while suction is traumatic and rarely helps.
H ~ Which principle allows giving an opioid dose that relieves pain even if it may hasten death: ~ The doctrine of double effect ~ Euthanasia ~ Physician-assisted suicide ~ Therapeutic privilege ~ A ~ The intended effect is relief of suffering and the harmful effect is foreseen but unintended and proportionate, which is ethically and legally distinct from acting in order to cause death.
H ~ Frailty in the elderly is best characterised as: ~ A multisystem loss of physiological reserve with reduced resilience to stressors ~ Simply the presence of many diseases ~ Chronological age above 80 ~ Cognitive impairment ~ A ~ It is measured with phenotypic criteria such as weight loss, exhaustion, slow gait, weakness and low activity, and it predicts outcomes better than age or diagnosis lists.
