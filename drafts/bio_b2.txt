@ Biochemistry ~ Haem synthesis, porphyrias & bilirubin metabolism ~ BC_HAE ~ Vasudevan Textbook of Biochemistry for Medical Students 9e, Ch. Haemoglobin and Porphyrins
M ~ A man with hepatitis C and iron overload has blistering and fragile skin on the backs of his hands with hypertrichosis, and pink fluorescent urine. Which enzyme is deficient? ~ Ferrochelatase ~ Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase, giving porphyria cutanea tarda ~ ALA dehydratase ~ Porphobilinogen deaminase ~ B ~ It is the commonest porphyria, is usually acquired with hepatitis C, alcohol or iron as triggers, and responds to venesection or low-dose hydroxychloroquine rather than to the haem infusion used for acute attacks.
H ~ A child develops immediate burning and swelling of sun-exposed skin without blistering, and red cell protoporphyrin is markedly raised. Which enzyme is deficient? ~ Ferrochelatase, giving erythropoietic protoporphyria ~ Uroporphyrinogen III synthase ~ Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase ~ ALA synthase ~ A ~ The terminal step inserting ferrous iron into protoporphyrin IX fails, so the accumulated photoreactive porphyrin causes acute painful photosensitivity, and liver disease from protoporphyrin deposition is the serious complication.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes Crigler-Najjar type II from type I? ~ Type II has no enzyme activity at all ~ Type II retains partial glucuronyl transferase activity and responds to phenobarbitone ~ Type II presents with conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia ~ Type II is acquired rather than inherited ~ B ~ Because the enzyme is inducible in type II the bilirubin falls with an enzyme inducer, whereas type I has absent activity, does not respond, and requires phototherapy and ultimately liver transplantation to prevent kernicterus.
E ~ A young man has mild fluctuating unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia that rises with fasting or illness, with normal liver enzymes and no haemolysis. What is the diagnosis? ~ Gilbert syndrome ~ Dubin-Johnson syndrome ~ Rotor syndrome ~ Crigler-Najjar type I ~ A ~ Reduced glucuronyl transferase activity of about 30 percent explains it, the condition is benign and needs no treatment, and its main importance is avoiding unnecessary investigation and predicting irinotecan toxicity.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes Rotor syndrome from Dubin-Johnson syndrome? ~ Rotor syndrome shows unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia ~ In Rotor syndrome the liver is not pigmented and gallbladder visualisation on cholecystography is normal ~ Rotor syndrome is associated with a black liver ~ Rotor syndrome causes progressive cirrhosis ~ B ~ Both cause benign conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia from defective canalicular excretion, but the dense melanin-like pigment and the non-visualised gallbladder belong to Dubin-Johnson syndrome.
H ~ Which statement correctly describes the cellular compartments used in haem synthesis? ~ The whole pathway occurs in the cytosol ~ The whole pathway occurs in mitochondria ~ The first and the last three steps occur in mitochondria while the intermediate steps occur in the cytosol ~ Only the final step occurs in the cytosol ~ C ~ This shuttling means the pathway needs succinyl-CoA from the citric acid cycle to begin, and mitochondrial disease or lead, which inhibits both cytosolic ALA dehydratase and mitochondrial ferrochelatase, disrupts it at more than one point.
M ~ Why does unconjugated rather than conjugated bilirubin cause kernicterus in a neonate? ~ Unconjugated bilirubin is lipid soluble and, when it exceeds albumin binding capacity, crosses the immature blood-brain barrier ~ Conjugated bilirubin is more lipid soluble ~ Unconjugated bilirubin is actively transported into neurons ~ Conjugated bilirubin binds albumin more weakly ~ A ~ This is why acidosis, hypoalbuminaemia, sepsis and drugs that displace bilirubin from albumin such as sulphonamides and ceftriaxone lower the threshold at which exchange transfusion becomes necessary.
E ~ Which group of drugs can precipitate an acute attack of intermittent porphyria and why? ~ Barbiturates and other cytochrome P450 inducers, because induction increases haem demand and de-represses ALA synthase ~ Beta blockers, by reducing hepatic blood flow ~ Antacids, by altering iron absorption ~ Antihistamines, by inhibiting ferrochelatase ~ A ~ Fasting, infection and hormonal fluctuation act by the same final route, so avoiding porphyrinogenic drugs and giving carbohydrate and haem arginate together suppress the overactive first step.
@ Biochemistry ~ Integration of metabolism: fed/fasting, diabetes, ketosis ~ BC_INT ~ Harper's Illustrated Biochemistry 32e, Ch. Metabolic Integration
H ~ Which regulatory step explains why fatty acids are oxidised rather than synthesised during fasting? ~ Malonyl-CoA falls, releasing inhibition of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 so fatty acids enter the mitochondrion ~ Acetyl-CoA carboxylase is activated by glucagon ~ Hormone-sensitive lipase is inhibited by adrenaline ~ Carnitine synthesis increases during fasting ~ A ~ The same molecule that commits carbon to synthesis also blocks its oxidation, so this single switch prevents a futile cycle and explains why insulin favours storage and glucagon favours oxidation.
E ~ What is the respiratory quotient for the complete oxidation of carbohydrate compared with that of fat? ~ 1.0 for carbohydrate and about 0.7 for fat ~ 0.7 for carbohydrate and 1.0 for fat ~ 1.0 for both ~ 0.8 for carbohydrate and 0.9 for fat ~ A ~ Fat is more reduced so it consumes more oxygen per carbon dioxide produced, and a value above 1.0 indicates net lipogenesis, which is why overfeeding a ventilated patient increases the carbon dioxide load.
M ~ Roughly how long do hepatic glycogen stores maintain blood glucose during a fast, and what takes over? ~ About 24 hours, after which gluconeogenesis predominates ~ About 7 days, after which ketogenesis begins ~ About 2 hours, after which muscle glycogen is used ~ About 3 days, after which protein breakdown begins ~ A ~ Liver glycogen is limited to roughly 100 grams whereas adipose triacylglycerol holds many days of energy, and muscle glycogen cannot contribute directly because muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.
H ~ Why can the liver synthesise ketone bodies but not use them as fuel? ~ It lacks the ketone transporter ~ It lacks succinyl-CoA acetoacetate transferase, the thiophorase needed to activate acetoacetate ~ It lacks HMG-CoA lyase ~ It cannot oxidise acetyl-CoA ~ B ~ This compartmentalisation guarantees that the ketone bodies made in the liver are exported for use by brain, heart and skeletal muscle rather than consumed at the site of production.
M ~ Which cycle transfers both carbon and nitrogen from muscle to liver during fasting? ~ The Cori cycle, transferring lactate only ~ The glucose-alanine cycle, in which alanine carries the amino nitrogen for ureagenesis and the carbon skeleton for gluconeogenesis ~ The urea cycle ~ The citrate shuttle ~ B ~ Muscle transaminates pyruvate to alanine because it cannot make urea, so the liver receives a substrate that serves gluconeogenesis and nitrogen disposal at the same time.
M ~ A severely malnourished patient develops weakness, arrhythmia and a falling serum phosphate 3 days after starting nutritional support. What has happened? ~ Refeeding syndrome, in which the insulin surge drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells ~ Hepatic encephalopathy ~ Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state ~ Thiamine excess ~ A ~ Depleted intracellular stores are unmasked when anabolism resumes, so feeding is introduced slowly with thiamine before calories and with daily electrolyte monitoring during the first week.
H ~ Which single metabolite reciprocally activates glycolysis and inhibits gluconeogenesis in the liver? ~ Citrate ~ Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate ~ Acetyl-CoA ~ AMP ~ B ~ Insulin raises it and glucagon lowers it through a bifunctional enzyme, so one molecule sets the direction of hepatic glucose handling and prevents the two opposing pathways running at once.
E ~ What is meant by the protein-sparing effect of dietary carbohydrate? ~ Adequate carbohydrate suppresses gluconeogenesis from amino acids so body protein is preserved ~ Carbohydrate is converted directly into protein ~ Carbohydrate improves protein absorption ~ Carbohydrate increases nitrogen excretion ~ A ~ This is why a minimum carbohydrate intake matters in the management of protein-energy malnutrition and why the negative nitrogen balance of starvation is corrected by energy before extra protein.
@ Biochemistry ~ Minerals, trace elements & acid-base biochemistry ~ BC_MIN ~ Vasudevan Textbook of Biochemistry for Medical Students 9e, Ch. Minerals and Acid-Base Balance
E ~ Deficiency of which trace element impairs glutathione peroxidase and causes an endemic cardiomyopathy? ~ Selenium, in Keshan disease ~ Copper, in Menkes disease ~ Manganese ~ Cobalt ~ A ~ The element sits in the enzyme as selenocysteine, so deficiency leaves cells vulnerable to peroxide injury, and Kashin-Beck osteoarthropathy is the skeletal counterpart of the same deficiency.
M ~ A neonate has intractable seizures, lens dislocation and raised urinary sulphite. Deficiency of which trace element cofactor explains this? ~ Zinc ~ Molybdenum, required by sulphite oxidase ~ Selenium ~ Iodine ~ B ~ The same cofactor is needed by xanthine oxidase and aldehyde oxidase, so urate is characteristically low, and the combination of low urate with high sulphite points to the cofactor rather than to a single enzyme defect.
E ~ Which trace element is required for normal insulin action and glucose tolerance? ~ Chromium ~ Nickel ~ Vanadium ~ Silicon ~ A ~ Deficiency has been described in long-term parenteral nutrition without supplementation, producing glucose intolerance and peripheral neuropathy that reverse when the element is replaced.
M ~ Which protein regulates iron absorption, and how is it altered in hereditary haemochromatosis? ~ Ferritin, which is increased ~ Hepcidin, which is inappropriately low so ferroportin remains active and absorption continues ~ Transferrin, which is saturated ~ Ceruloplasmin, which is reduced ~ B ~ Hepcidin normally degrades ferroportin to shut off absorption when stores are full, so its failure explains the progressive overload, and in anaemia of inflammation the opposite occurs with high hepcidin trapping iron in macrophages.
H ~ A patient has a normal anion gap metabolic acidosis. Which measurement distinguishes renal tubular acidosis from gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss? ~ The serum osmolar gap ~ The urinary anion gap, which is positive in renal tubular acidosis and negative with diarrhoea ~ The serum lactate ~ The arterial oxygen tension ~ B ~ A negative value reflects the large ammonium excretion of an appropriately responding kidney, so its absence indicates that the defect in acid handling lies in the kidney itself.
M ~ A patient with severe metabolic acidosis has a measured serum osmolality 30 mOsm above the calculated value. Which poisoning does this suggest? ~ Salicylate ~ Methanol or ethylene glycol ~ Paracetamol ~ Iron ~ B ~ The parent alcohol is osmotically active before it is metabolised, so an early osmolar gap with a later widening anion gap is the classic sequence, and fomepizole or ethanol blocks alcohol dehydrogenase to prevent formation of the toxic acid metabolites.
H ~ Why is the bicarbonate system the most effective extracellular buffer despite a pKa of 6.1, well away from blood pH? ~ It has an unusually high buffering capacity per molecule ~ It is an open system in which carbon dioxide is continuously removed by the lungs and bicarbonate regulated by the kidney ~ It is present at higher concentration than any intracellular buffer ~ Its pKa shifts to 7.4 in plasma ~ B ~ A closed buffer would be nearly exhausted at this distance from its pKa, so it is the physiological regulation of both components rather than the chemistry alone that gives the system its power.
M ~ Which role does phosphate play in renal acid excretion? ~ It acts as titratable acid, accepting a proton in the tubular fluid to allow net acid excretion ~ It is reabsorbed in exchange for bicarbonate ~ It buffers only in the interstitium ~ It is the main urinary buffer at all urine pH values ~ A ~ Ammonium excretion is the larger and more adaptable component, increasing several fold in chronic acidosis, whereas titratable acid is limited by the amount of phosphate filtered.
@ Biochemistry ~ Nucleotide metabolism: purine/pyrimidine, gout ~ BC_NUC ~ Vasudevan Textbook of Biochemistry for Medical Students 9e, Ch. Nucleotide Metabolism
M ~ Which enzyme does 5-fluorouracil inhibit, and what is the consequence? ~ Dihydrofolate reductase, depleting tetrahydrofolate ~ Thymidylate synthase, so dTMP cannot be made and DNA synthesis fails ~ Ribonucleotide reductase, blocking deoxyribonucleotide formation ~ Xanthine oxidase, raising urate ~ B ~ Its active metabolite forms a stable complex with the enzyme and its folate cofactor, which is why leucovorin potentiates rather than rescues from fluorouracil, in contrast to its use after methotrexate.
H ~ A patient develops profound myelosuppression soon after starting azathioprine. Which enzyme polymorphism explains this? ~ Low thiopurine methyltransferase activity, so more drug is shunted to active thioguanine nucleotides ~ Increased xanthine oxidase activity ~ Deficiency of hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase ~ Increased glucuronyl transferase activity ~ A ~ Testing activity or genotype before treatment identifies those needing a reduced dose, and coprescribing allopurinol is hazardous because blocking the other catabolic route diverts still more drug to the cytotoxic pathway.
M ~ How does probenecid lower serum urate, and when is it inappropriate? ~ It inhibits xanthine oxidase, and is inappropriate in renal failure ~ It blocks urate reabsorption in the proximal tubule, and is inappropriate in urate overproduction or a history of urate stones ~ It converts urate to allantoin, and is inappropriate in G6PD deficiency ~ It alkalinises the urine, and is inappropriate in hypertension ~ B ~ Increasing urinary urate excretion risks crystallisation in the tubules, so a xanthine oxidase inhibitor is preferred when the 24-hour urinary urate is already high or stones have occurred.
M ~ Which agent is used to prevent tumour lysis nephropathy in a child with a high leukaemic burden, and by what mechanism? ~ Rasburicase, a recombinant urate oxidase that converts urate to soluble allantoin ~ Allopurinol, which converts urate to allantoin ~ Probenecid, which increases urate excretion ~ Colchicine, which prevents crystal formation ~ A ~ Humans lack urate oxidase, so supplying it bypasses the least soluble end product entirely, and it is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency because the hydrogen peroxide generated causes haemolysis.
H ~ Why does a complete block in pyrimidine catabolism cause less clinical harm than one in purine catabolism? ~ Pyrimidines are not catabolised in humans ~ Pyrimidine degradation yields highly water-soluble products such as beta-alanine and ammonia, whereas purine degradation ends in poorly soluble urate ~ Pyrimidines are excreted unchanged in bile ~ Purine catabolism produces no nitrogen ~ B ~ This solubility difference is the reason gout is a purine disorder, and it also explains why disorders of pyrimidine catabolism present with neurological or drug-toxicity phenotypes rather than with crystal deposition.
E ~ Which molecule is the common activated precursor for purine synthesis, pyrimidine synthesis and NAD salvage? ~ 5-phosphoribosyl-1-pyrophosphate ~ Carbamoyl phosphate ~ Inosine monophosphate ~ Orotic acid ~ A ~ Its central position explains why the enzyme that makes it is a key regulatory point, and why superactivity of that enzyme causes purine overproduction with gout in childhood.
M ~ Why do alcohol excess and lactic acidosis precipitate an acute attack of gout? ~ They increase de novo purine synthesis directly ~ Organic acids compete with urate for tubular secretion, so urate excretion falls ~ They inhibit xanthine oxidase ~ They increase intestinal purine absorption ~ B ~ Low-dose aspirin, thiazides and ketoacidosis act by the same competition at the tubular transporter, whereas beer also adds a purine load, so alcohol raises urate by two routes at once.
E ~ How does colchicine relieve an acute attack of gout? ~ It lowers serum urate rapidly ~ It binds tubulin and prevents microtubule assembly, so neutrophil migration and inflammasome activation are inhibited ~ It dissolves urate crystals in the joint ~ It inhibits cyclooxygenase ~ B ~ Because it does not alter urate it neither treats nor worsens the underlying hyperuricaemia, and its narrow margin with diarrhoea as the dose-limiting effect is why it is given in low dose.
