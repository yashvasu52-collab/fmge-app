@ Pediatrics ~ Acute respiratory infection, pneumonia & bronchiolitis ~ PED_ARI ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Respiratory System
E ~ Under the WHO and IMNCI approach, a child of 2 years with cough and a respiratory rate of 45 per minute with no chest indrawing is classified as: ~ Pneumonia, treated with oral amoxicillin at home ~ Severe pneumonia needing admission ~ No pneumonia, only a cough or cold ~ Very severe disease ~ A ~ The age-specific cut-offs are 60 per minute under 2 months, 50 from 2 to 12 months and 40 from 1 to 5 years, and fast breathing without indrawing defines pneumonia manageable with oral antibiotics.
E ~ Which sign in a child with cough indicates severe pneumonia requiring referral and injectable antibiotics? ~ Lower chest wall indrawing or any general danger sign ~ Fever above 38 degrees ~ Audible wheeze ~ Nasal discharge ~ A ~ Chest indrawing marks increased work of breathing from reduced compliance, and general danger signs are inability to drink, persistent vomiting, convulsions, lethargy or unconsciousness.
E ~ Bronchiolitis in an infant is most commonly caused by: ~ Respiratory syncytial virus ~ Streptococcus pneumoniae ~ Mycoplasma pneumoniae ~ Bordetella pertussis ~ A ~ Winter epidemics of respiratory syncytial virus cause obstruction of the small airways with hyperinflation, and the illness peaks at 3 to 5 days with recovery over 1 to 2 weeks.
E ~ The mainstay of treatment for an infant with bronchiolitis is: ~ Supportive care with oxygen, nasal suction and attention to feeding ~ Nebulised salbutamol ~ Systemic corticosteroids ~ Oral antibiotics ~ A ~ Trials show no benefit from bronchodilators, steroids or antibiotics in typical bronchiolitis, so management is minimal handling with oxygen and hydration, and nasogastric or intravenous fluids if feeding fails.
M ~ Which organism should be suspected in a school-aged child with a persistent dry cough, low-grade fever, patchy infiltrates and a normal-looking child on examination? ~ Mycoplasma pneumoniae ~ Staphylococcus aureus ~ Klebsiella pneumoniae ~ Respiratory syncytial virus ~ A ~ The classic mismatch between marked radiographic changes and mild clinical findings gives the name walking pneumonia, and a macrolide is used because the organism has no cell wall.
M ~ A 6-month-old with measles develops rapidly worsening pneumonia with pneumatoceles and an empyema on chest radiograph. The likely organism is: ~ Staphylococcus aureus ~ Streptococcus pneumoniae ~ Haemophilus influenzae ~ Mycoplasma pneumoniae ~ A ~ Staphylococcal pneumonia characteristically produces rapid progression with pneumatoceles, empyema and pyopneumothorax, and post-measles immunosuppression is a recognised predisposing factor.
M ~ Which measure has the greatest impact on childhood pneumonia mortality at a population level? ~ Pneumococcal and Haemophilus influenzae type b conjugate vaccination with exclusive breastfeeding ~ Routine antibiotic prophylaxis ~ Widespread use of cough syrups ~ Vitamin C supplementation ~ A ~ Vaccination against the two commonest bacterial causes, together with breastfeeding, reduced indoor air pollution and zinc for diarrhoea, addresses the determinants rather than the episode.
M ~ A 3-year-old presents with sudden onset of coughing and unilateral wheeze with reduced air entry after playing with peanuts. The most appropriate next step is: ~ Rigid bronchoscopy for removal of a foreign body ~ Nebulised bronchodilator and observation ~ Oral corticosteroids ~ A course of antibiotics ~ A ~ Unilateral signs of obstruction after a choking episode indicate an inhaled foreign body, most often lodging in the right main bronchus, and a normal radiograph does not exclude it.
H ~ Why is a child with bronchiolitis at higher risk of apnoea than an older child with pneumonia? ~ Immature central respiratory control in infants under 2 months, particularly if preterm ~ Higher airway resistance from a larger trachea ~ Greater lung compliance ~ Higher haemoglobin ~ A ~ Apnoea may be the presenting feature in young or preterm infants, which is why age under 2 months and prematurity are indications for admission and monitoring irrespective of oxygen saturation.
H ~ Which finding distinguishes empyema requiring drainage from a simple parapneumonic effusion? ~ Frank pus, pH below 7.2, glucose below 40 mg per dL or organisms on Gram stain ~ Any effusion visible on radiograph ~ A pleural protein above 2 g per dL ~ Fever persisting for 48 hours ~ A ~ These biochemical markers indicate a complicated collection that will loculate and not resolve with antibiotics alone, so intercostal drainage with or without fibrinolytics is required.
@ Pediatrics ~ Behavioural & developmental disorders; adolescent health ~ PED_BEH ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Behavioural and Developmental Disorders
E ~ Which pattern of findings defines autism spectrum disorder? ~ Persistent deficits in social communication with restricted repetitive behaviours, present from early childhood ~ Inattention with hyperactivity and impulsivity ~ Isolated delay in expressive speech ~ Selective mutism in school only ~ A ~ The two symptom domains must both be present and must impair function, and early identification matters because intensive behavioural intervention improves outcome.
E ~ A 7-year-old has inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity present at home and at school for over 6 months, beginning before age 12. The diagnosis is: ~ Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder ~ Oppositional defiant disorder ~ Conduct disorder ~ Specific learning disorder ~ A ~ Symptoms must be present in more than one setting to exclude situational behaviour, and behavioural parent training with school support is first line in younger children before methylphenidate is considered.
E ~ Nocturnal enuresis is defined as involuntary bed-wetting beyond the age of: ~ 5 years ~ 2 years ~ 3 years ~ 8 years ~ A ~ Most children achieve night-time continence by 5, and primary monosymptomatic enuresis is managed with reassurance, fluid timing, an enuresis alarm and desmopressin for short-term cover.
M ~ A 4-year-old with global developmental delay has coarse facies, macroglossia, a large tongue and delayed dentition, with an umbilical hernia. Which screening test is essential? ~ Thyroid function tests ~ Serum ammonia ~ Karyotype only ~ Electroencephalogram ~ A ~ Congenital hypothyroidism is treatable and its features are easily missed, so a delayed presentation with these signs must prompt thyroid testing; newborn screening detects most cases earlier.
M ~ A 3-year-old speaks in single words only, does not point to share interest and does not respond to his name, but hearing has not been tested. The essential first investigation is: ~ Formal audiological assessment ~ Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain ~ Karyotype ~ Electroencephalogram ~ A ~ Hearing impairment is the commonest reversible cause of speech delay and can mimic autism, so audiometry precedes any other investigation in a child with delayed language.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes a breath-holding spell from an epileptic seizure in a toddler? ~ A clear precipitant such as pain or anger with cyanosis or pallor preceding the loss of consciousness ~ Postictal drowsiness lasting an hour ~ Tongue biting ~ Occurrence during sleep ~ A ~ Breath-holding spells always have a trigger and the colour change comes first, whereas seizures may occur in sleep with prolonged postictal confusion; iron deficiency is a treatable contributor.
M ~ Which is the most appropriate first-line management of a 5-year-old with frequent temper tantrums and defiance? ~ Behavioural parent training with consistent limits and positive reinforcement ~ Risperidone ~ Methylphenidate ~ Referral for electroencephalography ~ A ~ Structured behavioural approaches are more effective and safer than medication for disruptive behaviour at this age, and medication is reserved for severe aggression after behavioural measures fail.
M ~ An adolescent girl has amenorrhoea, a body mass index of 15 with intense fear of weight gain and excessive exercise. Which complication requires the most urgent monitoring on refeeding? ~ Hypophosphataemia and cardiac failure of the refeeding syndrome ~ Hyperkalaemia ~ Hypercalcaemia ~ Metabolic alkalosis ~ A ~ Reintroducing carbohydrate triggers insulin release that drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells, so calories are increased gradually with daily electrolyte monitoring and thiamine.
H ~ Which developmental red flag at 18 months most strongly warrants immediate referral? ~ Loss of previously acquired babbling, gesture or social skills ~ Not yet running ~ Speaking fewer than 20 words ~ Not yet using a spoon ~ A ~ Regression is never normal at any age and raises the possibility of a neurodegenerative or metabolic disorder as well as autism, so it takes priority over isolated delay in a single milestone.
H ~ Which principle governs consent for a competent 16-year-old adolescent seeking contraception in India? ~ Confidential counselling should be provided, with the adolescent's best interest and the legal framework on age of consent both considered ~ Parental consent is always mandatory before any discussion ~ Contraception can never be provided under 18 ~ No documentation is required ~ A ~ Adolescent-friendly services aim to be accessible and confidential while clinicians remain alert to coercion, abuse and mandatory reporting obligations under child protection law.
@ Pediatrics ~ Childhood tuberculosis & paediatric HIV ~ PED_PTB ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Tuberculosis and HIV in Children
E ~ Which specimen is preferred for microbiological confirmation of pulmonary tuberculosis in a child too young to expectorate? ~ Gastric aspirate or induced sputum for cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification testing ~ Blood culture ~ Nasal swab ~ Urine microscopy ~ A ~ Young children swallow their secretions, so early morning gastric aspirate, induced sputum or a nasopharyngeal aspirate gives the best yield, and molecular testing also reports rifampicin resistance.
E ~ Childhood tuberculosis differs from adult disease in that children more often have: ~ Paucibacillary disease with lymph node and disseminated involvement rather than cavitation ~ Cavitary upper lobe disease ~ Positive sputum smears ~ Isolated pleural effusion ~ A ~ Immaturity of cell-mediated immunity favours haematogenous spread with miliary and meningeal disease, which is why the BCG vaccine targets exactly those severe forms.
E ~ Which regimen is recommended for tuberculosis preventive treatment of a 3-year-old household contact of a smear-positive case after active disease is excluded? ~ Isoniazid daily for 6 months, or a shorter rifampicin-containing regimen per current programme guidance ~ No treatment as the child is asymptomatic ~ Four-drug therapy for 6 months ~ BCG revaccination ~ A ~ Contacts under 5 are at highest risk of progression to disease, so preventive treatment after excluding active tuberculosis is one of the highest-yield interventions in the programme.
M ~ A child on antitubercular therapy for tuberculous meningitis should also receive: ~ Corticosteroids, which reduce mortality and neurological sequelae ~ Intravenous immunoglobulin ~ Prophylactic phenytoin for a year ~ Repeated therapeutic lumbar punctures ~ A ~ Dexamethasone or prednisolone reduces the inflammatory exudate that causes basal meningeal fibrosis, cranial nerve palsies and hydrocephalus, and this is one of the few clear indications for steroids in tuberculosis.
M ~ Which feature on chest radiograph is most characteristic of primary tuberculosis in a child? ~ Hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy, sometimes with airway compression ~ Apical cavitation ~ Bilateral upper lobe fibrosis ~ Lobar consolidation with air bronchogram ~ A ~ Nodal enlargement out of proportion to the parenchymal focus is the hallmark, and compression of a bronchus can cause lobar collapse or hyperinflation from a ball-valve effect.
M ~ Which finding indicates severe immunosuppression in a child under 5 with HIV, according to WHO staging? ~ A CD4 percentage below 25 percent in a child under 12 months ~ A CD4 count below 200 cells per microlitre at any age ~ Any detectable viral load ~ Weight for age below the third centile ~ A ~ Because absolute CD4 counts are physiologically high and variable in infancy, percentages are used in young children, and infants are treated regardless of counts because progression is rapid.
M ~ Which antiretroviral consideration is specific to infants diagnosed with HIV? ~ Antiretroviral therapy is started immediately regardless of clinical or immunological stage ~ Therapy is deferred until CD4 falls ~ Only two drugs are used ~ Therapy is started after 2 years of age ~ A ~ Without treatment about a third of infected infants die in the first year, so early therapy following early infant diagnosis by nucleic acid testing at 6 weeks transformed survival.
M ~ Which method is used for early infant diagnosis of HIV in a 6-week-old born to a positive mother? ~ HIV DNA or RNA nucleic acid testing ~ Rapid antibody test ~ Western blot ~ CD4 count ~ A ~ Maternal IgG persists for up to 18 months so antibody tests cannot distinguish infection from passive transfer, making virological testing essential in the first months of life.
H ~ Why can a tuberculin skin test be negative in a child with miliary tuberculosis? ~ Overwhelming disease suppresses cell-mediated immunity, producing anergy ~ The antigen is degraded in disseminated disease ~ Antibody blocks the reaction ~ The test is only valid after 5 years of age ~ A ~ Other causes of false negatives include HIV, malnutrition, measles, corticosteroids and testing within the window before hypersensitivity develops, so a negative test never excludes tuberculosis.
H ~ Which paediatric HIV presentation should prompt testing even without known maternal status? ~ Recurrent severe bacterial infection with failure to thrive, chronic parotid swelling and generalised lymphadenopathy ~ Isolated febrile seizure ~ Single episode of otitis media ~ Iron deficiency anaemia ~ A ~ Persistent generalised lymphadenopathy, parotitis, oral thrush beyond infancy, recurrent severe infection and growth failure are the classic clinical clues to vertically acquired HIV.
@ Pediatrics ~ Congenital & rheumatic heart disease in children ~ PED_CHD ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Cardiovascular System
E ~ Which is the commonest congenital heart defect in children? ~ Ventricular septal defect ~ Atrial septal defect ~ Tetralogy of Fallot ~ Patent ductus arteriosus ~ A ~ Ventricular septal defect accounts for around a third of congenital heart disease, and small muscular defects often close spontaneously in the first years of life.
E ~ Which congenital heart lesion is the commonest cause of cyanotic heart disease presenting after infancy? ~ Tetralogy of Fallot ~ Transposition of the great arteries ~ Total anomalous pulmonary venous connection ~ Tricuspid atresia ~ A ~ Transposition presents in the first days of life, whereas the degree of pulmonary stenosis in tetralogy determines the age of presentation, and squatting increases systemic resistance to reduce the right-to-left shunt.
E ~ A neonate is deeply cyanosed on day 1 with a single loud second sound and an egg-on-side cardiac silhouette. The immediate life-saving measure is: ~ Prostaglandin E1 infusion to maintain ductal patency, with consideration of balloon atrial septostomy ~ Indomethacin to close the duct ~ Immediate corrective surgery in the first hour ~ Oxygen alone ~ A ~ In transposition survival depends on mixing, so keeping the duct open and enlarging the atrial communication buys time until an arterial switch operation.
M ~ Which lesion produces a continuous machinery murmur with bounding pulses and a wide pulse pressure in a preterm infant? ~ Patent ductus arteriosus ~ Ventricular septal defect ~ Atrial septal defect ~ Coarctation of the aorta ~ A ~ Failure of ductal closure is common in prematurity and causes a left-to-right shunt with pulmonary overcirculation, and indomethacin, ibuprofen or paracetamol is used to close it.
M ~ A 6-year-old has a continuous murmur, differential blood pressure between arms and legs, and rib notching on radiograph. The diagnosis is: ~ Coarctation of the aorta ~ Aortic stenosis ~ Patent ductus arteriosus ~ Pulmonary stenosis ~ A ~ Collateral intercostal vessels erode the ribs over years, and the association with Turner syndrome and with bicuspid aortic valve means both should be sought.
M ~ Which finding distinguishes an innocent murmur from a pathological one in a child? ~ A soft systolic murmur that varies with posture, with normal pulses, no thrill and a normal second sound ~ A grade 4 murmur with a thrill ~ A diastolic murmur ~ A continuous murmur with a fixed split second sound ~ A ~ Innocent murmurs are systolic, soft, position-dependent and unaccompanied by other signs, whereas any diastolic murmur, thrill or abnormal second sound requires echocardiography.
M ~ According to the revised Jones criteria, which change was made for populations at high risk of rheumatic fever such as India? ~ Subclinical carditis on echocardiography counts as a major criterion and monoarthritis or polyarthralgia may substitute for polyarthritis ~ Chorea was removed as a major criterion ~ Fever was removed entirely ~ Two minor criteria alone became sufficient ~ A ~ Recognising echocardiographic carditis and lower joint thresholds improves sensitivity where the disease burden is high, at some cost in specificity.
M ~ Secondary prophylaxis after an attack of rheumatic fever without carditis should continue for: ~ 5 years or until age 21, whichever is longer ~ 1 year ~ 10 years or until age 40 ~ Lifelong in all cases ~ A ~ Duration is graded by the presence and severity of carditis, extending to 10 years or age 21 with carditis and often lifelong with residual valve disease.
H ~ Which congenital lesion is associated with maternal lithium use, and what is the classic echocardiographic finding? ~ Ebstein anomaly, with apical displacement of the tricuspid valve and a large right atrium ~ Tetralogy of Fallot with a boot-shaped heart ~ Transposition with an egg-on-side silhouette ~ Coarctation with rib notching ~ A ~ Downward displacement of the septal and posterior tricuspid leaflets atrialises the right ventricle, causing tricuspid regurgitation and often an associated atrial communication with right-to-left shunting.
H ~ Why does a large ventricular septal defect present at 6 to 8 weeks rather than at birth? ~ Pulmonary vascular resistance falls over the first weeks, so the left-to-right shunt and its symptoms increase ~ The defect enlarges with growth ~ Haemoglobin falls physiologically ~ The ductus closes at that time ~ A ~ At birth right and left pressures are nearly equal so shunting is minimal, and heart failure appears as resistance falls; this is why the murmur may also become louder with time.
@ Pediatrics ~ Fluid & electrolyte therapy and diarrhoeal disease in children ~ PED_DIA ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Diarrhoeal Diseases and Fluid Therapy
E ~ Which osmolarity characterises the WHO-recommended reduced osmolarity oral rehydration solution? ~ About 245 mOsm per litre with 75 mmol sodium and 75 mmol glucose ~ About 311 mOsm per litre ~ About 400 mOsm per litre ~ About 150 mOsm per litre ~ A ~ The lower osmolarity reduces stool output, vomiting and the need for unscheduled intravenous fluid compared with the older formulation, without increasing hyponatraemia.
E ~ A 9-month-old is treated for acute diarrhoea. For how long, and at what dose, should zinc be continued after the diarrhoea stops? ~ 14 days in total at 20 mg daily ~ Only until the diarrhoea stops ~ 3 days at 10 mg daily ~ 6 weeks at 40 mg daily ~ A ~ The full 14-day course is what reduces recurrence over the following two to three months, and infants under 6 months receive 10 mg daily; stopping when stools settle loses most of the benefit.
E ~ A child with diarrhoea is restless and irritable with sunken eyes, drinks eagerly and has a skin pinch that goes back slowly. The classification and plan are: ~ Some dehydration, treated with oral rehydration solution under supervision (Plan B) ~ No dehydration, Plan A ~ Severe dehydration, Plan C ~ Severe malnutrition with shock ~ A ~ Two or more of these signs define some dehydration, requiring about 75 mL per kg of oral rehydration solution over 4 hours with reassessment, whereas lethargy or inability to drink indicates severe dehydration.
M ~ In severe dehydration in a child over 12 months, the recommended intravenous regimen is: ~ Ringer lactate 100 mL per kg, giving 30 mL per kg in the first 30 minutes then 70 mL per kg over 2.5 hours ~ Normal saline 20 mL per kg once only ~ Dextrose 5 percent 100 mL per kg over 6 hours ~ Half-strength saline over 24 hours ~ A ~ Infants under 12 months receive the same total volume but more slowly, with the first 30 mL per kg over 1 hour, and reassessment guides repetition of the bolus.
M ~ A 2-year-old with acute watery diarrhoea is brought by parents requesting a stool test and an antibiotic. He is alert with no dehydration. The correct advice is: ~ No stool test or antibiotic is needed; give oral rehydration solution, zinc and continued feeding ~ Send a stool culture and start ciprofloxacin ~ Give an antimotility agent to reduce stool frequency ~ Withhold feeds for 24 hours to rest the bowel ~ A ~ Most acute watery diarrhoea is viral and self-limiting, routine stool culture changes nothing, antimotility drugs are contraindicated in children, and withholding feeds worsens nutrition and mucosal recovery.
M ~ Which electrolyte abnormality should be anticipated in a malnourished child with prolonged diarrhoea and is a common cause of persistent lethargy? ~ Hypokalaemia ~ Hypernatraemia ~ Hypercalcaemia ~ Metabolic alkalosis ~ A ~ Total body potassium is depleted by stool losses and by malnutrition itself, so oral rehydration solution and potassium supplementation are given, and correction is essential before nutritional rehabilitation.
M ~ Which fluid is preferred in a child with severe acute malnutrition who is in shock, and why? ~ Half-strength Darrow solution with dextrose or Ringer lactate with dextrose, given cautiously because of the risk of cardiac failure ~ Full-volume rapid Ringer lactate bolus as in a normally nourished child ~ Normal saline 40 mL per kg rapidly ~ Colloid ~ A ~ Myocardial function and sodium handling are impaired in severe malnutrition, so slower, smaller boluses with close monitoring for pulmonary oedema are used, and ReSoMal is preferred for oral rehydration.
M ~ Persistent diarrhoea in a child is defined as an episode lasting: ~ 14 days or longer ~ 3 days or longer ~ 7 days or longer ~ 28 days or longer ~ A ~ Persistent diarrhoea carries a high mortality because of coexisting malnutrition, and management centres on a lactose-reduced diet, micronutrients and treating specific infections rather than on antimotility drugs.
H ~ Why does glucose in oral rehydration solution enhance sodium and water absorption even when the enterocyte is damaged by cholera toxin? ~ The sodium-glucose cotransporter SGLT1 remains intact and drives coupled sodium absorption with osmotic water movement ~ Glucose neutralises the toxin ~ Glucose blocks chloride secretion ~ Glucose increases intestinal motility ~ A ~ Cholera toxin increases chloride secretion but does not damage the absorptive cotransporter, which is the physiological insight that made oral rehydration therapy possible and saved millions of lives.
H ~ A child with diarrhoea develops seizures and a serum sodium of 160 mmol per litre. Correction must be gradual because: ~ Rapid lowering of sodium causes cerebral oedema as water shifts into brain cells that have accumulated osmolytes ~ Rapid correction causes osmotic demyelination ~ Sodium cannot be corrected orally ~ Seizures always require hypertonic saline ~ A ~ In hypernatraemia the brain accumulates idiogenic osmoles, so sodium is lowered by no more than 10 to 12 mmol per litre per day; osmotic demyelination is the hazard of correcting chronic hyponatraemia too fast.
