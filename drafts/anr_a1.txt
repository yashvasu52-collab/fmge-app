@ Anaesthesia ~ General anaesthetics: IV induction & inhalational agents ~ AN_GA ~ Morgan and Mikhail Clinical Anesthesiology 6e, Ch. Inhalational and Intravenous Anesthetics
M ~ Which inhalational agent is avoided in a patient at risk of raised intracranial pressure, and why? ~ Nitrous oxide and high concentrations of volatile agents, because they cause cerebral vasodilatation and increase cerebral blood volume ~ Sevoflurane at low concentration, because it reduces cerebral metabolic rate ~ Isoflurane, because it causes cerebral vasoconstriction ~ All inhalational agents are equally safe ~ A ~ Intravenous agents such as propofol and thiopentone reduce cerebral blood flow and metabolic rate in parallel, which is why a total intravenous technique with controlled ventilation is preferred in neuroanaesthesia.
M ~ Which factor most increases the speed of induction with an inhalational agent? ~ A low blood-gas partition coefficient combined with high inspired concentration and high alveolar ventilation ~ A high blood-gas partition coefficient ~ A high cardiac output ~ A high body fat content ~ A ~ A high cardiac output actually slows the rise of alveolar concentration because more agent is carried away from the lung, which is why induction is faster in shock and slower in a hyperdynamic circulation.
E ~ Which intravenous induction agent provides analgesia and maintains airway reflexes and blood pressure, making it useful in field conditions? ~ Ketamine ~ Propofol ~ Thiopentone ~ Etomidate ~ A ~ It produces dissociative anaesthesia with bronchodilatation, so it is valuable in the hypovolaemic patient and in asthma, and its rise in intracranial and intraocular pressure and emergence phenomena are the limiting concerns.
M ~ Which adverse effect limits the use of etomidate for maintenance or repeated dosing? ~ Adrenocortical suppression through inhibition of 11-beta-hydroxylase ~ Malignant hyperthermia ~ Hepatic necrosis ~ Prolonged neuromuscular blockade ~ A ~ Its haemodynamic stability makes it attractive for a single induction dose in cardiac disease, but the suppression of cortisol synthesis is the reason infusions were abandoned.
H ~ Why does diffusion hypoxia occur at the end of an anaesthetic that included nitrous oxide? ~ Large volumes of the poorly soluble gas leave the blood and dilute alveolar oxygen, so 100 percent oxygen is given for several minutes at the end ~ The agent binds haemoglobin and displaces oxygen ~ It causes bronchospasm on emergence ~ It suppresses the respiratory centre for hours ~ A ~ The same rapid movement of large volumes explains why the gas expands closed air spaces, which is why it is avoided in pneumothorax, bowel obstruction and middle ear surgery.
@ Anaesthesia ~ Muscle relaxants, monitoring, anaesthesia machine & breathing circuits ~ AN_MR ~ Morgan and Mikhail Clinical Anesthesiology 6e, Ch. Neuromuscular Blocking Agents and Breathing Systems
M ~ Which agent reverses rocuronium blockade by encapsulating the drug rather than by inhibiting cholinesterase? ~ Sugammadex ~ Neostigmine ~ Edrophonium ~ Pyridostigmine ~ A ~ Because it removes the drug from the neuromuscular junction rather than raising acetylcholine, it can reverse even profound blockade and needs no antimuscarinic cover, avoiding the bradycardia and secretions caused by neostigmine with glycopyrrolate.
M ~ Which safety feature of the anaesthesia machine prevents delivery of a hypoxic gas mixture? ~ The oxygen-nitrous oxide proportioning system with an oxygen failure alarm and fail-safe valve ~ The vaporiser interlock ~ The scavenging system ~ The reservoir bag ~ A ~ The pin index system separately prevents a wrong cylinder being fitted and the non-interchangeable colour-coded pipeline connections prevent a wrong wall supply, so several independent layers guard the same hazard.
E ~ Which monitor is mandatory for confirming correct tracheal placement of an endotracheal tube and detecting disconnection? ~ Continuous waveform capnography ~ Pulse oximetry ~ Precordial stethoscope ~ Chest radiograph ~ A ~ A sustained carbon dioxide waveform over several breaths confirms tracheal placement, and its sudden loss signals disconnection, obstruction, oesophageal displacement or cardiac arrest, which is why it is a standard of care.
M ~ Which condition should be suspected when the peak airway pressure rises sharply with a slanted upstroke on the capnograph and wheeze during anaesthesia? ~ Bronchospasm, treated by deepening anaesthesia and giving a bronchodilator ~ Exhausted soda lime ~ Malignant hyperthermia ~ Circuit disconnection ~ A ~ The differential includes a kinked or blocked tube, endobronchial intubation and light anaesthesia, so the tube position and circuit must be checked at the same time as the bronchodilator is given.
H ~ Which breathing system is most efficient for controlled ventilation, and why? ~ The Mapleson D system and its coaxial Bain modification, because fresh gas enters near the patient so alveolar gas is preferentially vented ~ The Mapleson A system, which is most efficient for controlled ventilation ~ The Mapleson B system ~ An open drop system ~ A ~ The Mapleson A arrangement is instead the most efficient for spontaneous ventilation, so the efficiency ranking reverses between spontaneous and controlled modes.
@ Anaesthesia ~ Regional, spinal & epidural anaesthesia; postoperative care, pain & complications ~ AN_RA ~ Morgan and Mikhail Clinical Anesthesiology 6e, Ch. Spinal, Epidural and Caudal Blocks
M ~ Which treatment is specific for local anaesthetic systemic toxicity causing cardiac arrest? ~ Intravenous lipid emulsion in addition to standard resuscitation, with prolonged resuscitation efforts ~ Sodium bicarbonate alone ~ Calcium gluconate ~ Flumazenil ~ A ~ Bupivacaine binds avidly to cardiac sodium channels so recovery can take a long time, which is why resuscitation is continued far longer than usual and lipid is given to provide a sink for the drug.
M ~ Which factor most determines the spread of a hyperbaric spinal anaesthetic? ~ The baricity of the solution together with patient position and the dose injected ~ The speed of injection alone ~ The patient weight ~ The gauge of the needle ~ A ~ Height and volume are far less influential than commonly assumed, and understanding this is what allows a saddle block or a unilateral block to be produced deliberately by positioning.
E ~ Which analgesic approach is the basis of the World Health Organization ladder for cancer pain? ~ Stepwise escalation from non-opioid, to weak opioid, to strong opioid, with adjuvants at every step and dosing by the clock ~ Strong opioids only when death is imminent ~ Intramuscular injections on demand ~ Neuraxial blocks as the first step ~ A ~ Giving analgesia by the clock rather than as required and by mouth wherever possible are as important as the choice of drug, and adjuvants such as antidepressants and anticonvulsants address neuropathic pain at any step.
M ~ Which complication of an epidural catheter presents with progressive back pain, leg weakness and sphincter disturbance and requires urgent imaging? ~ Epidural haematoma or abscess, needing urgent magnetic resonance imaging and decompression within hours ~ Post-dural puncture headache ~ Transient neurological symptoms from lidocaine ~ Horner syndrome ~ A ~ Delay beyond a few hours before decompression predicts permanent paraplegia, which is why anticoagulant timing rules around catheter insertion and removal are followed strictly and neurological observations are charted.
H ~ Why does an intrathecal opioid such as morphine risk delayed respiratory depression? ~ Its low lipid solubility keeps it in cerebrospinal fluid, allowing rostral spread to the brainstem respiratory centres over several hours ~ It is absorbed rapidly into the blood ~ It causes bronchospasm ~ It blocks the phrenic nerve ~ A ~ Lipophilic drugs such as fentanyl are taken up by cord tissue and act more locally and briefly, which is why a patient given intrathecal morphine needs monitoring of respiration and sedation for up to 24 hours.
@ Anaesthesia ~ Preop assessment (ASA), airway management ~ AN_PRE ~ Morgan and Mikhail Clinical Anesthesiology 6e, Ch. Preoperative Assessment and Airway Management
M ~ Which bedside findings together predict a difficult direct laryngoscopy? ~ A short thyromental distance, restricted neck extension, limited mouth opening, a high Mallampati class and prominent incisors ~ A high body mass index alone ~ A history of smoking ~ Age above 60 years alone ~ A ~ No single test is reliable so they are combined, and the prediction matters because it determines whether an awake or videolaryngoscope-assisted technique is planned rather than discovered after induction.
M ~ How long should elective surgery be deferred after an uncomplicated myocardial infarction, and what governs the decision? ~ Ideally at least 6 weeks and preferably longer, with the decision governed by functional capacity, ongoing ischaemia and the urgency of surgery ~ Two days, if the patient is pain free ~ Five years in all cases ~ Deferral is unnecessary ~ A ~ Recent coronary stenting adds the separate constraint of dual antiplatelet therapy duration, so cardiology involvement in timing is what prevents both perioperative infarction and stent thrombosis.
@ Radiology ~ Chest X-ray signs ~ RD_CXR ~ Sutton Textbook of Radiology and Imaging 7e, Ch. The Chest
M ~ A chest radiograph shows a well-defined opacity with an air-fluid level and a surrounding rim within a cavity in a patient with haemoptysis. Which sign is this and what does it indicate? ~ The air crescent or meniscus sign, indicating a fungal ball or aspergilloma in a pre-existing cavity ~ The silhouette sign, indicating consolidation ~ The tram-track sign, indicating bronchiectasis ~ The deep sulcus sign, indicating pneumothorax ~ A ~ It is seen in an old tuberculous cavity colonised by Aspergillus, and a similar crescent appearing during recovery from invasive aspergillosis reflects retraction of necrotic lung and carries a risk of major haemoptysis.
M ~ A supine trauma radiograph shows an abnormally deep and lucent lateral costophrenic angle on one side. Which diagnosis does this indicate? ~ Pneumothorax, shown by the deep sulcus sign because free air collects anteriorly and basally in the supine position ~ Pleural effusion ~ Lobar collapse ~ Diaphragmatic rupture ~ A ~ The usual apical air rim is not seen when the patient is supine, so this sign is what prevents a tension pneumothorax being missed on a portable film in a ventilated trauma patient.
E ~ Which radiographic feature indicates lobar collapse rather than consolidation? ~ Volume loss with mediastinal or tracheal shift towards the opacity, crowded ribs and elevation of the hemidiaphragm ~ An air bronchogram with preserved lung volume ~ Shift of the mediastinum away from the opacity ~ Widening of the intercostal spaces on the affected side ~ A ~ Consolidation fills the alveoli without reducing volume, so the direction of any mediastinal shift is the most useful single discriminator between collapse, consolidation and a large effusion.
@ Radiology ~ Choice of imaging modality; contrast ~ RD_MOD ~ Sutton Textbook of Radiology and Imaging 7e, Ch. Imaging Techniques and Contrast Media
M ~ Which imaging modality is the investigation of choice for suspected acute cholecystitis, and why? ~ Ultrasound, because it detects stones, wall thickening and pericholecystic fluid without radiation and at the bedside ~ Non-contrast computed tomography, because it detects all gallstones ~ Magnetic resonance imaging, because it is fastest ~ Plain abdominal radiograph, because most stones are radio-opaque ~ A ~ Only a minority of gallstones are calcified enough to be seen on a plain film, and magnetic resonance cholangiography is reserved for suspected ductal stones when ultrasound is inconclusive.
M ~ Which patient factor most increases the risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis after gadolinium contrast? ~ Severe renal impairment with an estimated glomerular filtration rate below 30 ~ A previous reaction to iodinated contrast ~ Well-controlled diabetes with normal renal function ~ Asthma ~ A ~ Iodinated contrast carries the different risk of contrast-associated acute kidney injury, so knowing which contrast agent carries which renal hazard is what determines the pre-imaging checks and the choice of modality.
