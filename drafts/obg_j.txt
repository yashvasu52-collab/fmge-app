E ~ Preeclampsia can now be diagnosed without proteinuria if new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks is accompanied by: ~ Thrombocytopenia, renal or hepatic involvement, pulmonary oedema or neurological features ~ Oedema of the ankles ~ Excessive weight gain ~ A raised uric acid alone ~ A ~ The modern definition recognises preeclampsia as a multisystem disorder, so end-organ dysfunction substitutes for proteinuria; oedema and uric acid were dropped as they lack specificity.
E ~ Which drug given from the first trimester reduces the incidence of preterm preeclampsia in high-risk women? ~ Low-dose aspirin started before 16 weeks ~ Calcium supplementation alone in calcium-replete women ~ Magnesium sulphate ~ Vitamin C and E ~ A ~ The ASPRE trial showed aspirin 150 mg at night from 11 to 14 weeks reduced preterm preeclampsia by over 60 percent, and starting before placentation is complete appears necessary for benefit.
E ~ A woman with preeclampsia at 33 weeks has a blood pressure of 168/112 mmHg. Which oral drug is appropriate for acute control? ~ Nifedipine ~ Atenolol ~ Enalapril ~ Sodium nitroprusside ~ A ~ Oral nifedipine, intravenous labetalol and hydralazine are the accepted acute agents; atenolol impairs fetal growth, ACE inhibitors are fetotoxic and nitroprusside risks fetal cyanide accumulation.
M ~ A woman with preeclampsia at 30 weeks has a platelet count of 70000, alanine aminotransferase of 180 U/L and a lactate dehydrogenase of 800 U/L with fragmented red cells. The diagnosis and management are: ~ HELLP syndrome, requiring stabilisation, magnesium sulphate and delivery ~ Acute fatty liver of pregnancy, requiring dextrose ~ Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, requiring steroids ~ Viral hepatitis, requiring supportive care ~ A ~ Haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets constitute a severe variant of preeclampsia in which delivery is the only definitive treatment regardless of gestation.
M ~ Which criterion permits expectant management of severe preeclampsia rather than immediate delivery? ~ Gestation between 24 and 34 weeks with stable maternal condition and reassuring fetal status in a unit able to monitor closely ~ Any gestation if blood pressure is controlled ~ Presence of HELLP syndrome ~ Eclampsia that has been controlled ~ A ~ Expectant management buys fetal maturity but is abandoned for uncontrollable hypertension, eclampsia, HELLP, pulmonary oedema, abruption, renal failure or non-reassuring fetal testing.
M ~ A woman at 32 weeks has continuous abdominal pain, a tense tender uterus, minimal vaginal bleeding and fetal bradycardia. Her fundal height is increasing. The likely diagnosis is: ~ Concealed placental abruption ~ Placenta praevia ~ Uterine rupture ~ Vasa praevia ~ A ~ Retroplacental bleeding may be largely concealed, so revealed blood loss badly underestimates the true deficit; the woody uterus, rising fundal height and fetal compromise are the guides to severity.
M ~ Which coagulation complication most often accompanies severe placental abruption? ~ Disseminated intravascular coagulation from release of thromboplastin ~ Isolated thrombocytopenia ~ Haemophilia-like factor VIII deficiency ~ Vitamin K deficiency ~ A ~ Abruption is the commonest obstetric cause of disseminated intravascular coagulation, so fibrinogen and platelets are checked early and blood products given alongside delivery.
M ~ A woman at 36 weeks has painless bleeding and ultrasound shows the placental edge 1.5 cm from the internal os. The recommended mode of delivery is: ~ Caesarean section, as a placental edge within 2 cm of the os risks major haemorrhage ~ Induction of labour at 37 weeks ~ Await spontaneous labour ~ Vacuum-assisted vaginal delivery ~ A ~ A low-lying placenta within 2 cm of the os is generally delivered abdominally because the lower segment cannot retract around it, and the anterior position with a previous scar further raises the risk of accreta.
H ~ A woman in labour has sudden painless vaginal bleeding immediately after membrane rupture, with acute fetal bradycardia and a sinusoidal trace. The diagnosis and action are: ~ Ruptured vasa praevia, requiring immediate caesarean section ~ Placental abruption, requiring magnesium ~ Cervical tear, requiring suture ~ Placenta praevia, requiring tocolysis ~ A ~ The blood lost is fetal, so a small volume is rapidly fatal to the fetus; survival depends on delivery within minutes, which is why antenatal detection in a woman with a velamentous cord or succenturiate lobe matters so much.
H ~ Magnesium sulphate given for severe preeclampsia acts to prevent eclampsia chiefly by: ~ Cerebral vasodilatation with membrane stabilisation and NMDA receptor antagonism, raising the seizure threshold ~ Lowering blood pressure ~ Increasing urine output ~ Reducing proteinuria ~ A ~ Magnesium is an anticonvulsant rather than an antihypertensive, so blood pressure must be treated separately, and the MAGPIE trial confirmed it halves the risk of eclampsia.
H ~ What is the pathological hallmark of preeclampsia that explains its multisystem features? ~ Failure of the second wave of trophoblast invasion leaving the spiral arteries narrow, causing placental ischaemia and release of antiangiogenic factors ~ Excess placental growth factor ~ Immune tolerance to paternal antigen ~ Increased nitric oxide production ~ A ~ Incomplete remodelling of the spiral arteries produces a hypoperfused placenta that releases soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1 and soluble endoglin, which antagonise vascular endothelial growth factor and placental growth factor and cause systemic endothelial dysfunction.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Preterm labour, PROM & post-term pregnancy ~ OBG_PTL ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Preterm Labour and Prolonged Pregnancy
E ~ Antenatal corticosteroids given to a woman at risk of preterm birth reduce which outcomes most? ~ Respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular haemorrhage, necrotising enterocolitis and neonatal death ~ Only respiratory distress syndrome ~ Only neonatal jaundice ~ Maternal infection ~ A ~ A single course between 24 and 34 weeks, ideally 24 to 48 hours before delivery, produces benefits across several organ systems, which is why it is one of the highest-value interventions in obstetrics.
E ~ Magnesium sulphate given to a woman in established preterm labour before 32 weeks is intended to: ~ Provide fetal neuroprotection, reducing cerebral palsy ~ Act as the primary tocolytic ~ Prevent maternal seizures ~ Accelerate lung maturity ~ A ~ Meta-analyses show a reduction in cerebral palsy and gross motor dysfunction, and this indication is separate from its use in preeclampsia and from tocolysis.
E ~ Which test on vaginal fluid supports the diagnosis of preterm rupture of membranes when the history is equivocal? ~ Insulin-like growth factor binding protein-1 or placental alpha microglobulin-1 ~ Whiff test ~ Vaginal pH alone ~ Urine dipstick for protein ~ A ~ These biochemical markers are present in amniotic fluid at high concentration and have better specificity than nitrazine testing, which is confounded by blood, semen and bacterial vaginosis.
M ~ A woman has preterm prelabour rupture of membranes at 29 weeks with no signs of infection. Management includes: ~ Antibiotics such as erythromycin, corticosteroids and expectant management with surveillance for chorioamnionitis ~ Immediate delivery ~ Tocolysis until 37 weeks ~ Amnioinfusion ~ A ~ The ORACLE trial showed erythromycin prolongs pregnancy and reduces neonatal morbidity; co-amoxiclav is avoided because it increased necrotising enterocolitis.
M ~ Which finding indicates chorioamnionitis and mandates delivery regardless of gestation? ~ Maternal fever with uterine tenderness, fetal tachycardia and offensive liquor ~ A rising white cell count alone after steroids ~ Reduced amniotic fluid index ~ Braxton Hicks contractions ~ A ~ Steroids raise the white cell count so it cannot be used alone; clinical chorioamnionitis threatens both maternal sepsis and fetal inflammatory injury, so antibiotics and delivery are required.
M ~ Which tocolytic is preferred in preterm labour because of a better maternal side-effect profile than beta agonists? ~ Nifedipine or atosiban ~ Ritodrine ~ Salbutamol infusion ~ Ethanol ~ A ~ Beta agonists cause tachycardia, hyperglycaemia and pulmonary oedema; calcium channel blockade or oxytocin receptor antagonism achieves comparable delay with fewer adverse effects, and tocolysis is used mainly to allow steroids and transfer.
M ~ A woman is at 41 weeks and 5 days with a favourable cervix and a normal fetal assessment. The recommended management is: ~ Offer induction of labour, as continuing beyond 42 weeks increases perinatal mortality ~ Wait until 43 weeks ~ Immediate caesarean section ~ Weekly assessment with no intervention ~ A ~ Placental function declines and the risks of meconium aspiration, oligohydramnios and stillbirth rise beyond term, so induction between 41 and 42 weeks reduces perinatal death without increasing caesarean rates.
H ~ Which fetal complication is characteristic of true post-term pregnancy with placental insufficiency? ~ Dysmaturity with loss of subcutaneous fat, meconium staining and long peeling nails ~ Macrosomia in every case ~ Hydrops fetalis ~ Neonatal polycythaemia alone ~ A ~ The postmaturity or Ballantyne syndrome reflects chronic undernutrition, and these babies are at particular risk of meconium aspiration, oligohydramnios-related cord compression and neonatal hypoglycaemia.
H ~ Why does a short cervix on transvaginal ultrasound predict preterm birth better than digital examination? ~ It measures the closed functional length objectively and detects funnelling before any external change ~ It detects infection ~ It measures fetal fibronectin ~ It assesses uterine contractility ~ A ~ Cervical length below 25 mm before 24 weeks identifies women who benefit from vaginal progesterone or cerclage, and the measurement is reproducible in a way that digital assessment is not.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Prolapse, urogynaecology & genital fistula ~ OBG_PRO ~ Shaw's Textbook of Gynaecology 17e, Ch. Genital Prolapse and Urogynaecology
E ~ Which ligament provides the main support to the uterine cervix and upper vagina, and its weakness causes uterine descent? ~ Cardinal (Mackenrodt) and uterosacral ligaments ~ Round ligament ~ Broad ligament ~ Ovarian ligament ~ A ~ Level I support by the cardinal and uterosacral complex suspends the cervix and upper vagina; the round and broad ligaments contribute almost nothing to support, which is why round ligament plication fails.
E ~ In the POP-Q system, a point Ba at plus 3 cm indicates: ~ The anterior vaginal wall descending 3 cm beyond the hymen ~ A normal anterior wall ~ Descent of the cervix ~ Enterocele ~ A ~ The system uses the hymen as the fixed reference with negative values above and positive below, which makes prolapse staging reproducible between examiners and over time.
E ~ Which type of prolapse presents with difficulty in defaecation requiring digital pressure on the posterior vaginal wall? ~ Rectocele ~ Cystocele ~ Enterocele ~ Uterine descent ~ A ~ A posterior wall defect allows the rectum to bulge forward and trap stool, so patients learn to splint the perineum; an enterocele instead produces a dragging sensation and may contain small bowel.
M ~ A 40-year-old woman with symptomatic second-degree uterine prolapse wishes to preserve her uterus and future fertility. The appropriate surgery is: ~ Sling or sacrohysteropexy, or a Manchester-Fothergill type repair depending on cervical elongation ~ Vaginal hysterectomy with repair ~ Le Fort colpocleisis ~ Anterior colporrhaphy alone ~ A ~ Uterus-preserving procedures maintain fertility, whereas colpocleisis obliterates the vagina and is reserved for frail elderly women who are not sexually active.
M ~ Which investigation distinguishes stress urinary incontinence from detrusor overactivity when symptoms overlap? ~ Multichannel urodynamics ~ Mid-stream urine culture ~ Post-void residual volume ~ Cystoscopy ~ A ~ Urodynamics demonstrates leakage with raised abdominal pressure and no detrusor contraction in stress incontinence, whereas detrusor overactivity shows uninhibited contractions during filling, and the treatments differ entirely.
M ~ First-line management of stress urinary incontinence is: ~ Supervised pelvic floor muscle training for at least 3 months ~ Mid-urethral sling ~ Anticholinergic drugs ~ Sacral neuromodulation ~ A ~ Conservative treatment cures or improves a substantial proportion at no surgical risk; anticholinergics treat urgency rather than stress leakage, and surgery follows failed physiotherapy.
M ~ A woman develops continuous urinary leakage 10 days after an abdominal hysterectomy, and a three-swab test stains the upper swab. The likely lesion is: ~ Vesicovaginal fistula at the vault ~ Ureterovaginal fistula ~ Urethrovaginal fistula ~ Stress incontinence ~ A ~ Staining of the upper swab with methylene blue instilled in the bladder localises a vesicovaginal fistula; if the upper swab is wet but unstained the leak is ureteric, which is confirmed by intravenous dye.
H ~ Which surgical principle governs timing of repair of an obstetric vesicovaginal fistula? ~ Repair after about 3 months when tissue inflammation and slough have resolved, unless it is a fresh surgical injury which may be repaired immediately ~ Repair within 48 hours in all cases ~ Repair only after 2 years ~ Repair is never possible ~ A ~ Operating on oedematous infected tissue guarantees failure, so continuous bladder drainage, nutrition and skin care precede repair; a clean surgical injury recognised at once is different and can be closed primarily.
H ~ A woman has recurrent vault prolapse after vaginal hysterectomy. Which procedure has the highest anatomical success rate? ~ Abdominal sacrocolpopexy ~ Anterior colporrhaphy ~ Posterior colporrhaphy ~ Vaginal pessary alone ~ A ~ Suspending the vault to the sacral promontory with mesh gives the most durable apical support, and failure to address the apex is the commonest reason isolated anterior or posterior repairs recur.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Puberty, amenorrhoea & abnormal uterine bleeding ~ OBG_AUB ~ Shaw's Textbook of Gynaecology 17e, Ch. Puberty and Amenorrhoea
E ~ What is the normal sequence of pubertal events in a girl? ~ Breast budding, then pubic hair, then growth spurt, then menarche ~ Menarche, then breast budding, then pubic hair ~ Pubic hair, then menarche, then breast budding ~ Growth spurt, then menarche, then breast budding ~ A ~ Thelarche is the first sign under ovarian oestrogen, and menarche follows about 2 to 2.5 years later; deviation from this order suggests an androgen excess or a disorder of sexual development.
E ~ A 16-year-old has never menstruated, has normal breasts and pubic hair but a blind vaginal pouch with an absent uterus on ultrasound, and a 46,XX karyotype. The diagnosis is: ~ Mullerian agenesis (Mayer-Rokitansky-Kuster-Hauser syndrome) ~ Androgen insensitivity syndrome ~ Turner syndrome ~ Imperforate hymen ~ A ~ Ovaries are present and functioning so secondary sexual characteristics are normal, and associated renal and skeletal anomalies should be sought; androgen insensitivity would show a 46,XY karyotype with absent pubic hair.
E ~ A 15-year-old has cyclical lower abdominal pain with a bulging bluish membrane at the introitus and a pelvic mass. The management is: ~ Cruciate incision of the imperforate hymen with drainage of the haematocolpos ~ Hormonal suppression ~ Hysterectomy ~ Observation ~ A ~ Obstruction to menstrual outflow with a normal upper tract produces cryptomenorrhoea, and simple incision restores drainage and relieves the pain and mass.
M ~ A 17-year-old with primary amenorrhoea has a height of 142 cm, a webbed neck, widely spaced nipples and streak gonads. The expected karyotype and a key complication to exclude are: ~ 45,X with coarctation of the aorta and bicuspid aortic valve ~ 46,XY with gonadal malignancy ~ 47,XXX with learning difficulty ~ 46,XX with renal agenesis ~ A ~ Turner syndrome requires cardiac imaging because aortic dissection is a leading cause of death, alongside growth hormone therapy and later oestrogen replacement.
M ~ A woman with secondary amenorrhoea after a puerperal curettage fails to bleed after a progestogen challenge and again fails to bleed after sequential oestrogen followed by progestogen. Gonadotropins are normal. The likely diagnosis is: ~ Asherman syndrome with intrauterine adhesions ~ Premature ovarian insufficiency ~ Hyperprolactinaemia ~ Polycystic ovary syndrome ~ A ~ Failure to bleed even after adequate oestrogen priming localises the problem to the endometrium rather than the ovary or pituitary, and normal gonadotropins exclude ovarian failure; hysteroscopy confirms adhesions and permits adhesiolysis with a barrier to prevent re-formation.
M ~ Which endocrine pattern indicates hypogonadotropic hypogonadism as the cause of amenorrhoea? ~ Low follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinising hormone with low oestradiol ~ High follicle-stimulating hormone with low oestradiol ~ High luteinising hormone to follicle-stimulating hormone ratio ~ High prolactin with normal gonadotropins ~ A ~ Low gonadotropins point to a hypothalamic or pituitary cause such as weight loss, exercise, stress, Kallmann syndrome or a tumour, and treatment addresses the cause rather than the ovary.
M ~ A 45-year-old with heavy irregular bleeding has an endometrial thickness of 14 mm on ultrasound. The essential next step is: ~ Endometrial sampling to exclude hyperplasia or carcinoma ~ Start tranexamic acid ~ Insert a levonorgestrel intrauterine system ~ Reassure and review in 6 months ~ A ~ Structural and premalignant causes must be excluded before symptomatic treatment in a woman over 40 or with risk factors, because starting hormonal therapy could mask a carcinoma.
H ~ Which non-hormonal drug reduces menstrual blood loss most in ovulatory heavy menstrual bleeding? ~ Tranexamic acid ~ Mefenamic acid ~ Ethamsylate ~ Vitamin K ~ A ~ Antifibrinolysis reduces loss by about 50 percent compared with roughly 25 percent for a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, and neither is contraceptive, which suits women trying to conceive.
H ~ A girl of 6 has breast development, pubic hair and vaginal bleeding, with an advanced bone age and a raised luteinising hormone response to gonadotropin-releasing hormone. The diagnosis and treatment are: ~ Central precocious puberty, treated with a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist to preserve final height ~ Peripheral precocious puberty, treated with an aromatase inhibitor ~ Premature thelarche, needing observation ~ McCune-Albright syndrome, treated with surgery ~ A ~ A pubertal gonadotropin response confirms activation of the hypothalamic axis, and continuous agonist therapy paradoxically suppresses it by downregulating receptors, delaying epiphyseal fusion.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Puerperium, lactation & puerperal sepsis ~ OBG_PUE ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. The Puerperium
E ~ By what time after delivery does the uterus normally become impalpable abdominally? ~ About 2 weeks ~ 24 hours ~ 6 weeks ~ 3 days ~ A ~ Involution proceeds at about 1 to 1.5 cm per day, so the fundus is at the umbilicus immediately after delivery and no longer palpable per abdomen by roughly the second week, reaching non-pregnant size by 6 weeks.
E ~ Which hormone change initiates copious milk secretion after delivery? ~ The abrupt fall in progesterone with maintained prolactin ~ A rise in progesterone ~ A fall in prolactin ~ A rise in oestrogen ~ A ~ High placental progesterone blocks the lactogenic action of prolactin during pregnancy, so its withdrawal at delivery permits lactogenesis II, and retained placental fragments can delay milk coming in.
E ~ Which reflex is responsible for milk ejection and is inhibited by maternal anxiety and pain? ~ Oxytocin-mediated let-down reflex ~ Prolactin reflex ~ Ferguson reflex ~ Rooting reflex ~ A ~ Suckling stimulates oxytocin release which contracts myoepithelial cells, and because the pathway is neuroendocrine it is readily suppressed by catecholamines, so privacy and analgesia support breastfeeding.
M ~ A woman 4 days postpartum has a wedge-shaped tender erythematous area in one breast with fever of 39 degrees. The management is: ~ Continue breastfeeding or expressing, with flucloxacillin and analgesia ~ Stop breastfeeding from that breast permanently ~ Incise and drain immediately ~ Give a dopamine agonist to suppress lactation ~ A ~ Effective milk drainage is central to resolving mastitis, and stopping feeding causes stasis that promotes abscess formation; abscess is suspected if a fluctuant mass persists and needs aspiration or drainage.
M ~ Which organism most commonly causes puerperal sepsis with a rapidly progressive course and can cause toxic shock? ~ Group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes) ~ Candida albicans ~ Lactobacillus ~ Staphylococcus epidermidis ~ A ~ Group A streptococcal sepsis remains a leading cause of direct maternal death, progressing within hours, so persistent tachycardia and pain out of proportion to signs must prompt urgent broad-spectrum antibiotics and senior review.
M ~ Which finding suggests septic pelvic thrombophlebitis rather than simple endometritis in a woman with persistent puerperal fever? ~ Persistent fever despite appropriate antibiotics with a normal pelvic examination, responding to anticoagulation ~ Offensive lochia ~ A tender bulky uterus ~ Wound discharge ~ A ~ It is a diagnosis of exclusion, sometimes with thrombus seen on CT, and the classic teaching is a prompt lysis of fever after starting heparin.
M ~ A woman is exclusively breastfeeding and 4 weeks postpartum requests contraception. Which method is appropriate? ~ Progestogen-only pill or an implant, as they do not affect milk supply ~ Combined oral contraceptive pill ~ Nothing is needed while breastfeeding ~ Immediate sterilisation ~ A ~ Oestrogen can reduce milk volume and adds thrombotic risk in the early puerperium, so progestogen-only methods, the copper device or the levonorgestrel system are preferred.
H ~ Which condition presents in the first 2 weeks postpartum with confusion, delusions about the baby and severe mood disturbance, and is a psychiatric emergency? ~ Puerperal psychosis ~ Postnatal depression ~ Baby blues ~ Postpartum thyroiditis ~ A ~ It affects about 1 to 2 per 1000 deliveries, carries a real risk of suicide and infanticide, requires admission ideally to a mother and baby unit, and has a high recurrence rate in subsequent pregnancies.
H ~ Which factor most reliably distinguishes physiological postpartum blues from postnatal depression? ~ Duration, as blues resolve within about 10 days whereas depression persists beyond 2 weeks with functional impairment ~ Presence of tearfulness ~ Sleep disturbance ~ Anxiety about the baby ~ A ~ Because the symptoms overlap, the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale and the time course are used, and depression requires active treatment while blues need support and reassurance.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Third-stage complications, retained placenta & uterine inversion ~ OBG_THI ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Complications of the Third Stage of Labour
E ~ The third stage of labour is considered prolonged, and the placenta retained, if it is undelivered after: ~ 30 minutes with active management ~ 5 minutes ~ 2 hours ~ 24 hours ~ A ~ With active management the threshold is 30 minutes and with physiological management 60 minutes, after which manual removal is arranged because the risk of haemorrhage rises steeply.
E ~ Which sign indicates that the placenta has separated and is ready to be delivered? ~ Lengthening of the cord with a suprapubic bulge and the uterus becoming firm and globular ~ Gush of blood alone ~ Maternal urge to push ~ Rising fundal height ~ A ~ Cord lengthening and the change in uterine shape are the reliable signs, and controlled cord traction should only be applied with counter-pressure once separation has occurred.
E ~ Manual removal of the placenta requires which essential precondition? ~ Adequate anaesthesia with aseptic technique and intravenous access, and antibiotic cover ~ No anaesthesia is needed ~ Ergometrine given first to contract the uterus ~ The bladder to be full ~ A ~ Uterine relaxation is needed to admit the hand, so a contracting agent before removal makes the procedure difficult and risks perforation; regional or general anaesthesia is standard.
M ~ A woman has a retained placenta and attempts at manual removal find no cleavage plane between placenta and myometrium. The diagnosis and correct action are: ~ Placenta accreta, so stop, leave the placenta, arrange senior help and prepare for hysterectomy or conservative management ~ Continue forceful removal ~ Use a curette vigorously ~ Give more oxytocin and re-attempt ~ A ~ Persisting with removal of a morbidly adherent placenta causes catastrophic haemorrhage, and recognising the absent plane and stopping is the single most important decision.
M ~ Immediately after delivery of the placenta a woman becomes profoundly shocked with severe lower abdominal pain, and no fundus is palpable abdominally while a mass fills the vagina. The diagnosis is: ~ Acute uterine inversion ~ Uterine rupture ~ Atonic postpartum haemorrhage ~ Vulval haematoma ~ A ~ The degree of shock is often out of proportion to the blood loss because of vagal stimulation from traction on the ligaments and ovaries, and recognition depends on noting the absent fundus.
M ~ The immediate management of acute uterine inversion is: ~ Immediate manual replacement before the cervix contracts, with resuscitation, and stopping any uterotonic ~ Give ergometrine at once ~ Pack the vagina ~ Proceed directly to hysterectomy ~ A ~ Replacement becomes progressively harder as the cervical ring clamps down, so the fundus is pushed back at once; uterotonics are withheld until the uterus is replaced and then given to maintain it.
M ~ Which hydrostatic method is used for uterine inversion when manual replacement fails? ~ O'Sullivan hydrostatic method using warm saline instilled into the vagina ~ Bakri balloon inflation ~ Vaginal packing with gauze ~ Cervical cerclage ~ A ~ The instilled fluid distends the vagina and its pressure pushes the fundus back, with the operator sealing the introitus; surgical approaches such as the Haultain or Huntington procedures follow if this fails.
H ~ Which manoeuvre is used for a partially separated but retained placenta before proceeding to manual removal? ~ Brandt-Andrews manoeuvre of controlled cord traction with suprapubic counter-pressure ~ Credé manoeuvre with fundal squeezing ~ Kristeller manoeuvre ~ Ritgen manoeuvre ~ A ~ The Brandt-Andrews technique guards against inversion by supporting the uterus, whereas the Credé method of squeezing the fundus risks inversion and incomplete removal and is no longer recommended.
H ~ Which finding on antenatal ultrasound should prompt planning for possible placenta accreta spectrum at delivery? ~ An anterior placenta praevia overlying a previous caesarean scar with loss of the retroplacental clear zone and lacunae ~ A posterior fundal placenta ~ A two-vessel cord ~ Polyhydramnios ~ A ~ Antenatal recognition allows delivery in a centre with blood bank, interventional radiology and surgical expertise, which is the main determinant of outcome in accreta.
