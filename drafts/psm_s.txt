@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Nutrition programmes, food fortification & food hygiene ~ PSM_NUT ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Nutrition and Health
E ~ Which staple is fortified with iron, folic acid and vitamin B12 under India's fortification standards to address anaemia? ~ Wheat flour and rice ~ Sugar ~ Tea ~ Pulses ~ A ~ Salt is fortified with iodine and iron, edible oil and milk with vitamins A and D, and rice fortification through the public distribution system is being scaled up because staples reach the whole population without behaviour change.
E ~ Which programme provides a hot cooked meal to school children to improve enrolment, attendance and nutrition? ~ PM POSHAN, formerly the Mid-Day Meal Scheme ~ Integrated Child Development Services ~ Anaemia Mukt Bharat ~ National Deworming Day ~ A ~ Beyond nutrition it addresses classroom hunger and social equity through shared eating, and it is one of the largest school feeding programmes in the world.
E ~ Which food adulterant of mustard oil causes epidemic dropsy with oedema, glaucoma and cardiac failure? ~ Argemone mexicana seed oil ~ Lathyrus sativus ~ Aflatoxin ~ Metanil yellow ~ A ~ Sanguinarine is the toxic alkaloid, detected by the nitric acid or paper chromatography test, whereas Lathyrus causes neurolathyrism and aflatoxin causes hepatotoxicity and hepatocellular carcinoma.
M ~ Which nutrition programme component targets severe acute malnutrition at the facility level? ~ Nutrition rehabilitation centres providing therapeutic feeding with medical care ~ Anganwadi supplementary nutrition ~ School mid-day meals ~ Take-home rations ~ A ~ Children with complications or failed appetite test need inpatient care with cautious refeeding, whereas uncomplicated cases are managed in the community with ready-to-use therapeutic food.
M ~ Which anthropometric criterion defines severe acute malnutrition in a child of 6 to 59 months? ~ Weight for height below minus 3 standard deviations, mid-upper arm circumference below 11.5 cm, or bilateral pitting oedema ~ Weight for age below the third centile ~ Height for age below minus 2 standard deviations ~ Body mass index below 15 ~ A ~ Weight for height and arm circumference measure acute wasting, whereas height for age measures chronic stunting, and either criterion or oedema alone qualifies a child for therapeutic feeding.
M ~ Which principle governs the first phase of feeding a child with severe acute malnutrition and oedema? ~ Cautious low-protein, low-sodium feeding with F-75 to stabilise before catch-up growth, to avoid refeeding syndrome and heart failure ~ Immediate high-protein high-calorie feeding ~ Intravenous nutrition ~ Withholding feeds for 24 hours ~ A ~ The ten steps of WHO management treat hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, dehydration, electrolytes and infection first, and only in the rehabilitation phase is F-100 or ready-to-use therapeutic food used for catch-up.
H ~ Why does universal salt iodisation remain necessary in a country that has eliminated goitre as a public health problem? ~ Iodine deficiency recurs quickly if fortification lapses, and its most important effect is on fetal brain development rather than goitre ~ Goitre is the only consequence ~ Iodine is stored for years in the body ~ Dietary iodine has increased naturally ~ A ~ The most consequential outcome is preventable intellectual impairment, which is invisible at the individual level, so monitoring of salt iodine content and urinary iodine concentration must continue indefinitely.
H ~ Why is stunting a more informative indicator of population nutrition than underweight? ~ It reflects chronic cumulative deprivation including maternal nutrition, infection and care, and predicts cognitive and economic outcomes ~ It is easier to measure ~ It responds faster to intervention ~ It excludes genetic factors ~ A ~ Because linear growth failure is largely established by 2 years and is not reversed later, the first thousand days from conception has become the focus of nutrition policy.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Nutritional requirements & deficiency diseases ~ PSM_NRD ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Nutrition and Health
E ~ Which term describes the average daily intake sufficient to meet the requirement of nearly all healthy individuals in a group? ~ Recommended dietary allowance ~ Estimated average requirement ~ Tolerable upper intake level ~ Adequate intake ~ A ~ The allowance is set two standard deviations above the estimated average requirement so that it covers 97.5 percent of the group, which is why it is used for planning diets rather than for assessing an individual.
M ~ Which measure describes the proportion of dietary protein retained for growth and maintenance and is highest for egg? ~ Net protein utilisation, related to biological value ~ Protein energy ratio ~ Nitrogen balance ~ Digestibility coefficient ~ A ~ Cereal proteins are limiting in lysine and pulse proteins in methionine, so combining them raises the overall quality, which is the nutritional basis of the traditional cereal and pulse diet.
M ~ Which deficiency causes angular stomatitis, cheilosis, magenta tongue and a normocytic anaemia? ~ Riboflavin deficiency ~ Thiamine deficiency ~ Niacin deficiency ~ Pyridoxine deficiency ~ A ~ Riboflavin deficiency is common in populations with milk-poor diets, and it also impairs the conversion of tryptophan to niacin and of pyridoxine to its active form, so deficiencies cluster.
H ~ Why does a diet based largely on polished rice predispose to beriberi? ~ Thiamine is concentrated in the bran and germ, which are removed by polishing, while the carbohydrate load raises thiamine requirement ~ Rice contains a thiamine antagonist ~ Rice is low in protein ~ Cooking destroys all thiamine ~ A ~ Parboiling before milling drives thiamine into the endosperm and protects against deficiency, which is why parboiled rice-eating populations have lower rates of beriberi.
H ~ Why is a diet adequate in total calories still capable of producing kwashiorkor? ~ A high-carbohydrate low-protein diet gives adequate energy but insufficient protein, so hypoalbuminaemia and oedema develop ~ Calories are never adequate in kwashiorkor ~ Kwashiorkor requires vitamin deficiency ~ Protein is stored indefinitely ~ A ~ The classic setting is a child weaned onto a starchy staple after the birth of a sibling, and the oedema masks weight loss so weight for height can appear misleadingly adequate.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Occupational health & industrial diseases ~ PSM_OCC ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Occupational Health
E ~ Which pneumoconiosis carries a markedly increased risk of tuberculosis and is caused by inhalation of free crystalline silica? ~ Silicosis ~ Asbestosis ~ Byssinosis ~ Bagassosis ~ A ~ Silica impairs macrophage function, so silicotuberculosis is common, and eggshell calcification of hilar nodes with upper-zone nodular fibrosis is characteristic on the chest radiograph.
E ~ Which occupational lung disease is caused by inhalation of cotton dust and characteristically improves as the working week continues? ~ Byssinosis, with Monday morning chest tightness ~ Farmer's lung ~ Silicosis ~ Asbestosis ~ A ~ The Monday fever pattern reflects tachyphylaxis to the dust, and progression to irreversible airflow obstruction occurs with continued exposure, so dust control and periodic spirometry are needed.
E ~ Which malignancy is specifically associated with asbestos exposure and can follow even brief exposure decades earlier? ~ Malignant mesothelioma of the pleura or peritoneum ~ Small cell carcinoma of the lung only ~ Renal cell carcinoma ~ Osteosarcoma ~ A ~ Asbestos also multiplies the risk of bronchogenic carcinoma synergistically with smoking, and the long latency of 20 to 40 years means cases continue long after exposure ceases.
M ~ Which biological monitoring test is used for occupational lead exposure? ~ Blood lead level, with zinc protoporphyrin or urinary delta-aminolaevulinic acid as supporting tests ~ Serum ferritin ~ Urinary mercury ~ Serum calcium ~ A ~ Lead inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, so precursors accumulate, and clinical features include basophilic stippling, a punctate blue gum line, colic, wrist drop and encephalopathy in children.
M ~ Under the Factories Act, which statutory provision requires the reporting and compensation of a defined list of occupational illnesses? ~ Notification of notifiable occupational diseases with compensation under the Employees Compensation Act ~ Voluntary reporting by the employer ~ Reporting only of fatal accidents ~ No statutory provision exists ~ A ~ The notification and compensation framework, together with pre-placement and periodic medical examination, forms the legal backbone of occupational health practice in India.
M ~ Which measure sits highest in the hierarchy of control for a workplace hazard? ~ Elimination or substitution of the hazardous agent ~ Personal protective equipment ~ Administrative rotation of workers ~ Health surveillance ~ A ~ The hierarchy runs elimination, substitution, engineering control, administrative control and finally personal protection, and reliance on protective equipment alone is the least effective because it depends on individual compliance.
H ~ Why is the sick worker or healthy worker effect important when interpreting mortality studies in an occupational cohort? ~ Employed populations are healthier than the general population, so comparing them to national rates underestimates occupational risk ~ Workers are always sicker ~ It only affects incidence studies ~ It is a form of confounding by age ~ A ~ Internal comparisons between exposure levels within the cohort, or comparison with another working population, avoid the bias that arises because entering and remaining in employment requires a degree of health.
H ~ Why does a threshold limit value not guarantee that no worker will be harmed? ~ It is based on average responses and does not account for individual susceptibility, mixed exposures or non-threshold carcinogens ~ It is legally unenforceable ~ It applies only to gases ~ It is measured only annually ~ A ~ For genotoxic carcinogens no safe threshold is assumed, so exposure is reduced as low as reasonably achievable rather than merely kept below a numerical limit.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Respiratory & vaccine-preventable disease epidemiology (measles, diphtheria, polio) ~ PSM_VPD ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Epidemiology of Communicable Diseases
E ~ Which criterion must be met before a country can be certified free of wild poliovirus transmission? ~ Three consecutive years of no wild virus with certification-standard acute flaccid paralysis surveillance ~ One year with no cases ~ Ninety percent immunisation coverage ~ Absence of imported cases only ~ A ~ Surveillance quality is as important as case absence, so non-polio acute flaccid paralysis rate and stool adequacy indicators must meet targets for the silence to be believable.
E ~ Which surveillance activity is the cornerstone of polio eradication? ~ Acute flaccid paralysis surveillance with stool testing of every case under 15 years ~ Serological survey of school children ~ Sentinel hospital reporting of paralysis only ~ Environmental sampling alone ~ A ~ Environmental surveillance of sewage supplements it and can detect circulation before a paralytic case occurs, which has repeatedly triggered response campaigns.
E ~ What is the basic reproduction number of measles, and what immunisation coverage does it imply? ~ About 12 to 18, requiring roughly 92 to 95 percent immunity for elimination ~ About 2, requiring 50 percent ~ About 5, requiring 80 percent ~ About 30, requiring 99 percent ~ A ~ Because measles is among the most transmissible human infections, even small pockets of unimmunised children sustain outbreaks, which is why two doses and outbreak response immunisation are needed.
M ~ Which case definition is used for measles surveillance in the elimination phase? ~ Any person with fever and maculopapular rash with cough, coryza or conjunctivitis, investigated with serology or PCR ~ Rash with Koplik spots only ~ Laboratory-confirmed cases only ~ Fever with any rash in a child under 5 ~ A ~ A sensitive clinical definition with laboratory confirmation of every suspected case and genotyping to trace transmission chains is what distinguishes elimination-standard surveillance from routine reporting.
M ~ Why has diphtheria re-emerged in adolescents and adults in some Indian states despite high infant coverage? ~ Waning immunity without booster doses leaves older age groups susceptible ~ The vaccine has lost potency ~ A new toxigenic strain has appeared ~ Infant coverage has fallen ~ A ~ This is the rationale for the tetanus and adult diphtheria booster in adolescence, and outbreak investigation typically finds cases clustering in under-immunised or unboosted older children.
M ~ Which measure is used to control a diphtheria outbreak in a community? ~ Case isolation with antitoxin and antibiotics, contact tracing with prophylaxis and immunisation, and a catch-up campaign ~ Antitoxin for all contacts ~ Immunisation of cases only ~ School closure alone ~ A ~ Antitoxin neutralises circulating toxin in the case and must be given on clinical suspicion without waiting for culture, while contacts receive antibiotics, swabs and vaccine according to their immunisation status.
H ~ Why does an outbreak of measles occurring in a highly immunised population not by itself indicate vaccine failure? ~ When most of the population is immunised, most cases can occur in vaccinated people even though their risk is far lower, so attack rates must be compared by vaccination status ~ Vaccinated cases are always misdiagnosed ~ Serology is unreliable ~ Outbreaks cannot occur above 90 percent coverage ~ A ~ Calculating vaccine effectiveness from the attack rates in vaccinated and unvaccinated groups resolves this apparent paradox, which is a recurring source of public misunderstanding.
H ~ Why is the switch from trivalent to bivalent oral polio vaccine combined with introducing inactivated polio vaccine? ~ Withdrawing type 2 removed most vaccine-derived paralysis risk, and inactivated vaccine maintains type 2 immunity in case of re-emergence ~ Inactivated vaccine is cheaper ~ Bivalent vaccine covers all three types ~ Type 2 wild virus is still circulating ~ A ~ Type 2 accounted for most circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus, so its removal from the oral vaccine reduced that risk while at least one dose of inactivated vaccine preserved population immunity against it.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Study designs; incidence vs prevalence ~ PSM_SDI ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Epidemiology
M ~ A survey measures the number of people with diabetes in a town on a single day. Which measure has been obtained, and what determines it? ~ Point prevalence, determined by incidence and the average duration of the disease ~ Incidence rate, determined by new cases only ~ Cumulative incidence ~ Attack rate ~ A ~ Because prevalence approximates incidence multiplied by duration, a treatment that prolongs life without preventing disease will raise prevalence while incidence falls, which is a common source of misinterpretation.
H ~ Which design is best suited to studying a rapidly fatal disease with a very short duration, and why? ~ A cohort or surveillance design measuring incidence, because prevalence would be misleadingly low ~ A cross-sectional prevalence survey ~ A case-control study ~ An ecological study ~ A ~ Short duration keeps prevalence low regardless of how common the disease is, so incidence and case fatality are the informative measures and prevalence surveys will systematically understate the burden.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Vector-borne disease: dengue, chikungunya, filaria, kala-azar ~ PSM_VBD ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Epidemiology of Communicable Diseases
E ~ Which vector control measure is the mainstay against Aedes aegypti in an urban dengue outbreak? ~ Source reduction by eliminating domestic and peridomestic water-holding containers, with community participation ~ Indoor residual spraying ~ Long-lasting insecticidal nets ~ Larvivorous fish in rivers ~ A ~ Because the vector breeds in clean stored water inside and around homes and bites by day, nets and residual spraying are far less effective than removing breeding sites.
E ~ Which vector transmits kala-azar in India, and what is the principal control measure? ~ Phlebotomus argentipes, controlled by indoor residual spraying of dwellings and cattle sheds ~ Culex quinquefasciatus, controlled by larvicides ~ Aedes aegypti, controlled by source reduction ~ Anopheles stephensi, controlled by nets ~ A ~ The sandfly rests in cracks in mud walls and breeds in damp organic soil, so residual spraying with early diagnosis and complete treatment of cases is the elimination strategy in the Indian subcontinent.
E ~ Which drug regimen is used for mass drug administration in India's lymphatic filariasis elimination programme? ~ Annual single-dose diethylcarbamazine with albendazole, moving to triple-drug therapy with ivermectin in selected districts ~ Praziquantel alone ~ Chloroquine with primaquine ~ Rifampicin with dapsone ~ A ~ Mass treatment suppresses microfilaraemia below the level needed for transmission, and morbidity management with hygiene for lymphoedema and hydrocele surgery addresses existing disease.
M ~ Which entomological index is used to assess the risk of dengue transmission by measuring the proportion of water containers infested with larvae? ~ Container index, alongside the house index and Breteau index ~ Annual parasite index ~ Slide positivity rate ~ Man-hour density ~ A ~ The house index gives the percentage of houses infested and the Breteau index the number of positive containers per 100 houses, and the Breteau index is generally considered the most informative of the three.
M ~ Which strategy underlies the elimination of kala-azar as a public health problem, and what is the target threshold? ~ Fewer than one case per 10000 population at block level, achieved through active case detection, treatment and vector control ~ Zero cases nationally ~ Fewer than one case per 1000 at district level ~ Vaccination of the population ~ A ~ Single-dose liposomal amphotericin B and miltefosine shortened treatment and improved adherence, and post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis must also be detected and treated because it is a human reservoir.
M ~ Which entomological measure indicates the effectiveness of indoor residual spraying against Anopheles? ~ Fall in indoor resting density measured as man-hour density, with susceptibility testing for insecticide resistance ~ Rise in the container index ~ Fall in the slide positivity rate alone ~ Increase in the annual blood examination rate ~ A ~ Because residual spraying acts on the resting adult female, entomological monitoring detects failure from poor coverage, wall plastering or resistance before the parasitological indices deteriorate.
H ~ Why does chikungunya cause explosive outbreaks with very high attack rates but rarely recurs in the same population soon after? ~ Infection confers durable immunity and there is a single serotype, so the susceptible pool is exhausted ~ The vector disappears ~ The virus mutates rapidly ~ Cross-immunity with dengue develops ~ A ~ This contrasts with dengue, where four serotypes allow repeated epidemics and where secondary infection with a different serotype carries a higher risk of severe disease.
H ~ Why is integrated vector management preferred to reliance on a single insecticide? ~ It combines environmental, biological, chemical and personal measures, delaying resistance and suiting local vector behaviour ~ Insecticides are ineffective ~ It is cheaper in every setting ~ It removes the need for surveillance ~ A ~ Rotating insecticide classes based on susceptibility data, using larval source management and engaging the community together achieve more durable suppression than any single tool.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Water/waste, biomedical waste, vital statistics ~ PSM_WWB ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Environment and Health and Health Information
E ~ What is the recommended minimum free residual chlorine level in piped drinking water after a contact period of one hour? ~ 0.5 mg per litre ~ 0.05 mg per litre ~ 2 mg per litre ~ 5 mg per litre ~ A ~ The break-point chlorination principle requires enough chlorine to satisfy the demand and leave a measurable residual, and the residual is raised during outbreaks or where the distribution system is unreliable.
E ~ Which test indicates recent faecal contamination of a water supply and is used as the routine bacteriological standard? ~ Detection of Escherichia coli or thermotolerant coliforms, which should be absent in 100 mL ~ Total bacterial count ~ Presence of Clostridium spores ~ Turbidity measurement ~ A ~ Escherichia coli is a more specific indicator than total coliforms because the latter include environmental organisms, and spores indicate remote rather than recent contamination.
E ~ Which method of sewage disposal is appropriate for a rural household without piped water supply? ~ A sanitary latrine such as a twin-pit pour-flush or ventilated improved pit latrine ~ Septic tank with soakaway only ~ Discharge into a nearby stream ~ Open trenching ~ A ~ The twin-pit design allows one pit to rest and the contents to become safe manure while the other fills, which suits low water availability and gives a usable product.
M ~ Which registration system in India provides continuous legal recording of births and deaths, and under which act? ~ Civil Registration System, under the Registration of Births and Deaths Act 1969 ~ Sample Registration System, under the Census Act ~ National Family Health Survey ~ Health Management Information System ~ A ~ Completeness of civil registration has improved substantially but medical certification of cause of death remains limited, which is why the Sample Registration System still supplies national vital rates.
M ~ Which indicator is derived from the Sample Registration System rather than the census? ~ Infant mortality rate and crude birth and death rates, estimated annually ~ Sex ratio at the district level ~ Literacy rate ~ Population size by age ~ A ~ The dual record system of continuous enumeration with a half-yearly retrospective survey allows annual estimates of vital rates between decennial censuses.
M ~ Under the Bio-Medical Waste Rules 2016, which waste category goes into a yellow bag? ~ Human anatomical waste, soiled waste, expired medicines, chemical waste, discarded linen and laboratory waste ~ Contaminated recyclable plastics ~ Waste sharps ~ Glassware and metallic implants ~ A ~ Yellow waste is incinerated or treated by plasma pyrolysis rather than autoclaved and recycled like red-category plastics, so segregation at the point of generation determines the treatment route.
H ~ Why is the standard plate count of limited value in judging the safety of drinking water? ~ It counts organisms that may be harmless environmental flora and does not indicate faecal contamination ~ It is technically difficult ~ It cannot be performed on chlorinated water ~ It measures only spores ~ A ~ Indicator organism testing answers the relevant question of whether faecal material has reached the supply, which is why coliform and Escherichia coli testing are the standards rather than total counts.
H ~ Why does incomplete medical certification of cause of death limit health planning even when birth and death registration is complete? ~ Without reliable cause-of-death data, the pattern of disease driving mortality cannot be known, so priorities cannot be set or progress measured ~ Registration completeness is irrelevant ~ Verbal autopsy is never useful ~ Cause of death is inferred from age alone ~ A ~ Verbal autopsy in survey settings and expansion of medical certification are the two routes to filling the gap, and the international form of medical certificate distinguishes the underlying from the immediate cause.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Zoonoses: rabies, plague, leptospirosis, brucellosis ~ PSM_ZOO ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Zoonoses
M ~ Which measure is most effective for preventing human rabies at a population level? ~ Mass dog vaccination achieving over 70 percent coverage, with population management and public education ~ Post-exposure prophylaxis alone ~ Culling of stray dogs ~ Pre-exposure prophylaxis of the whole population ~ A ~ Interrupting transmission in the reservoir is what eliminates human deaths, and culling has repeatedly failed because it disturbs pack structure and is quickly offset by immigration and breeding.
H ~ Why is leptospirosis often described as an occupational and a disaster-associated disease? ~ Transmission follows skin or mucosal contact with water contaminated by animal urine, which rises with paddy farming, sewer work and after floods ~ It spreads person to person ~ It requires an arthropod vector ~ It is transmitted only by rodent bite ~ A ~ Prevention combines rodent control, protective footwear, wound care and, where exposure is intense and time-limited, doxycycline chemoprophylaxis, alongside heightened clinical suspicion after flooding.
