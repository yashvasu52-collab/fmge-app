@ Medicine ~ Adrenal disorders: Cushing's, Conn's, Addison's ~ MED_ADC ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 379 (Disorders of the Adrenal Cortex)
E ~ The best initial screening test for Cushing's syndrome in an outpatient with central obesity and purple striae is: ~ Overnight 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test ~ Random morning serum cortisol ~ Serum ACTH ~ CT abdomen ~ A ~ Random cortisol is uninterpretable because of pulsatile secretion; the low-dose dexamethasone test, late-night salivary cortisol and 24-hour urinary free cortisol are the three accepted screens, and ACTH is measured only after hypercortisolism is proved.
E ~ Hyperpigmentation of buccal mucosa and palmar creases in an adrenal insufficiency patient indicates: ~ Primary adrenal insufficiency with high ACTH ~ Secondary adrenal insufficiency ~ Exogenous steroid excess ~ Tertiary adrenal insufficiency ~ A ~ ACTH is cleaved from POMC, which also yields melanocyte-stimulating hormone; only primary (adrenal-level) failure raises ACTH enough to pigment the skin, so pituitary causes are pale.
E ~ The commonest cause of primary hyperaldosteronism worldwide is: ~ Bilateral idiopathic adrenal hyperplasia ~ Aldosterone-producing adenoma ~ Adrenal carcinoma ~ Glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism ~ A ~ Bilateral hyperplasia now outnumbers Conn's adenoma in screened hypertensive cohorts and matters because it is treated medically with spironolactone rather than by adrenalectomy.
M ~ A hypertensive patient has hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis, and a raised aldosterone-to-renin ratio. The next step is: ~ Confirmatory saline suppression or fludrocortisone test ~ Immediate adrenalectomy ~ Start ACE inhibitor and reassess ~ Renal artery Doppler ~ A ~ The ratio only screens; aldosterone must be shown to be non-suppressible before imaging and adrenal vein sampling decide between adenoma and hyperplasia.
M ~ In adrenal crisis the single most important immediate measure is: ~ Intravenous hydrocortisone with normal saline ~ Intravenous fludrocortisone ~ Vasopressor infusion alone ~ Broad-spectrum antibiotics alone ~ A ~ Hydrocortisone 100 mg supplies both glucocorticoid and enough mineralocorticoid activity, and volume repletion corrects the hypovolaemic hypotension that vasopressors alone will not reverse.
M ~ An incidentally found 3 cm adrenal mass with Hounsfield unit value under 10 on unenhanced CT is most likely: ~ Benign lipid-rich adenoma ~ Phaeochromocytoma ~ Adrenocortical carcinoma ~ Metastasis ~ A ~ Attenuation below 10 HU reflects intracytoplasmic lipid, which is characteristic of a benign cortical adenoma; malignant and catecholamine-secreting lesions are lipid-poor and denser.
M ~ Persistent hypertension after biochemical cure of Cushing's syndrome is best explained by: ~ Established vascular remodelling and nephrosclerosis ~ Ongoing mineralocorticoid excess ~ Rebound ACTH secretion ~ Adrenal vein thrombosis ~ A ~ Long-standing cortisol excess produces structural arterial and renal change that does not reverse when cortisol normalises, so many patients need continued antihypertensive therapy.
H ~ A patient with ACTH-dependent Cushing's fails to suppress on high-dose dexamethasone and has hypokalaemia with rapid onset of weakness. The most likely source is: ~ Ectopic ACTH from a small-cell lung carcinoma ~ Pituitary corticotroph adenoma ~ Adrenal adenoma ~ Exogenous glucocorticoid ~ A ~ Ectopic tumours lack glucocorticoid feedback receptors so they do not suppress at high dose, and their extreme cortisol levels overwhelm renal 11-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase to give a florid mineralocorticoid picture.
H ~ Hyponatraemia in Addison's disease is caused mainly by: ~ Aldosterone deficiency plus loss of cortisol restraint on vasopressin ~ Excess atrial natriuretic peptide ~ Renal tubular acidosis ~ Primary polydipsia ~ A ~ Sodium is lost through mineralocorticoid deficiency while absent cortisol permits inappropriate vasopressin release, so free water is retained on top of the sodium loss.
@ Medicine ~ Anaemias & haematinic deficiencies ~ MED_ANE ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 97 (Iron Deficiency and Other Hypoproliferative Anemias)
E ~ The earliest laboratory abnormality in developing iron deficiency is: ~ Fall in serum ferritin ~ Fall in haemoglobin ~ Rise in RDW ~ Microcytosis on the smear ~ A ~ Ferritin mirrors storage iron, which is exhausted before transport iron falls and long before erythropoiesis becomes iron-restricted enough to change cell size or haemoglobin.
E ~ A raised red cell distribution width with a normal MCV in an anaemic patient suggests: ~ Combined iron and vitamin B12 deficiency ~ Thalassaemia trait ~ Anaemia of chronic disease ~ Aplastic anaemia ~ A ~ Microcytes from iron deficiency and macrocytes from B12 deficiency average to a normal MCV, but the population is heterogeneous, which is exactly what a high RDW measures.
E ~ Oral iron is best absorbed when given: ~ On an empty stomach with vitamin C ~ With milk ~ With antacids ~ With tea after meals ~ A ~ Ferrous iron needs an acid environment and ascorbate keeps it reduced, whereas calcium, phytates, tannins and acid suppression all convert it to poorly absorbed ferric salts.
M ~ In a patient with megaloblastic anaemia, hypersegmented neutrophils and a normal serum B12, the most useful next test is: ~ Serum methylmalonic acid and homocysteine ~ Repeat serum B12 ~ Bone marrow biopsy ~ Reticulocyte count ~ A ~ Serum B12 is insensitive in early deficiency; methylmalonic acid rises only in B12 deficiency while homocysteine rises in both B12 and folate deficiency, which separates the two.
M ~ Anaemia of chronic disease is distinguished from iron deficiency mainly by: ~ Normal or raised ferritin with low transferrin saturation ~ Low ferritin with low saturation ~ High total iron binding capacity ~ Marked microcytosis ~ A ~ Hepcidin traps iron in macrophages, so stores are adequate or high while delivery to the marrow is poor, and hepcidin also lowers rather than raises the binding capacity.
M ~ Treating vitamin B12 deficiency with folate alone is dangerous because: ~ Haematological response occurs while neurological damage progresses ~ It causes haemolysis ~ It precipitates hypokalaemia ~ It causes iron overload ~ A ~ Folate bypasses the methionine synthase block for DNA synthesis but not for methylmalonyl-CoA metabolism, so subacute combined degeneration advances behind a correcting blood count.
M ~ A brisk reticulocytosis about one week after starting iron therapy indicates: ~ An appropriate marrow response confirming the diagnosis ~ Haemolysis ~ Iron overload ~ Treatment failure ~ A ~ The reticulocyte peak at day 5 to 10 is the earliest proof that the anaemia was iron-responsive, and haemoglobin should then rise by about 1 g/dL every two to three weeks.
H ~ Pancytopenia with a hypercellular marrow, raised LDH and low haptoglobin in a patient with nocturnal haemoglobinuria points to: ~ Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria ~ Aplastic anaemia ~ Myelodysplastic syndrome ~ Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia ~ A ~ Loss of the GPI anchor removes CD55 and CD59 from red cells, exposing them to complement lysis; flow cytometry for those markers has replaced the older Ham and sucrose lysis tests.
@ Medicine ~ Arrhythmias & ECG interpretation ~ MED_ARR ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 249 (The Tachyarrhythmias)
E ~ An irregularly irregular pulse with absent P waves on the ECG indicates: ~ Atrial fibrillation ~ Atrial flutter with fixed block ~ Sinus arrhythmia ~ Second degree AV block Mobitz I ~ A ~ Chaotic atrial activity produces fibrillatory waves rather than P waves and variable AV conduction gives the irregular ventricular response.
E ~ The rate of atrial depolarisation in typical atrial flutter is approximately: ~ 300 per minute ~ 150 per minute ~ 450 per minute ~ 100 per minute ~ A ~ The macro-reentrant circuit around the tricuspid annulus cycles at about 300, so a 2 to 1 block gives the classic ventricular rate near 150 with sawtooth waves in the inferior leads.
E ~ Progressive PR prolongation followed by a dropped beat describes: ~ Mobitz type I second degree AV block ~ Mobitz type II ~ First degree AV block ~ Complete heart block ~ A ~ The decremental conduction lies in the AV node itself, is usually vagally mediated and benign, and rarely progresses to complete block unlike infranodal Mobitz II.
M ~ The drug of choice for terminating a haemodynamically stable narrow-complex paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia after vagal manoeuvres fail is: ~ Intravenous adenosine ~ Intravenous amiodarone ~ Intravenous digoxin ~ Intravenous lignocaine ~ A ~ Adenosine transiently blocks the AV node, which is an obligatory limb of the reentry circuit, and its half-life of seconds makes it safe even if the rhythm turns out to be atrial in origin.
M ~ In a patient with atrial fibrillation, a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 3 in a man calls for: ~ Long-term oral anticoagulation ~ Aspirin alone ~ No antithrombotic therapy ~ Clopidogrel alone ~ A ~ Anticoagulation is indicated at a score of 2 or more in men, and direct oral anticoagulants are preferred over warfarin except in mechanical valves and moderate to severe mitral stenosis.
M ~ Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia with a twisting axis and a long QT interval is treated first with: ~ Intravenous magnesium sulphate ~ Intravenous amiodarone ~ Intravenous procainamide ~ Oral beta blocker ~ A ~ Magnesium suppresses the early afterdepolarisations that trigger torsades even when serum magnesium is normal, whereas amiodarone and procainamide prolong the QT further.
M ~ A short PR interval with a delta wave and a history of palpitations indicates: ~ Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome ~ Lown-Ganong-Levine syndrome ~ Left bundle branch block ~ Left ventricular hypertrophy ~ A ~ An accessory pathway pre-excites ventricular myocardium, slurring the QRS upstroke; the danger is atrial fibrillation conducting rapidly down the bypass tract to ventricular fibrillation.
H ~ Atrial fibrillation with a very rapid irregular broad-complex tachycardia in pre-excitation should be treated with: ~ Procainamide or synchronised cardioversion ~ Intravenous verapamil ~ Intravenous digoxin ~ Intravenous adenosine ~ A ~ AV nodal blockers push conduction preferentially down the accessory pathway and can precipitate ventricular fibrillation, so the pathway itself must be blocked or the rhythm cardioverted.
H ~ Cannon a waves in the jugular venous pulse with variable first heart sound intensity suggest: ~ Complete atrioventricular dissociation ~ Atrial fibrillation ~ Tricuspid stenosis ~ Constrictive pericarditis ~ A ~ When atria contract at random against closed tricuspid valves the venous pulse shows intermittent giant a waves, and the varying PR relationship changes mitral leaflet position and hence S1 intensity.
@ Medicine ~ Bleeding disorders, thrombophilia & anticoagulation ~ MED_COAG ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 65 (Disorders of Coagulation and Thrombosis)
E ~ An isolated prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time that corrects on mixing with normal plasma suggests: ~ A clotting factor deficiency ~ Lupus anticoagulant ~ A specific factor inhibitor ~ Vitamin K excess ~ A ~ Correction on mixing means the patient plasma lacks something that normal plasma supplies, whereas an inhibitor would carry its effect into the mixture and fail to correct.
E ~ Menorrhagia since menarche with easy bruising, a normal platelet count and a mildly prolonged APTT in a young woman suggests: ~ Von Willebrand disease ~ Haemophilia A carrier state ~ Immune thrombocytopenic purpura ~ Scurvy ~ A ~ Von Willebrand factor carries factor VIII and mediates platelet adhesion, so its deficiency gives mucocutaneous bleeding with a borderline APTT and a normal count.
E ~ The antidote for dabigatran is: ~ Idarucizumab ~ Protamine sulphate ~ Vitamin K ~ Andexanet alfa ~ A ~ Idarucizumab is a monoclonal antibody fragment with high affinity for dabigatran; andexanet alfa is the reversal agent for the factor Xa inhibitors rivaroxaban and apixaban.
M ~ A fall in platelet count between days 5 and 10 of heparin therapy with new thrombosis indicates: ~ Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia ~ Disseminated intravascular coagulation ~ Immune thrombocytopenic purpura ~ Dilutional thrombocytopenia ~ A ~ Antibodies to the heparin-platelet factor 4 complex activate platelets, so the paradox is thrombosis with a falling count; heparin must stop and a non-heparin anticoagulant such as argatroban or fondaparinux started.
M ~ Prolonged prothrombin time and APTT with low fibrinogen, high D-dimer and thrombocytopenia in a septic patient indicates: ~ Disseminated intravascular coagulation ~ Liver failure ~ Vitamin K deficiency ~ Primary fibrinolysis ~ A ~ Systemic thrombin generation consumes factors and platelets while secondary fibrinolysis raises D-dimer, and treatment is directed at the underlying sepsis with product support as needed.
M ~ The commonest inherited thrombophilia in populations of European descent is: ~ Factor V Leiden ~ Antithrombin deficiency ~ Protein C deficiency ~ Prothrombin gene mutation ~ A ~ The point mutation makes factor Va resistant to cleavage by activated protein C; it is common but confers a far lower risk per carrier than the rarer antithrombin deficiency.
M ~ Warfarin-induced skin necrosis in the first days of therapy is due to: ~ Early fall in protein C creating a transient prothrombotic state ~ Direct toxic effect on skin ~ Immune vasculitis ~ Heparin cross-reactivity ~ A ~ Protein C has a short half-life like factor VII, so anticoagulant protein falls before the procoagulant factors II and X, which is also why heparin must overlap warfarin initiation.
H ~ A prolonged APTT that fails to correct on mixing, with a history of recurrent miscarriage and thrombosis, indicates: ~ Antiphospholipid syndrome ~ Haemophilia A ~ Von Willebrand disease ~ Factor XIII deficiency ~ A ~ Lupus anticoagulant interferes with phospholipid-dependent clotting in vitro but causes thrombosis in vivo, and diagnosis needs a positive test repeated after twelve weeks.
H ~ Normal PT, APTT and platelet count with delayed bleeding and poor wound healing suggests deficiency of: ~ Factor XIII ~ Factor VIII ~ Factor VII ~ Fibrinogen ~ A ~ Factor XIII cross-links fibrin after the clot has formed, so all routine screening tests that measure clot formation are normal and diagnosis needs a urea clot solubility test.
@ Medicine ~ COPD & asthma ~ MED_CA ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 286 (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease)
E ~ The spirometric criterion for airflow obstruction in COPD is: ~ Post-bronchodilator FEV1 to FVC ratio below 0.70 ~ FEV1 below 80 percent predicted alone ~ FVC below 80 percent predicted ~ Reduced total lung capacity ~ A ~ The fixed ratio after bronchodilator defines persistent obstruction, and the FEV1 percentage predicted is then used to grade severity rather than to make the diagnosis.
E ~ The only interventions proven to slow decline and reduce mortality in COPD are: ~ Smoking cessation and long-term oxygen in chronic hypoxaemia ~ Inhaled corticosteroids ~ Theophylline ~ Mucolytics ~ A ~ Bronchodilators improve symptoms and exacerbation rate but do not alter the slope of FEV1 decline, while stopping smoking and correcting sustained hypoxaemia do change survival.
E ~ Target oxygen saturation during an acute exacerbation of COPD is: ~ 88 to 92 percent ~ 94 to 98 percent ~ Above 98 percent ~ 80 to 85 percent ~ A ~ Excess oxygen worsens ventilation-perfusion matching and reduces hypoxic drive, precipitating carbon dioxide retention, so titration to a modest target is safer.
M ~ Non-invasive ventilation in an acute COPD exacerbation is indicated when arterial blood gas shows: ~ pH below 7.35 with rising PaCO2 despite medical therapy ~ Isolated hypoxaemia ~ Normal pH with high PaCO2 ~ Metabolic acidosis ~ A ~ It is acute respiratory acidosis, not the absolute carbon dioxide level, that identifies decompensation, and NIV in that setting reduces intubation rates and mortality.
M ~ The stepwise addition of an inhaled corticosteroid to a long-acting beta agonist in COPD is most justified when: ~ Blood eosinophils are raised with frequent exacerbations ~ FEV1 is below 50 percent alone ~ The patient is a current smoker ~ There is chronic sputum production ~ A ~ Eosinophilic inflammation predicts corticosteroid responsiveness, whereas in the eosinophil-low patient the pneumonia risk of inhaled steroids outweighs the benefit.
M ~ A young adult with emphysema affecting the lower zones and deranged liver enzymes should be tested for: ~ Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency ~ Cystic fibrosis ~ Sarcoidosis ~ Lymphangioleiomyomatosis ~ A ~ Unopposed neutrophil elastase destroys basal lung tissue and the misfolded Z protein accumulates in hepatocytes, giving the combination of basal emphysema and liver disease.
M ~ A fall in peak expiratory flow to below 50 percent of personal best with a silent chest indicates: ~ Life-threatening acute severe asthma ~ Moderate exacerbation ~ Good control ~ Vocal cord dysfunction ~ A ~ Wheeze needs airflow to generate it, so a quiet chest with poor flows means airflow has almost ceased, and this patient needs urgent bronchodilators, systemic steroids and consideration of ventilation.
H ~ A normal or rising PaCO2 in a patient with acute severe asthma signifies: ~ Impending respiratory failure from fatigue ~ Adequate response to treatment ~ Coexisting metabolic alkalosis ~ Hyperventilation ~ A ~ Acute asthma normally causes hypocapnia from tachypnoea, so a normalising carbon dioxide means alveolar ventilation is falling as the patient tires and is an indication for critical care.
@ Medicine ~ Cardiomyopathies & adult congenital heart disease ~ MED_CMP ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 258 (Cardiomyopathy and Myocarditis)
E ~ Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is most often inherited as: ~ Autosomal dominant sarcomeric protein mutation ~ Autosomal recessive ~ X-linked recessive ~ Mitochondrial ~ A ~ Beta-myosin heavy chain and myosin-binding protein C mutations account for most cases, so first-degree relatives need screening even when asymptomatic.
E ~ The murmur of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy characteristically: ~ Increases with Valsalva and standing ~ Increases with squatting ~ Decreases with Valsalva ~ Is unaffected by posture ~ A ~ Anything that reduces preload narrows the outflow tract and worsens the dynamic gradient, which is the opposite of the behaviour of fixed aortic stenosis.
E ~ The commonest congenital heart defect surviving undetected into adulthood is: ~ Atrial septal defect ~ Ventricular septal defect ~ Tetralogy of Fallot ~ Transposition of the great arteries ~ A ~ The left-to-right shunt is low pressure and often silent for decades, presenting later with a fixed split second sound, atrial arrhythmias or pulmonary hypertension.
M ~ Wide fixed splitting of the second heart sound is characteristic of: ~ Atrial septal defect ~ Ventricular septal defect ~ Patent ductus arteriosus ~ Pulmonary stenosis ~ A ~ The interatrial communication equalises the respiratory variation in venous return between the two atria, so the pulmonary component stays late in both phases of respiration.
M ~ Sudden cardiac death in a young athlete is most commonly caused in India by: ~ Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy ~ Dilated cardiomyopathy ~ Aortic dissection ~ Myocarditis ~ A ~ Disarrayed hypertrophied myocardium is an arrhythmogenic substrate, and exertion provokes ventricular tachyarrhythmia, so competitive sport is restricted once the diagnosis is made.
M ~ Reversal of a left-to-right shunt with cyanosis and clubbing due to pulmonary vascular disease is called: ~ Eisenmenger syndrome ~ Ebstein anomaly ~ Lutembacher syndrome ~ Shone complex ~ A ~ Chronic high pulmonary flow remodels the arterioles until pulmonary resistance exceeds systemic, at which point closure of the defect is contraindicated because the shunt is now a decompression pathway.
M ~ Restrictive cardiomyopathy in India is most often due to: ~ Endomyocardial fibrosis ~ Amyloidosis ~ Haemochromatosis ~ Sarcoidosis ~ A ~ Tropical endomyocardial fibrosis obliterates the ventricular apices with fibrous tissue and eosinophilia is often implicated, whereas amyloid dominates restrictive disease in Western series.
H ~ Apical ballooning of the left ventricle after severe emotional stress with normal coronary arteries describes: ~ Takotsubo cardiomyopathy ~ Acute myocarditis ~ Anterior myocardial infarction ~ Peripartum cardiomyopathy ~ A ~ Catecholamine-mediated microvascular dysfunction produces a distinctive apical akinesis with basal hypercontractility that usually recovers completely within weeks.
H ~ Peripartum cardiomyopathy is defined as heart failure developing: ~ In the last month of pregnancy or within five months of delivery ~ In the first trimester ~ At any time in pregnancy ~ Only after twelve months postpartum ~ A ~ The window matters because earlier presentation usually reflects pre-existing cardiomyopathy unmasked by the volume load rather than the peripartum entity itself.
