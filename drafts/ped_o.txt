@ Pediatrics ~ Growth & development milestones ~ PED_GDM ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Growth and Development
M ~ A 4-year-old boy can hop on one foot, copy a cross, dress without help and tell a connected story. His development is: ~ Appropriate for age across gross motor, fine motor, language and social domains ~ Delayed in fine motor skills ~ Advanced for age ~ Delayed in language ~ A ~ Copying a cross and hopping belong to 4 years, copying a square and a triangle to 4.5 and 5 years, and using a spoon well and pulling off shoes to 2 years, so mapping each skill to its domain and age is how delay is identified.
H ~ A term infant's weight has fallen from the 50th to below the 3rd centile over 4 months while length and head circumference remain on the 50th. This pattern indicates: ~ Wasting from inadequate calorie intake or increased losses, the earliest anthropometric change in undernutrition ~ Familial short stature ~ Congenital hypothyroidism ~ Constitutional growth delay ~ A ~ Weight falls first, then length (stunting) and only late does head circumference fall, so the sequence of centile crossing distinguishes acute undernutrition from an endocrine or genetic cause.
@ Pediatrics ~ Immunization & common infections ~ PED_ICI ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Immunization
E ~ A child presents at 9 months having received only BCG, OPV-0 and hepatitis B at birth. The correct approach is: ~ Give all due vaccines simultaneously at separate sites and complete the schedule without restarting ~ Restart the whole schedule from the beginning ~ Give one vaccine per visit at monthly intervals ~ Defer until the child is 1 year old ~ A ~ There is no maximum interval between doses, so a lapsed schedule is resumed rather than restarted, and simultaneous administration at separate sites is safe and improves coverage.
E ~ Which vaccine is contraindicated in a child with symptomatic HIV and severe immunosuppression? ~ BCG and other live vaccines such as oral polio and measles-containing vaccine in severe immunosuppression ~ Hepatitis B vaccine ~ Pentavalent vaccine ~ Inactivated polio vaccine ~ A ~ Live vaccines risk disseminated vaccine-strain disease, notably BCG-osis, whereas inactivated vaccines are safe and indeed particularly important in immunodeficient children.
E ~ A 5-year-old sustains a clean minor wound and has received all five doses of DPT and DT as scheduled. The tetanus prophylaxis required is: ~ None, as the primary course plus boosters gives adequate protection for a clean minor wound ~ Toxoid alone ~ Toxoid plus immunoglobulin ~ Immunoglobulin alone ~ A ~ Fully immunised children need no additional dose for a clean minor wound, whereas a dirty or deep wound needs a booster if the last dose was over 5 years ago.
M ~ Which is the correct site and route for BCG vaccine in a newborn? ~ Intradermal into the left upper arm over the deltoid insertion ~ Intramuscular into the anterolateral thigh ~ Subcutaneous over the scapula ~ Oral ~ A ~ The intradermal route produces the papule, ulcer and scar sequence over 6 to 12 weeks, and injecting too deeply causes an abscess rather than the intended local reaction.
M ~ Which condition is a true contraindication to further doses of pertussis-containing vaccine? ~ Anaphylaxis to a previous dose, or encephalopathy within 7 days of a previous dose ~ Fever of 39 degrees after the last dose ~ A family history of seizures ~ Persistent crying for 2 hours after the last dose ~ A ~ Most reactions once considered contraindications, including prolonged crying, high fever and febrile seizures, are now regarded as precautions only, so unnecessary withholding of pertussis vaccine is a common error.
M ~ Which statement about the vitamin A supplementation schedule under the national programme is correct? ~ The first dose of 100000 IU is given at 9 months with measles-rubella vaccine, then 200000 IU every 6 months up to 5 years ~ A single dose is given at birth only ~ It is given monthly throughout infancy ~ It is given only to children with xerophthalmia ~ A ~ Nine biannual megadoses reduce all-cause child mortality by improving epithelial integrity and immune function, and giving it with measles vaccine improves coverage.
M ~ Which finding after measles vaccination in a well-nourished child is expected and requires only reassurance? ~ Mild fever with a transient rash 5 to 12 days later ~ Immediate urticaria ~ Rash within 24 hours ~ Persistent fever beyond 3 weeks ~ A ~ The live attenuated virus replicates over about a week, so any fever or rash appears in that window; immediate reactions suggest hypersensitivity to a vaccine component rather than vaccine viraemia.
M ~ Which vaccine must never be frozen, and why? ~ Adsorbed vaccines such as DPT, hepatitis B and pentavalent, because freezing damages the aluminium adjuvant and destroys potency ~ Oral polio vaccine, because freezing inactivates it ~ Measles vaccine, because freezing causes reversion ~ BCG, because freezing kills the bacilli ~ A ~ The shake test detects a frozen adsorbed vaccine, whereas live vaccines such as oral polio and measles tolerate freezing and are stored in the deep freezer, which is why ice packs are conditioned before use.
H ~ Why does the birth dose of hepatitis B vaccine need to be given within 24 hours? ~ Perinatal transmission occurs mainly at delivery, and immunisation within 24 hours prevents the 90 percent chronicity risk of neonatal infection ~ The vaccine is ineffective after 24 hours at any age ~ Maternal antibody blocks later doses ~ It reduces the number of subsequent doses needed ~ A ~ The younger the age at infection the higher the chronicity rate, so the timeliness of the birth dose, with immunoglobulin where available for infants of highly viraemic mothers, is a key hepatitis B control indicator.
H ~ Which principle explains why oral polio vaccine contributes to herd protection more than inactivated polio vaccine? ~ It induces intestinal mucosal immunity and vaccine virus spreads to unimmunised contacts ~ It contains more serotypes ~ It is given more often ~ It produces higher serum antibody ~ A ~ Secretory IgA in the gut interrupts wild virus transmission and the shed vaccine virus immunises contacts, which is also the mechanism by which circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus arises in under-immunised populations.
@ Pediatrics ~ Inborn errors, newborn screening & chromosomal syndromes ~ PED_IEM ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Inborn Errors of Metabolism and Genetics
E ~ Which condition included in newborn screening causes irreversible intellectual disability if not treated within the first weeks of life? ~ Congenital hypothyroidism ~ Cystic fibrosis ~ Sickle cell disease ~ Biotinidase deficiency ~ A ~ Thyroid hormone is essential for myelination and cortical development in the first months, so screening at 48 to 72 hours with prompt levothyroxine prevents the cretinism that would otherwise follow.
E ~ Which clinical clue should prompt urgent testing for an inborn error of metabolism in a previously well neonate? ~ Deterioration after a symptom-free interval with vomiting, lethargy, acidosis and a family history of neonatal death ~ Jaundice on day 3 ~ A single episode of poor feeding ~ Physiological weight loss ~ A ~ The characteristic pattern is a well baby who decompensates as feeding is established and the toxic metabolite accumulates, so first-line tests are glucose, blood gas, ammonia, lactate and urine ketones.
E ~ A neonate with vomiting, hyperammonaemia and a respiratory alkalosis has an inborn error most likely affecting: ~ The urea cycle ~ Fatty acid oxidation ~ Glycogen storage ~ Lysosomal storage ~ A ~ Ammonia stimulates the respiratory centre, so an early respiratory alkalosis with hyperammonaemia and no acidosis points to a urea cycle defect rather than an organic acidaemia, which causes acidosis with ketosis.
M ~ Which chromosomal syndrome is most likely in an infant with hypotonia, upslanting palpebral fissures, a single palmar crease, a flat nasal bridge and duodenal atresia? ~ Trisomy 21 ~ Trisomy 18 ~ Trisomy 13 ~ Turner syndrome ~ A ~ The double bubble of duodenal atresia, atrioventricular septal defect and Hirschsprung disease are the classic structural associations, and the risk of transient myeloproliferative disorder and later leukaemia is increased.
M ~ Which finding differentiates trisomy 18 from trisomy 13 in a newborn? ~ Clenched hands with overlapping index finger and rocker-bottom feet in trisomy 18, versus midline defects and polydactyly in trisomy 13 ~ Hypotonia in trisomy 18 only ~ Cardiac defect in trisomy 13 only ~ Cleft palate in trisomy 18 only ~ A ~ Trisomy 13 features holoprosencephaly, cleft lip and palate, microphthalmia and postaxial polydactyly, whereas trisomy 18 features the characteristic hand posture, small chin and short sternum.
M ~ Which metabolic disorder is suggested by a positive urinary reducing substance test with a negative glucose oxidase dipstick and cataracts in an infant? ~ Galactosaemia ~ Diabetes mellitus ~ Fructose intolerance ~ Renal glycosuria ~ A ~ Galactose is a reducing sugar not detected by the glucose-specific strip, and galactitol accumulation causes cataract; Escherichia coli sepsis is a classic accompanying presentation and a lactose-free diet is life-saving.
M ~ Which sample and timing are used for newborn screening by tandem mass spectrometry? ~ Heel-prick dried blood spot taken after 24 hours of feeding, ideally at 48 to 72 hours ~ Cord blood at delivery ~ Urine at 1 week ~ Venous blood at 1 month ~ A ~ Sampling before feeding is established gives false negatives for disorders that require substrate accumulation, and sampling too early gives false positives for congenital hypothyroidism because of the physiological TSH surge.
M ~ A child with coarse facies, corneal clouding, hepatosplenomegaly, joint stiffness and normal intelligence has which class of disorder? ~ Mucopolysaccharidosis, likely Hurler-Scheie or Scheie type ~ Glycogen storage disease ~ Urea cycle defect ~ Peroxisomal disorder ~ A ~ Urinary glycosaminoglycan screening followed by enzyme assay identifies the type, and preserved intelligence with corneal clouding distinguishes the attenuated forms from Hurler disease and from Hunter disease, which spares the cornea.
H ~ Why does a child with phenylketonuria on a strict low-phenylalanine diet still require tyrosine supplementation? ~ Tyrosine becomes an essential amino acid because it can no longer be made from phenylalanine ~ Tyrosine is lost in urine ~ Tyrosine inhibits phenylalanine absorption ~ Tyrosine prevents cataract ~ A ~ The blocked hydroxylation step removes the sole endogenous source of tyrosine, which is the precursor of dopamine, noradrenaline, thyroxine and melanin, explaining the fair hair and pale skin of untreated disease.
H ~ Which principle governs the acute management of a metabolic crisis in a suspected inborn error before the diagnosis is known? ~ Stop protein intake, give high-dose intravenous dextrose to halt catabolism, and treat hyperammonaemia while awaiting results ~ Give a high-protein feed to correct deficiency ~ Withhold all fluids ~ Start corticosteroids ~ A ~ Reversing catabolism removes the substrate driving toxin accumulation, and specific measures such as sodium benzoate, carnitine or vitamin cofactors are added as the pathway is identified, with dialysis for severe hyperammonaemia.
@ Pediatrics ~ Neonatal jaundice, sepsis, resuscitation ~ PED_NJS ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Neonatology
E ~ Jaundice appearing within the first 24 hours of life is always pathological and most often indicates: ~ Haemolysis from Rh or ABO incompatibility, or G6PD deficiency ~ Physiological jaundice ~ Breast milk jaundice ~ Neonatal hepatitis ~ A ~ Physiological jaundice never appears before 24 hours, so early onset demands immediate measurement of bilirubin with blood group, direct Coombs test, haematocrit and reticulocyte count.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes breast milk jaundice from breastfeeding failure jaundice? ~ Breast milk jaundice appears in the second week in a thriving baby, whereas failure jaundice appears in the first week with weight loss and dehydration ~ Both appear on day 1 ~ Breast milk jaundice causes conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia ~ Failure jaundice requires exchange transfusion ~ A ~ The distinction matters because the treatment of breastfeeding failure is to support and increase feeding, whereas breast milk jaundice is benign and breastfeeding is continued.
M ~ Prolonged jaundice beyond 2 weeks with pale stools, dark urine and a raised conjugated bilirubin requires urgent evaluation for: ~ Biliary atresia, because the Kasai portoenterostomy is most successful before 8 weeks of age ~ Physiological jaundice ~ Breast milk jaundice ~ Gilbert syndrome ~ A ~ Any conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia in an infant is pathological, and delay past 8 to 10 weeks sharply reduces the success of surgical drainage and the chance of avoiding transplantation.
M ~ During neonatal resuscitation, which action is taken if the heart rate remains below 100 after 30 seconds of effective positive pressure ventilation? ~ Check and correct ventilation technique using the corrective steps, then continue ventilation ~ Begin chest compressions immediately ~ Give adrenaline ~ Intubate and give surfactant ~ A ~ Ventilation is the cornerstone of neonatal resuscitation because the problem is almost always respiratory, and compressions are started only if the heart rate stays below 60 despite 30 seconds of effective ventilation.
H ~ Which finding in a neonate with sepsis carries the highest mortality and requires the most urgent intervention? ~ Shock with poor perfusion, prolonged capillary refill and metabolic acidosis ~ Isolated temperature instability ~ Single episode of apnoea ~ Feed intolerance alone ~ A ~ Neonatal septic shock progresses rapidly, so antibiotics within the first hour with careful volume expansion and inotropic support determine outcome, and the commonest early organisms are group B streptococcus, Escherichia coli and Klebsiella.
@ Pediatrics ~ Newborn assessment, prematurity & low birth weight ~ PED_NB ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Care of the Newborn
E ~ Low birth weight is defined as a birth weight below: ~ 2500 g regardless of gestation ~ 2000 g ~ 1500 g ~ 1000 g ~ A ~ Very low birth weight is below 1500 g and extremely low birth weight below 1000 g, and a low birth weight baby may be preterm, small for gestational age, or both.
E ~ Which method reduces mortality in stable low birth weight infants and can be provided in low-resource settings? ~ Kangaroo mother care with continuous skin-to-skin contact and exclusive breastfeeding ~ Routine incubator care only ~ Prophylactic antibiotics ~ Formula supplementation ~ A ~ Skin-to-skin contact stabilises temperature, heart rate and breathing, promotes breastfeeding and reduces infection and mortality, and it can begin as soon as the baby is haemodynamically stable.
E ~ Which score is used to estimate gestational age from physical and neuromuscular maturity in a newborn? ~ New Ballard score ~ Apgar score ~ Silverman-Andersen score ~ Downes score ~ A ~ The Apgar score assesses transition at birth, and the Silverman-Andersen and Downes scores grade respiratory distress, so each score answers a different question.
M ~ Which complication is a preterm infant of 28 weeks most at risk of in the first 72 hours? ~ Intraventricular haemorrhage from the fragile germinal matrix ~ Retinopathy of prematurity ~ Bronchopulmonary dysplasia ~ Necrotising enterocolitis ~ A ~ The subependymal germinal matrix is highly vascular with poor autoregulation, so fluctuations in pressure and carbon dioxide in the first days cause bleeding, whereas the other complications develop over weeks.
M ~ Screening for retinopathy of prematurity is indicated in which infants, and when? ~ Infants below 34 weeks or 1750 g, or those who received oxygen, screened from 3 to 4 weeks of age ~ All newborns at birth ~ Only infants with visible squint ~ At 6 months of age ~ A ~ Timely screening allows laser or anti-VEGF treatment at the threshold stage, and both hyperoxia and fluctuating oxygen saturations contribute, which is why saturation targets are set carefully.
M ~ Which feature suggests a small for gestational age infant is asymmetrically growth restricted rather than constitutionally small? ~ Reduced weight with relatively preserved length and head circumference and a raised ponderal index deficit ~ Proportionate reduction in all parameters ~ Normal weight with short length ~ Large head with normal weight ~ A ~ Asymmetrical restriction indicates late uteroplacental insufficiency with brain sparing, and these infants are at particular risk of hypoglycaemia, polycythaemia and hypothermia after birth.
M ~ Which measure best prevents hypothermia in a preterm infant immediately after delivery? ~ Delivery room temperature of 25 to 28 degrees with immediate drying, a pre-warmed radiant warmer, cap and plastic wrap ~ Bathing the baby immediately ~ Routine incubator at 40 degrees ~ Delayed drying to allow vernix absorption ~ A ~ Heat is lost by evaporation, conduction, convection and radiation, and preterm infants have a high surface-area-to-mass ratio with little brown fat, so the warm chain must begin in the delivery room.
H ~ Why is a preterm infant at greater risk of necrotising enterocolitis than a term infant? ~ Immature gut motility, barrier function and immunity with abnormal bacterial colonisation, aggravated by rapid feed advancement and formula ~ Higher gastric acid output ~ Excess pancreatic enzymes ~ Delayed cord clamping ~ A ~ Exclusive human milk, standardised slow feed advancement and avoiding prolonged empirical antibiotics reduce risk, and pneumatosis intestinalis on abdominal radiograph is the diagnostic hallmark.
H ~ Which physiological feature explains why a preterm infant develops apnoea of prematurity? ~ Immature central chemoreceptor response with a paradoxical ventilatory response to hypoxia and an immature Hering-Breuer reflex ~ Surfactant deficiency alone ~ Upper airway obstruction only ~ Excess carbon dioxide sensitivity ~ A ~ Caffeine acts on adenosine receptors to raise central respiratory drive and reduces the frequency of apnoea and the need for ventilation, which is why it is standard in very preterm infants.
@ Pediatrics ~ Paediatric GIT, liver disease & malabsorption ~ PED_GIT ~ OP Ghai Essential Pediatrics 9e, Ch. Gastrointestinal and Liver Disorders
E ~ A 6-week-old male has projectile non-bilious vomiting after feeds with visible peristalsis and a palpable olive-shaped epigastric mass. The expected metabolic abnormality is: ~ Hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis with paradoxical aciduria ~ Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis ~ Metabolic acidosis with ketosis ~ Respiratory alkalosis ~ A ~ Loss of gastric hydrochloric acid drives the alkalosis, and as potassium is depleted the kidney exchanges hydrogen rather than potassium for sodium, producing acid urine despite systemic alkalosis; correction precedes pyloromyotomy.
E ~ A 9-month-old has intermittent severe colicky pain with drawing up of the legs, a sausage-shaped abdominal mass and redcurrant jelly stools. The initial management is: ~ Resuscitation followed by air or hydrostatic reduction under imaging guidance ~ Immediate laparotomy in all cases ~ Observation with antispasmodics ~ Barium follow-through ~ A ~ Non-operative reduction succeeds in most cases of ileocolic intussusception, and surgery is reserved for peritonitis, perforation, shock or failed reduction; a pathological lead point is more likely beyond 2 years.
E ~ Which feature suggests coeliac disease in a child with chronic diarrhoea and failure to thrive? ~ Positive tissue transglutaminase IgA with a raised total IgA, confirmed by duodenal biopsy showing villous atrophy ~ Positive stool culture ~ Raised faecal calprotectin alone ~ Lactose intolerance on breath testing ~ A ~ Serology must be taken while the child is on a gluten-containing diet, total IgA is measured because deficiency causes false negatives, and biopsy shows crypt hyperplasia with intraepithelial lymphocytosis.
M ~ Which finding distinguishes Hirschsprung disease from functional constipation in an infant? ~ Delayed passage of meconium beyond 48 hours with an empty rectum on examination and explosive stool on withdrawal of the finger ~ Soiling with a loaded rectum ~ Onset after weaning ~ Response to dietary fibre ~ A ~ Aganglionosis causes failure of the rectosphincteric reflex, so anorectal manometry and rectal biopsy showing absent ganglion cells with hypertrophied nerve trunks confirm it.
M ~ A neonate has bilious vomiting on day 2 with a double bubble on abdominal radiograph and no distal gas. The likely diagnosis and association are: ~ Duodenal atresia, associated with Down syndrome ~ Malrotation with volvulus, associated with situs inversus ~ Jejunal atresia, associated with cystic fibrosis ~ Hirschsprung disease, associated with trisomy 18 ~ A ~ Bilious vomiting in a neonate is always pathological, and the absence of distal gas distinguishes complete atresia from stenosis or malrotation, which requires urgent contrast study because volvulus threatens the whole midgut.
M ~ Which is the commonest cause of chronic liver disease in Indian children and is screened for by which test? ~ Wilson disease, screened by serum ceruloplasmin and 24-hour urinary copper ~ Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, screened by protein electrophoresis ~ Autoimmune hepatitis, screened by ANA alone ~ Haemochromatosis, screened by ferritin ~ A ~ Wilson disease is treatable and easily missed, so ceruloplasmin, urinary copper, slit-lamp examination for Kayser-Fleischer rings and sometimes liver copper estimation are performed in any child with unexplained liver disease.
M ~ A 3-year-old has recurrent painless rectal bleeding, and a technetium pertechnetate scan shows uptake in the right lower quadrant. The diagnosis is: ~ Meckel diverticulum with ectopic gastric mucosa ~ Juvenile polyp ~ Anal fissure ~ Intussusception ~ A ~ The rule of twos describes the diverticulum, and the scan localises ectopic gastric mucosa whose acid secretion ulcerates adjacent ileal mucosa, causing the bleeding.
H ~ Which pattern of liver function tests suggests neonatal cholestasis rather than physiological jaundice, and what is the immediate imperative? ~ A conjugated fraction above 20 percent of total bilirubin with pale stools, requiring urgent evaluation for biliary atresia ~ Unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia with dark stools ~ Isolated transaminase elevation ~ Normal bilirubin with a raised alkaline phosphatase ~ A ~ Stool colour cards and early measurement of the conjugated fraction are the practical screening tools, because the window for a successful Kasai procedure closes within the first two months.
H ~ Why is fat-soluble vitamin supplementation essential in a child with chronic cholestasis? ~ Reduced bile salt delivery to the intestine impairs micelle formation, so absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K fails ~ Cholestasis increases urinary vitamin loss ~ Hepatic storage is unaffected ~ Vitamin absorption is normal but utilisation is impaired ~ A ~ Deficiency causes night blindness, rickets, neuropathy and coagulopathy respectively, so water-miscible preparations at high dose with monitoring of levels and prothrombin time are needed.
