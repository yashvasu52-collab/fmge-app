@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Menopause, HRT & osteoporosis ~ OBG_MEN ~ Shaw's Textbook of Gynaecology 17e, Ch. Menopause
E ~ Menopause is diagnosed clinically when a woman over 45 has had: ~ Twelve consecutive months of amenorrhoea with no other cause ~ Six months of amenorrhoea ~ Any hot flushes with irregular periods ~ A single raised follicle-stimulating hormone value ~ A ~ The diagnosis is retrospective and clinical in this age group, so hormone measurement is unnecessary and may mislead because gonadotropins fluctuate widely during the perimenopause.
E ~ A 38-year-old woman has amenorrhoea for a year with two follicle-stimulating hormone values above 25 IU/L taken 4 weeks apart. The diagnosis and key management principle are: ~ Premature ovarian insufficiency, requiring hormone replacement until the average age of menopause ~ Physiological menopause needing no treatment ~ Polycystic ovary syndrome ~ Hypothalamic amenorrhoea ~ A ~ Oestrogen replacement in premature ovarian insufficiency is not optional hormone therapy but replacement of a deficiency, protecting bone and cardiovascular health until about age 51.
E ~ Which symptom of the menopause responds best to systemic oestrogen therapy? ~ Vasomotor flushes and night sweats ~ Established osteoarthritis ~ Stress urinary incontinence ~ Loss of libido in isolation ~ A ~ Oestrogen reduces flush frequency by 75 percent or more and is the most effective treatment; genitourinary atrophy responds to local oestrogen and low libido may need androgen in addition.
M ~ A woman with an intact uterus is prescribed hormone therapy. Why must a progestogen be added to oestrogen? ~ To prevent endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma from unopposed oestrogen ~ To improve vasomotor control ~ To protect the breast ~ To reduce thrombotic risk ~ A ~ Unopposed oestrogen increases endometrial cancer risk severalfold, so a progestogen or the levonorgestrel intrauterine system is mandatory unless the woman has had a hysterectomy.
M ~ Which route of oestrogen therapy carries the lowest risk of venous thromboembolism? ~ Transdermal ~ Oral conjugated equine oestrogen ~ Oral oestradiol ~ Intramuscular depot ~ A ~ Avoiding first-pass hepatic metabolism means transdermal oestrogen has less effect on clotting factors and is preferred in women with obesity, migraine or a raised thrombotic risk.
M ~ Which non-hormonal drug has the best evidence for reducing hot flushes in a woman with a history of breast cancer on tamoxifen? ~ Venlafaxine or gabapentin ~ Paroxetine ~ Tibolone ~ Systemic oestradiol ~ A ~ Paroxetine and fluoxetine inhibit CYP2D6 and reduce conversion of tamoxifen to its active metabolite, so venlafaxine, gabapentin or clonidine are preferred, and systemic oestrogen is contraindicated.
M ~ A postmenopausal woman has a DXA T-score of minus 2.7 at the femoral neck with no fracture. First-line pharmacological treatment is: ~ An oral bisphosphonate with calcium and vitamin D ~ Hormone therapy ~ Teriparatide ~ Calcitonin ~ A ~ A T-score of minus 2.5 or below defines osteoporosis, and bisphosphonates reduce vertebral and hip fracture with the best cost-effectiveness; teriparatide is reserved for severe disease or bisphosphonate failure.
H ~ Which finding would make a woman ineligible for menopausal hormone therapy? ~ Current or past oestrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer ~ Well-controlled hypertension ~ A family history of osteoporosis ~ Previous hysterectomy ~ A ~ Absolute contraindications include current or past hormone-dependent breast or endometrial cancer, active venous thromboembolism, undiagnosed vaginal bleeding and active liver disease.
H ~ Why is hormone therapy started within 10 years of menopause associated with a different cardiovascular effect than therapy started later? ~ Oestrogen is protective on relatively healthy endothelium but may destabilise established atherosclerotic plaque ~ Absorption differs with age ~ Progestogen requirements change ~ Older women metabolise oestrogen faster ~ A ~ This timing hypothesis reconciles the Women's Health Initiative findings with observational data and underlies the current window-of-opportunity approach to prescribing.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Menstrual disorders, PCOS, fibroids ~ OBG_MDP ~ Shaw's Textbook of Gynaecology 17e, Ch. Disorders of Menstruation and Fibroids
E ~ Which FIGO category describes abnormal uterine bleeding caused by a submucosal fibroid? ~ Leiomyoma, submucosal subtype ~ Adenomyosis ~ Coagulopathy ~ Ovulatory dysfunction ~ A ~ The PALM-COEIN system separates structural causes, polyp, adenomyosis, leiomyoma and malignancy, from non-structural ones, and submucosal fibroids are the subtype that most often causes heavy bleeding.
M ~ A 30-year-old with a 4 cm submucosal fibroid causing heavy bleeding wishes to preserve fertility. The best treatment is: ~ Hysteroscopic myomectomy ~ Uterine artery embolisation ~ Hysterectomy ~ Gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist alone ~ A ~ Submucosal fibroids distorting the cavity are removed transcervically with good improvement in bleeding and fertility, whereas embolisation is generally avoided in women planning pregnancy.
M ~ A woman with a rapidly enlarging uterine mass after the menopause with pain and a raised lactate dehydrogenase should be evaluated for: ~ Leiomyosarcoma ~ Degenerating fibroid ~ Adenomyosis ~ Endometrial polyp ~ A ~ Fibroids should regress after the menopause, so growth in a postmenopausal woman is a red flag; this is also why morcellation is avoided when sarcoma is suspected.
M ~ Which diagnostic criterion set defines polycystic ovary syndrome, requiring two of three features? ~ Rotterdam criteria: oligo-ovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology ~ Amsel criteria ~ Jones criteria ~ Bethesda criteria ~ A ~ Because two of three features suffice, women may have the syndrome without cysts on ultrasound, and other causes of hyperandrogenism such as congenital adrenal hyperplasia must be excluded.
H ~ A woman with polycystic ovary syndrome and oligomenorrhoea who does not want pregnancy needs endometrial protection because: ~ Chronic anovulation exposes the endometrium to unopposed oestrogen, raising the risk of hyperplasia and carcinoma ~ Androgens directly cause carcinoma ~ Insulin resistance is carcinogenic to the cervix ~ Prolactin is raised ~ A ~ Cyclical progestogen, a combined pill or the levonorgestrel intrauterine system induces regular shedding and reverses hyperplasia, and this is a core long-term management aim independent of fertility.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Multiple pregnancy, polyhydramnios & IUGR ~ OBG_MUL ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Multiple Pregnancy
E ~ Which ultrasound finding at 11 weeks establishes a monochorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy? ~ A T-sign at the intertwin membrane insertion with a single placental mass ~ The lambda or twin peak sign ~ Two separate placentas ~ Discordant fetal sex ~ A ~ Chorionicity is best determined in the first trimester, and the thin membrane inserting perpendicularly as a T marks monochorionicity, which carries all the specific complications of shared circulation.
E ~ Which complication is unique to monochorionic twins? ~ Twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome ~ Preterm labour ~ Anaemia ~ Malpresentation ~ A ~ Vascular anastomoses in a shared placenta allow unbalanced transfusion, producing a donor with oligohydramnios and a recipient with polyhydramnios, which is why monochorionic pregnancies need fortnightly scans from 16 weeks.
E ~ Amniotic fluid index above 24 cm with a single deepest pocket over 8 cm defines: ~ Polyhydramnios ~ Oligohydramnios ~ Normal fluid ~ Anhydramnios ~ A ~ Polyhydramnios raises the risk of preterm labour, malpresentation, cord prolapse and postpartum haemorrhage, and its causes include maternal diabetes, fetal swallowing defects and fetal anaemia.
M ~ A monochorionic twin pair at 20 weeks has one twin with a deepest pocket of 1 cm and the other with 10 cm. The treatment of choice is: ~ Fetoscopic laser ablation of the placental anastomoses ~ Serial amnioreduction alone ~ Expectant management ~ Immediate delivery ~ A ~ The Eurofoetus trial showed better survival and neurological outcome with laser photocoagulation than amnioreduction for stage II and above twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome before 26 weeks.
M ~ A fetus has an abdominal circumference below the third centile with a normal head circumference and normal umbilical artery Doppler. This pattern suggests: ~ Asymmetrical growth restriction from placental insufficiency, with brain sparing ~ Symmetrical growth restriction from aneuploidy ~ A constitutionally small but healthy fetus ~ Macrosomia ~ A ~ Late-onset placental insufficiency spares the head at the expense of the abdomen and subcutaneous fat, whereas early symmetrical restriction suggests an intrinsic fetal cause such as aneuploidy or infection.
M ~ Which measure has been shown to reduce the risk of preterm birth in a woman with a twin pregnancy and a short cervix? ~ Vaginal progesterone ~ Routine cervical cerclage in all twins ~ Bed rest ~ Prophylactic tocolysis ~ A ~ Meta-analysis shows vaginal progesterone reduces preterm birth and neonatal morbidity in twins with a cervix under 25 mm, whereas routine cerclage and bed rest are ineffective or harmful.
M ~ In a twin delivery, the first twin delivers vaginally as a vertex and the second is transverse with intact membranes. The appropriate action is: ~ Internal podalic version and breech extraction, or external cephalic version, by an experienced operator ~ Immediate caesarean section in all cases ~ Oxytocin augmentation and wait ~ Vacuum extraction ~ A ~ With the cervix fully dilated and the uterus relaxed, the second twin can usually be delivered by version and breech extraction under continuous monitoring, avoiding a combined vaginal and abdominal delivery.
H ~ Which mechanism explains twin reversed arterial perfusion sequence? ~ An acardiac twin is perfused retrogradely through artery-to-artery anastomoses by the pump twin ~ Unequal placental sharing alone ~ Cord entanglement ~ Discordant aneuploidy ~ A ~ Deoxygenated blood flowing backwards into the acardiac mass causes it to grow abnormally while imposing a high-output load on the pump twin, so cord occlusion of the acardiac twin is offered.
H ~ Monoamniotic twins are delivered by planned caesarean at about 32 to 34 weeks primarily because of: ~ The risk of sudden fetal death from cord entanglement ~ Twin-to-twin transfusion ~ Growth restriction ~ Maternal preeclampsia ~ A ~ With no dividing membrane, cords intertwine in nearly all monoamniotic pregnancies and can knot acutely, so inpatient surveillance and early planned delivery are standard.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Obstetric analgesia & anaesthesia; maternal mortality ~ OBG_ANA ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Obstetric Analgesia and Maternal Mortality
E ~ Which physiological change of pregnancy most increases the risk of failed intubation at caesarean section? ~ Airway oedema with increased Mallampati grade and large breasts ~ Reduced gastric emptying ~ Increased minute ventilation ~ Reduced systemic vascular resistance ~ A ~ Capillary engorgement narrows the airway and progresses through labour, and combined with a short neck and enlarged breasts makes laryngoscopy harder, so a smaller tube and a ramped position are used.
E ~ Why is a pregnant woman at increased risk of aspiration during general anaesthesia? ~ Progesterone-induced relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter with raised intragastric pressure ~ Increased gastric acid secretion only ~ Reduced tidal volume ~ Increased plasma volume ~ A ~ These changes underlie Mendelson syndrome, which is why regional anaesthesia is preferred and why acid prophylaxis, cricoid pressure and rapid sequence induction are used when general anaesthesia is unavoidable.
E ~ In a district recording 24 maternal deaths against 40000 live births in a year, the maternal mortality ratio is: ~ 60 per 100000 live births ~ 24 per 100000 live births ~ 6 per 1000 live births ~ 0.6 per 100000 live births ~ A ~ The ratio is maternal deaths divided by live births multiplied by 100000, so 24 divided by 40000 gives 60 per 100000; the denominator of live births is what distinguishes the ratio from the maternal mortality rate.
M ~ A woman receives an epidural in labour and 10 minutes later has a rapidly ascending block with hypotension, difficulty breathing and weak arms. The diagnosis is: ~ Inadvertent intrathecal injection causing a high or total spinal block ~ Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity ~ Amniotic fluid embolism ~ Supine hypotension syndrome ~ A ~ A total spinal requires immediate airway support, ventilation, fluids and vasopressors; a test dose and incremental administration are designed to detect misplacement before a full dose is given.
M ~ Which drug is used first for hypotension after spinal anaesthesia for caesarean section, and why? ~ Phenylephrine, because it maintains uterine perfusion better than ephedrine and causes less fetal acidosis ~ Ephedrine, because it is a direct agonist ~ Adrenaline, because of its inotropic action ~ Dopamine, because of renal protection ~ A ~ Ephedrine crosses the placenta and increases fetal metabolic rate and acidaemia, so a pure alpha agonist with left lateral tilt and fluid co-loading is now preferred.
M ~ Which category of maternal death is classed as a direct obstetric death? ~ Death from postpartum haemorrhage ~ Death from pre-existing rheumatic heart disease aggravated by pregnancy ~ Death in a road traffic accident during pregnancy ~ Death from tuberculosis diagnosed in pregnancy ~ A ~ Direct deaths result from obstetric complications of pregnancy, labour or the puerperium; aggravated pre-existing disease is an indirect death and accidents are incidental or coincidental deaths.
M ~ Which regional technique is preferred for a woman needing urgent caesarean section who already has a working labour epidural? ~ Top-up of the existing epidural ~ De novo spinal anaesthesia ~ General anaesthesia ~ Local infiltration ~ A ~ Extending an existing block is faster and avoids the risks of both general anaesthesia and a second neuraxial puncture, provided the epidural has been providing good analgesia.
H ~ A woman collapses shortly after delivery with hypoxia, hypotension, seizures and profound coagulopathy. The likely diagnosis and initial management are: ~ Amniotic fluid embolism, managed by immediate cardiorespiratory support and aggressive correction of coagulopathy ~ Pulmonary embolism, managed by thrombolysis ~ Eclampsia, managed by magnesium ~ Septic shock, managed by antibiotics ~ A ~ Amniotic fluid embolism is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion presenting with the triad of hypoxia, hypotension and disseminated intravascular coagulation, and there is no specific treatment beyond intensive supportive care.
H ~ Which change to the definition of a maternal death captures deaths occurring between 42 days and one year after delivery? ~ Late maternal death ~ Direct maternal death ~ Pregnancy-related death ~ Incidental death ~ A ~ Recognising late maternal deaths matters because suicide, cardiac disease and cancer contribute substantially to mortality beyond the traditional 42-day window used in maternal mortality ratios.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Physiological changes in pregnancy & fetal development ~ OBG_PHY ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Physiological Changes in Pregnancy
E ~ Which haematological change explains the physiological anaemia of pregnancy? ~ Plasma volume rises proportionately more than red cell mass, diluting haemoglobin ~ Red cell mass falls absolutely ~ Iron absorption decreases ~ Erythropoietin falls ~ A ~ Plasma volume rises by about 45 percent and red cell mass by only 20 to 30 percent, so haemoglobin concentration falls even though total oxygen-carrying capacity increases.
E ~ Systemic vascular resistance in normal pregnancy: ~ Falls, producing a mid-trimester nadir in blood pressure ~ Rises steadily throughout ~ Remains unchanged ~ Falls only in the third trimester ~ A ~ Progesterone and placental vasodilators reduce resistance from early pregnancy, so blood pressure dips around 20 to 24 weeks and returns to baseline by term, which can mask chronic hypertension at booking.
E ~ Which respiratory change is characteristic of normal pregnancy? ~ Increased tidal volume with unchanged respiratory rate, giving a mild respiratory alkalosis ~ Increased respiratory rate with unchanged tidal volume ~ Increased functional residual capacity ~ Reduced minute ventilation ~ A ~ Progesterone-driven hyperventilation lowers arterial carbon dioxide to about 30 mmHg with renal bicarbonate compensation, which facilitates transfer of fetal carbon dioxide across the placenta.
M ~ Which renal change makes a serum creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL abnormal in pregnancy? ~ Glomerular filtration rate rises by about 50 percent, so normal creatinine falls to about 0.4 to 0.8 mg/dL ~ Tubular secretion of creatinine falls ~ Muscle mass increases ~ Renal blood flow falls ~ A ~ Because filtration rises so much, a creatinine within the non-pregnant reference range may signify significant renal impairment, which is why pregnancy-specific ranges must be used.
M ~ Which change in the coagulation system of pregnancy explains the increased thrombotic risk? ~ Rises in fibrinogen and factors VII, VIII and X with a fall in protein S and increased plasminogen activator inhibitor ~ A fall in fibrinogen ~ Thrombocytosis alone ~ Increased antithrombin III ~ A ~ Pregnancy is a physiologically hypercoagulable state that limits bleeding at delivery, but combined with venous stasis and endothelial injury it fulfils Virchow's triad, so thromboprophylaxis is risk-assessed at every admission.
M ~ Which gastrointestinal change of pregnancy accounts for constipation and gallstone formation? ~ Progesterone-mediated smooth muscle relaxation slowing transit and reducing gallbladder emptying ~ Increased gastric acid ~ Increased pancreatic enzyme secretion ~ Reduced bile cholesterol saturation ~ A ~ Reduced motility prolongs colonic water absorption and allows biliary stasis with more lithogenic bile, so gallstones and cholestasis are commoner in pregnancy and the puerperium.
M ~ At what gestation does the fetal kidney begin to contribute the major part of amniotic fluid, making fluid volume a marker of renal function? ~ From about 16 weeks ~ From 8 weeks ~ From 28 weeks ~ Only after 34 weeks ~ A ~ Before 16 weeks amniotic fluid is largely a transudate, after which fetal urine dominates, which is why bilateral renal agenesis produces anhydramnios in the second trimester with Potter sequence.
H ~ Which fetal circulatory shunt closes functionally within minutes of birth as a result of rising arterial oxygen tension? ~ Ductus arteriosus ~ Ductus venosus ~ Foramen ovale ~ Umbilical vein ~ A ~ Oxygen constricts ductal smooth muscle while the fall in circulating prostaglandin E2 after placental separation removes the dilating stimulus, which is why prostaglandin synthesis inhibitors close a patent duct and prostaglandin infusion keeps it open.
H ~ Which substance crosses the placenta by simple diffusion and is therefore transferred at a rate limited by uterine blood flow rather than by carrier availability? ~ Oxygen ~ Glucose ~ Amino acids ~ Immunoglobulin G ~ A ~ Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse down their gradients so their transfer is flow-limited, glucose crosses by facilitated diffusion, amino acids by active transport and IgG by receptor-mediated endocytosis.
@ Obstetrics & Gynaecology ~ Postpartum haemorrhage ~ OBG_PH ~ DC Dutta's Textbook of Obstetrics 9e, Ch. Postpartum Haemorrhage
E ~ Which of the four Ts accounts for about 70 percent of primary postpartum haemorrhage? ~ Tone, that is uterine atony ~ Trauma ~ Tissue ~ Thrombin ~ A ~ Because atony predominates, active management of the third stage with a prophylactic uterotonic is the single most effective preventive measure and reduces haemorrhage by about 60 percent.
E ~ Which drug given within 3 hours of onset reduces death due to bleeding in postpartum haemorrhage? ~ Tranexamic acid ~ Recombinant factor VIIa ~ Vitamin K ~ Desmopressin ~ A ~ The WOMAN trial showed reduced death from bleeding when tranexamic acid was given early, with no increase in thrombotic events, so it is now part of standard first-line management alongside uterotonics.
E ~ Which uterotonic is contraindicated in a woman with asthma? ~ Carboprost ~ Oxytocin ~ Misoprostol ~ Methylergometrine ~ A ~ Carboprost is a prostaglandin F2-alpha analogue that causes bronchospasm, whereas methylergometrine is avoided in hypertension and oxytocin in large rapid boluses because of hypotension and water retention.
M ~ After delivery of the placenta the uterus is well contracted but bright red bleeding continues. The next step is: ~ Systematic examination of the cervix, vagina and perineum under good light for trauma ~ Repeat oxytocin ~ Bimanual compression ~ Hysterectomy ~ A ~ With a contracted uterus the cause is genital tract trauma, so a careful inspection with adequate exposure and analgesia identifies cervical or high vaginal tears that would not respond to uterotonics.
M ~ A woman has continuing atonic haemorrhage despite uterotonics. Which balloon-based measure is used before laparotomy? ~ Intrauterine balloon tamponade ~ Uterine artery embolisation ~ B-Lynch suture ~ Internal iliac ligation ~ A ~ A hydrostatic balloon achieves tamponade in about 85 percent of atonic cases, is quick and preserves fertility, and its failure is a useful trigger for moving to laparotomy.
M ~ Which compression suture is used at laparotomy for atonic postpartum haemorrhage? ~ B-Lynch brace suture ~ Shirodkar suture ~ McDonald suture ~ Purse-string suture of the cervix ~ A ~ The brace suture compresses the anterior and posterior uterine walls together, and variants such as Hayman and Cho square sutures serve the same purpose; the other options are cervical cerclage techniques.
M ~ Secondary postpartum haemorrhage at 10 days with a tender bulky uterus and offensive lochia is most often due to: ~ Endometritis with retained products of conception ~ Uterine atony ~ Coagulopathy ~ Cervical tear ~ A ~ Treatment is antibiotics with gentle evacuation once infection is controlled, and ultrasound helps but cannot always distinguish clot from retained tissue.
M ~ A woman with placenta praevia and a previous caesarean has bleeding at caesarean from the lower segment which does not respond to oxytocin. Why is this bleeding hard to control? ~ The lower segment has less myometrium and contracts poorly, so mechanical or surgical measures are needed ~ The placenta secretes an anticoagulant ~ Oxytocin receptors are absent in pregnancy ~ The uterine arteries are absent in the lower segment ~ A ~ Lower-segment atony after praevia is a recognised cause of intractable haemorrhage, addressed by local sutures, balloon tamponade, vessel ligation or hysterectomy rather than by more uterotonic.
H ~ Which intervention is definitive when postpartum haemorrhage persists despite uterotonics, tamponade, compression sutures and vessel ligation? ~ Subtotal or total hysterectomy ~ A further dose of carboprost ~ Repeat balloon insertion ~ Continued observation ~ A ~ Deciding on hysterectomy early enough is what saves lives, and delay while repeating failed measures is a recurrent theme in maternal mortality reviews.
H ~ Which late complication follows severe postpartum haemorrhage with prolonged hypotension and presents with failure of lactation and amenorrhoea? ~ Sheehan syndrome from pituitary infarction ~ Asherman syndrome ~ Premature ovarian insufficiency ~ Postpartum thyroiditis ~ A ~ The enlarged, portally supplied anterior pituitary of pregnancy is vulnerable to ischaemic necrosis, so panhypopituitarism follows, typically declaring itself first as failure of lactation.
