@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Association & causation, bias, confounding, measures of risk ~ PSM_ACB ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Epidemiology
E ~ In a cohort study, the incidence of lung cancer is 40 per 1000 in smokers and 8 per 1000 in non-smokers. The relative risk is: ~ 5 ~ 32 ~ 0.2 ~ 48 ~ A ~ Relative risk is the incidence in the exposed divided by the incidence in the unexposed, so 40 divided by 8 gives 5, meaning smokers have five times the risk; the attributable risk of 32 per 1000 gives the excess burden.
E ~ Using the same figures, the attributable risk percentage among smokers is approximately: ~ 80 percent ~ 20 percent ~ 32 percent ~ 500 percent ~ A ~ Attributable risk percentage is the excess risk divided by the risk in the exposed, so 32 divided by 40 gives 80 percent, meaning four-fifths of the disease in smokers is attributable to smoking.
E ~ Which measure of association is obtained from a case-control study? ~ Odds ratio ~ Relative risk ~ Incidence rate ~ Attributable risk ~ A ~ Because subjects are selected on outcome, incidence cannot be measured, so exposure odds are compared; the odds ratio approximates the relative risk when the disease is rare.
M ~ Cases of a disease are recruited from a specialist hospital while controls come from the general community. Which bias is most likely? ~ Selection bias, specifically Berkson bias ~ Recall bias ~ Interviewer bias ~ Lead-time bias ~ A ~ Hospital cases differ systematically from community cases in severity, comorbidity and referral pattern, which distorts the exposure distribution independently of any true association.
M ~ In a case-control study of birth defects, mothers of affected infants remember drug exposure more completely than mothers of healthy infants. This is: ~ Recall bias, a form of information bias ~ Confounding ~ Selection bias ~ Random error ~ A ~ Differential accuracy of recall between groups biases the exposure estimate, and it is reduced by using records rather than memory, by blinding interviewers and by using diseased controls.
M ~ A study finds coffee drinking associated with lung cancer, but coffee drinkers smoke more. Smoking here is acting as: ~ A confounder, being associated with the exposure and an independent risk factor for the outcome ~ An effect modifier ~ A mediator on the causal pathway ~ A source of random error ~ A ~ Confounding is controlled at design by randomisation, matching or restriction, and at analysis by stratification or multivariable adjustment; a variable on the causal pathway would be a mediator and should not be adjusted for.
M ~ Which of Bradford Hill's considerations for causation is generally regarded as the most compelling when present? ~ Temporality, since the exposure must precede the outcome ~ Strength of association ~ Biological plausibility ~ Analogy ~ A ~ Temporality is the only criterion that is strictly necessary; the others, including dose-response gradient, consistency and reversibility, strengthen the argument but none alone establishes causation.
H ~ A screening study reports that a drug halves relative risk, and the number needed to treat is 250. Which conclusion follows? ~ The absolute risk reduction is small, so the relative measure exaggerates the clinical impact ~ The drug is highly effective clinically ~ The baseline risk must be high ~ The result must be statistically insignificant ~ A ~ Number needed to treat is the reciprocal of absolute risk reduction, so 250 corresponds to an absolute reduction of 0.4 percent, illustrating why relative measures must always be read alongside absolute ones.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Child health programmes, IMNCI & school health ~ PSM_CHI ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Preventive Medicine in Obstetrics, Paediatrics and Geriatrics
E ~ Which strategy underpins the Integrated Management of Neonatal and Childhood Illness approach? ~ Syndromic assessment, classification and treatment of the sick child by a trained health worker using colour-coded charts ~ Laboratory-based diagnosis at every level ~ Referral of all sick children to district hospitals ~ Vertical programmes for each disease ~ A ~ The approach recognises that a sick child often has more than one condition, so a single integrated assessment covering danger signs, cough, diarrhoea, fever, malnutrition and immunisation status improves both case management and coverage.
E ~ Which programme provides facility-based newborn care through special newborn care units at district hospitals? ~ Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram and the newborn care continuum under the National Health Mission ~ Mid-day meal scheme ~ Integrated Child Development Services alone ~ Janani Suraksha Yojana alone ~ A ~ The newborn care continuum links home-based newborn care by ASHAs, newborn care corners at delivery points, newborn stabilisation units and special newborn care units, addressing the largest component of under-five mortality.
E ~ School health services are best justified on which ground? ~ Schools reach a large captive population at an age when health habits form and screenable conditions are prevalent ~ Schools can replace primary health centres ~ Teachers can prescribe medicines ~ It reduces the need for immunisation ~ A ~ Screening for vision, hearing, dental and nutritional problems, deworming, iron and folic acid supplementation and health education all use the school as an efficient platform.
M ~ Which four Ds does Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram screen for in children up to 18 years? ~ Defects at birth, deficiencies, diseases and developmental delays including disability ~ Diarrhoea, dysentery, diphtheria and dengue ~ Diabetes, dental caries, deafness and dyslexia ~ Dehydration, dystrophy, dysentery and delay ~ A ~ Early identification and free management through district early intervention centres is the design, covering 30 identified conditions across the four categories.
M ~ Under IMNCI, a 3-month-old infant with fast breathing, chest indrawing and a temperature of 38 degrees is classified as: ~ Severe pneumonia or very severe disease, requiring pre-referral antibiotic and urgent referral ~ Pneumonia, treated at home ~ Cough or cold, needing no antibiotic ~ Severe malnutrition ~ A ~ In the young infant group of under 2 months the classification is stricter still, and any severe classification triggers pre-referral treatment because deterioration is rapid.
M ~ Home-based newborn care visits by an ASHA for an institutional delivery are scheduled on which days? ~ Days 3, 7, 14, 21, 28 and 42 ~ Days 1, 2 and 3 only ~ Weekly for 6 months ~ Only if the mother requests ~ A ~ For a home delivery visits begin on day 1, and the schedule targets the days on which neonatal deaths cluster, with weighing, temperature assessment, danger sign recognition and breastfeeding support at each visit.
M ~ Which indicator best reflects the effectiveness of a child health programme in reducing preventable child deaths? ~ Under-five mortality rate ~ Crude death rate ~ Maternal mortality ratio ~ Life expectancy at birth ~ A ~ The under-five mortality rate is sensitive to nutrition, immunisation, infection control and access to care, which is why it is a Sustainable Development Goal indicator, and the neonatal component now dominates it in India.
H ~ Why has the neonatal mortality rate become the limiting factor in further reducing infant mortality in India? ~ Deaths from prematurity, intrapartum events and infection in the first 28 days now form the majority of infant deaths, and these need facility-based care rather than community measures alone ~ Post-neonatal deaths have risen ~ Immunisation coverage has fallen ~ Diarrhoeal deaths are increasing ~ A ~ Reducing them requires quality intrapartum and newborn care, kangaroo mother care, antenatal steroids and management of sepsis, which is a different and harder task than the diarrhoea and vaccine-preventable deaths already addressed.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Concepts of health & disease, natural history, levels of prevention ~ PSM_CON ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Concept of Health and Disease
E ~ Administering measles vaccine to a susceptible child is an example of: ~ Specific protection, a form of primary prevention ~ Health promotion ~ Secondary prevention ~ Tertiary prevention ~ A ~ Primary prevention has two arms, health promotion which is non-specific and specific protection which targets a defined agent, and both act before the disease process begins.
E ~ Screening a population for cervical cancer and treating precancerous lesions is: ~ Secondary prevention, aimed at early diagnosis and treatment ~ Primary prevention ~ Tertiary prevention ~ Health promotion ~ A ~ Secondary prevention acts during the presymptomatic phase of the natural history to halt progression, whereas HPV vaccination for the same disease is primary prevention.
E ~ Physiotherapy and vocational retraining after a stroke represent: ~ Tertiary prevention through disability limitation and rehabilitation ~ Secondary prevention ~ Specific protection ~ Health promotion ~ A ~ Tertiary prevention accepts that disease and its consequences have occurred and works to limit impairment and restore function, and it includes the social and vocational as well as the physical.
M ~ A serological survey in a district finds antibody to a virus in 40 percent of the population although only 300 clinical cases were notified. Which epidemiological concept does this illustrate, and what does it imply for control? ~ The iceberg phenomenon, implying that control based only on notified cases will fail ~ Herd immunity, implying no further action is needed ~ Lead-time bias, implying the survey is invalid ~ Secular trend, implying a long-term decline ~ A ~ Subclinical infections, carriers and undiagnosed cases greatly outnumber the clinical cases that come to notice, which is why serological surveys and surveillance rather than case counts alone guide policy.
M ~ Which concept describes the ability of a healthcare system to prevent disease when an agent, host and environment are all present? ~ Modification of any one arm of the epidemiological triad interrupts the process ~ Elimination of the agent alone is sufficient ~ The host must be treated first ~ Environment is irrelevant once the agent is present ~ A ~ Because the three interact, control can act on the agent by treatment or disinfection, on the host by immunisation or nutrition, or on the environment by sanitation and vector control, and combining them is usually most effective.
M ~ Positive health, as distinct from the absence of disease, is best expressed by which concept? ~ A spectrum from optimum wellbeing through to death, with health as a dynamic state rather than a dichotomy ~ Freedom from all pathology ~ Normal laboratory values ~ Absence of disability ~ A ~ This continuum underlies the WHO definition including social wellbeing and the later addition of spiritual dimensions, and it is why quality-of-life measures supplement mortality and morbidity indicators.
M ~ Which stage of the natural history of disease is targeted by chemoprophylaxis of contacts? ~ The stage of susceptibility or early presymptomatic disease before clinical manifestation ~ The stage of clinical disease ~ The stage of disability ~ The stage of convalescence ~ A ~ Tuberculosis preventive treatment of a household contact and antimalarial chemoprophylaxis for a traveller both act before clinical disease appears, straddling primary and secondary prevention.
H ~ Why is the concept of a lead time important when a screening programme reports improved survival? ~ Earlier diagnosis lengthens measured survival from diagnosis to death even if death occurs at the same moment, so mortality rather than survival must be the outcome measure ~ It shortens the disease duration ~ It reflects the sensitivity of the test ~ It measures programme cost ~ A ~ Together with length-time bias and overdiagnosis, lead time explains why only a randomised comparison of disease-specific mortality can establish that a screening programme works.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Diarrhoeal disease, enteric fever, hepatitis & helminths ~ PSM_DIA ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Epidemiology of Communicable Diseases
E ~ Which intervention has the greatest impact on reducing diarrhoeal mortality in children at a population level? ~ Oral rehydration therapy with zinc, safe water and sanitation, and exclusive breastfeeding ~ Routine antibiotics ~ Antimotility agents ~ Intravenous fluids for all cases ~ A ~ Rotavirus vaccination now adds to this package, and the combination addresses transmission, susceptibility and case fatality simultaneously.
E ~ Under the National Deworming Day programme, albendazole is given to which age group and at what interval? ~ Children and adolescents aged 1 to 19 years, twice yearly in high-prevalence areas ~ Only children under 5, annually ~ Adults over 20, twice yearly ~ Pregnant women only ~ A ~ Mass drug administration through schools and Anganwadi centres reduces worm burden, anaemia and school absenteeism, and does not require individual stool testing.
E ~ Which hepatitis viruses are transmitted by the faecal-oral route and cause water-borne epidemics? ~ Hepatitis A and hepatitis E ~ Hepatitis B and hepatitis C ~ Hepatitis C and hepatitis D ~ Hepatitis B and hepatitis E ~ A ~ Both are prevented by safe water, sanitation and food hygiene, neither causes chronic infection, and hepatitis E is notable for high mortality in pregnancy.
M ~ Which sanitation approach underlies the Swachh Bharat Mission's impact on diarrhoeal disease? ~ Elimination of open defaecation with community-led total sanitation and behaviour change ~ Provision of piped water alone ~ Chlorination of surface water only ~ Distribution of oral rehydration salts ~ A ~ Interrupting the faecal-oral route at source addresses the whole family of enteric infections including cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A and E and soil-transmitted helminths, which no single vaccine can do.
M ~ Which case definition is used for the epidemiological surveillance of cholera during an outbreak? ~ Acute watery diarrhoea with severe dehydration or death in a person aged 5 years or more, with laboratory confirmation in a proportion of cases ~ Any loose stool in any age group ~ Bloody diarrhoea with fever ~ Diarrhoea lasting over 14 days ~ A ~ Case definitions are deliberately simple so that they can be applied without laboratory support at the point of care, with confirmation of a sample of cases establishing the aetiology.
M ~ Typhoid conjugate vaccine differs from the older vaccines in that it: ~ Can be given from 6 months of age, gives longer protection and confers herd effect ~ Is a live oral vaccine ~ Requires annual boosting ~ Protects against paratyphoid also ~ A ~ Conjugation of the Vi polysaccharide to a carrier protein makes it immunogenic in infants and generates memory, which is why WHO recommends its introduction in high-burden settings.
M ~ Which measure best controls the spread of hepatitis A in a residential institution during an outbreak? ~ Hand hygiene, safe water and food handling, with vaccination or immunoglobulin for contacts ~ Isolation for 6 weeks ~ Mass antibiotic prophylaxis ~ Closure of the institution ~ A ~ Faecal shedding is greatest before jaundice appears, so by the time cases are recognised transmission has already occurred, making environmental measures and post-exposure immunisation the priority.
H ~ Why does improving water quality alone often fail to reduce diarrhoeal disease as much as expected? ~ Transmission also occurs through food, hands, flies and soil, so a single barrier leaves other routes intact ~ Water is rarely contaminated ~ Diarrhoea is mostly non-infective ~ Water quality cannot be measured ~ A ~ The F-diagram of fluids, fields, flies, fingers and food shows multiple pathways, so combining safe water with sanitation and hygiene gives far greater benefit than any one element.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Environmental health: air, noise, housing, climate ~ PSM_ENV ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Environment and Health
E ~ Which indoor air pollutant from biomass cooking fuel is most responsible for the burden of childhood pneumonia and chronic lung disease in rural India? ~ Fine particulate matter of 2.5 micrometres or less ~ Ozone ~ Sulphur dioxide ~ Radon ~ A ~ Particles of this size reach the alveoli and cause both acute infection risk and chronic inflammation, which is the rationale for clean cooking fuel programmes such as Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana.
E ~ Which pollutant is measured as an indicator of vehicular exhaust and causes methaemoglobinaemia at high exposure? ~ Oxides of nitrogen ~ Carbon dioxide ~ Methane ~ Chlorofluorocarbon ~ A ~ Nitrogen dioxide is a respiratory irritant that also contributes to photochemical smog, and carbon monoxide from the same source causes tissue hypoxia by binding haemoglobin with high affinity.
E ~ Continuous exposure above which sound level is generally accepted as causing occupational hearing loss over years? ~ 85 decibels for an 8-hour working day ~ 40 decibels ~ 60 decibels ~ 130 decibels only ~ A ~ Risk rises with both level and duration, so halving exposure time permits a 3-decibel higher level, and hearing conservation programmes combine engineering control, personal protection and audiometric surveillance.
M ~ Which housing standard is recommended to prevent overcrowding, expressed as floor area per person? ~ At least 50 to 100 square feet per person with no more than two persons per room for adults ~ 10 square feet per person ~ 200 square feet per person ~ There is no accepted standard ~ A ~ Overcrowding facilitates droplet-spread infection such as tuberculosis, measles and meningococcal disease, and standards also address ventilation, lighting and separation of the kitchen from the living space.
M ~ Which measure of ventilation adequacy is used for a living room, and what is the recommended value? ~ Air changes per hour, with 2 to 3 changes recommended for a living room ~ Cubic metres of room volume ~ Window area alone ~ Number of occupants ~ A ~ Ventilation dilutes carbon dioxide, water vapour, odours and pathogens, and requirements rise with occupancy and activity, being much higher for a hospital ward or an operating theatre.
M ~ Which greenhouse gas contributes most to anthropogenic global warming by total effect? ~ Carbon dioxide ~ Methane ~ Nitrous oxide ~ Sulphur hexafluoride ~ A ~ Methane and the fluorinated gases have far higher warming potential per molecule but far lower atmospheric concentration, so carbon dioxide dominates the total forcing, which is why energy policy is central to mitigation.
M ~ Which health effect of climate change is most directly mediated by changes in vector distribution? ~ Expansion of dengue and malaria transmission into new altitudes and latitudes ~ Increased road traffic injury ~ Rising rates of congenital anomaly ~ Increased dental caries ~ A ~ Temperature and rainfall determine vector breeding, survival and biting rate as well as the extrinsic incubation period of the pathogen, so surveillance and vector control must adapt as the climate shifts.
H ~ Why is the air quality index used for public communication rather than raw pollutant concentrations? ~ It converts multiple pollutants onto a single colour-coded health-risk scale reported against the worst-performing pollutant ~ It is more accurate than measurement ~ It measures indoor air only ~ It replaces the need for monitoring stations ~ A ~ Because the index is driven by whichever pollutant is worst relative to its standard, a good index value does not guarantee that every pollutant is low, which is a common misinterpretation.
@ PSM (Community Medicine) ~ Health care delivery: PHC/CHC, HWC, ASHA & IPHS ~ PSM_HCD ~ Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine 27e, Ch. Health Care of the Community
E ~ Under current norms, one primary health centre serves a population of approximately: ~ 30000 in plains and 20000 in hilly, tribal and difficult areas ~ 5000 in plains ~ 100000 in plains ~ 10000 everywhere ~ A ~ A sub-centre or health and wellness centre serves 5000 in plains and 3000 in difficult areas, and a community health centre serves 120000 and 80000 respectively, forming the three-tier rural structure.
E ~ Which cadre is the village-level volunteer link between the community and the public health system under the National Health Mission? ~ Accredited Social Health Activist ~ Auxiliary Nurse Midwife ~ Multipurpose Health Worker male ~ Anganwadi Worker ~ A ~ The ASHA is selected by and accountable to the community, works on performance-based incentives, and her core functions are mobilisation, escorting for institutional delivery, home-based newborn care and being a depot holder.
E ~ A community health centre is expected to be staffed with which minimum specialist complement? ~ Four specialists including a surgeon, physician, obstetrician-gynaecologist and paediatrician, with 30 beds ~ Two medical officers only ~ One medical officer and one nurse ~ Six specialists with 100 beds ~ A ~ The community health centre functions as the first referral unit, so operating theatre, laboratory, X-ray and blood storage facilities accompany the specialist posts under Indian Public Health Standards.
M ~ Health and wellness centres under Ayushman Bharat differ from the earlier sub-centre in that they: ~ Deliver an expanded package of twelve services including non-communicable disease screening, mental health and elderly care, led by a mid-level provider ~ Provide only maternal and child health ~ Replace community health centres ~ Function only in urban areas ~ A ~ The community health officer, a nurse or Ayurveda practitioner with bridge training, leads the team, shifting the system from selective to comprehensive primary health care.
M ~ What is the purpose of the Indian Public Health Standards? ~ To define minimum assured standards of infrastructure, staffing, drugs and services at each level of care for accountability and monitoring ~ To set fees for services ~ To regulate private hospitals ~ To license medical colleges ~ A ~ By making the expected inputs explicit, the standards allow gap analysis and provide the basis for facility certification such as the National Quality Assurance Standards.
M ~ Which committee recommended the integration of curative and preventive services and the creation of primary health centres, forming the blueprint of India's health system? ~ Bhore Committee, 1946 ~ Mudaliar Committee, 1962 ~ Kartar Singh Committee, 1973 ~ Srivastava Committee, 1975 ~ A ~ The Bhore Committee proposed the three-million and short-term plans with primary health centres and the social physician concept, while Mudaliar reviewed progress, Kartar Singh created the multipurpose worker and Srivastava proposed community health volunteers.
M ~ Which principle of primary health care as declared at Alma-Ata is most relevant to the ASHA programme? ~ Community participation, with local people involved in planning and delivering their own health care ~ Vertical disease control ~ Specialist-led care ~ Fee-for-service financing ~ A ~ The four pillars of the Alma-Ata declaration are equitable distribution, community participation, intersectoral coordination and appropriate technology, and the ASHA embodies the second and fourth of these.
H ~ Why does the referral chain frequently fail in practice even when facilities exist at each level? ~ Bypassing of lower levels because of perceived poor quality, absent staff, drug stock-outs and transport barriers ~ Patients are unaware that hospitals exist ~ There are too many specialists ~ Referral is prohibited by regulation ~ A ~ Strengthening confidence in the first level through assured drugs, diagnostics and staff presence is what makes a gatekeeping system work, which is the logic behind health and wellness centres and free diagnostics initiatives.
