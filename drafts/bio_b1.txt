@ Biochemistry ~ Cancer biology, oncogenes & tumour biochemistry ~ BC_CAN ~ Harper's Illustrated Biochemistry 32e, Ch. Cancer: An Overview
M ~ A child has an abdominal lymphoma whose cells show a translocation placing MYC next to the immunoglobulin heavy chain locus. Which mechanism activates the oncogene here? ~ Point mutation of the coding sequence ~ Gene amplification producing many copies ~ Translocation placing MYC under a strong constitutive promoter, so it is overexpressed ~ Deletion of one allele ~ C ~ Burkitt lymphoma carries t(8;14), and the same oncogene can be activated by different mechanisms in different tumours, amplification of MYCN in neuroblastoma being the classic amplification example.
M ~ Why does retinoblastoma occur earlier and bilaterally in the inherited form than in the sporadic form? ~ The inherited form involves an oncogene requiring one mutation ~ One RB1 allele is already mutated in every cell, so a single further somatic hit suffices ~ The inherited form has faster cell division ~ Both alleles are inherited mutated ~ B ~ This is the Knudson two-hit hypothesis, and because tumour suppressor loss is recessive at the cellular level, the second hit is what determines tumour onset.
E ~ A patient with chronic myeloid leukaemia has the Philadelphia chromosome. Which fusion protein results and which drug targets it? ~ BCR-ABL, a constitutively active tyrosine kinase inhibited by imatinib ~ PML-RARA, targeted by all-trans retinoic acid ~ EWS-FLI1, targeted by chemotherapy ~ HER2 amplification, targeted by trastuzumab ~ A ~ The t(9;22) fusion drives unregulated proliferation independent of growth factor signalling, and its selective inhibition is the founding example of targeted molecular therapy.
E ~ Which receptor, when amplified in breast cancer, predicts response to a specific monoclonal antibody? ~ Oestrogen receptor, predicting response to trastuzumab ~ HER2 (ERBB2), predicting response to trastuzumab ~ Progesterone receptor, predicting response to imatinib ~ Epidermal growth factor receptor, predicting response to tamoxifen ~ B ~ Amplification is tested by immunohistochemistry with fluorescence in situ hybridisation confirmation, and the same tumour is also assessed for hormone receptors because those predict response to endocrine therapy instead.
H ~ How does the retinoblastoma protein normally restrain the cell cycle, and how is that restraint lifted? ~ It degrades cyclins directly ~ It binds and sequesters E2F transcription factors until cyclin-dependent kinase phosphorylation releases them at the G1 to S checkpoint ~ It blocks DNA polymerase at the S phase ~ It activates caspases in G2 ~ B ~ Cyclin D with CDK4 and CDK6 drives that phosphorylation, which is why cyclin D amplification, CDK4 mutation and loss of the inhibitor p16 all mimic loss of the protein itself.
H ~ A family has early colorectal and endometrial cancers, and the tumours show microsatellite instability. Which repair pathway is defective? ~ Nucleotide excision repair, as in xeroderma pigmentosum ~ Base excision repair ~ DNA mismatch repair, involving MLH1 or MSH2 ~ Non-homologous end joining ~ C ~ Lynch syndrome tumours accumulate errors at repetitive sequences because slipped strands go uncorrected, and this same instability also predicts response to immune checkpoint inhibition.
E ~ Which tumour marker is most useful in monitoring a treated epithelial ovarian carcinoma? ~ CA 19-9 ~ Carcinoembryonic antigen ~ CA 125 ~ Alpha-fetoprotein ~ C ~ It is unsuitable for screening because it rises in endometriosis, pregnancy, cirrhosis and peritoneal inflammation, so its real value is in following a known cancer during and after treatment.
M ~ Which class of enzymes allows a carcinoma to breach the basement membrane and invade stroma? ~ Matrix metalloproteinases, which are zinc-dependent ~ Topoisomerases ~ Telomerases ~ Protein kinases ~ A ~ Invasion also requires loss of E-cadherin-mediated adhesion and increased integrin-mediated motility, so degradation of the extracellular matrix is only one step of the metastatic cascade.
@ Biochemistry ~ Carbohydrate chemistry, glycogen metabolism & HMP shunt ~ BC_CHO ~ Vasudevan Textbook of Biochemistry for Medical Students 9e, Ch. Metabolism of Carbohydrates
M ~ A child has hepatomegaly, fasting hypoglycaemia and a limit dextrin accumulating in liver and muscle. Which enzyme is deficient? ~ Glucose-6-phosphatase ~ Muscle phosphorylase ~ Debranching enzyme, giving Cori disease ~ Branching enzyme ~ C ~ Because only the outer chains can be removed, glycogen with short outer branches accumulates, and the hypoglycaemia is milder than in von Gierke disease since gluconeogenesis remains intact.
H ~ A neonate has cirrhosis with an abnormal glycogen resembling amylopectin with very long unbranched chains. Which enzyme is deficient and what is the prognosis? ~ Branching enzyme deficiency, Andersen disease, which is usually fatal in early childhood ~ Debranching enzyme deficiency, which is mild ~ Acid maltase deficiency, which spares the liver ~ Phosphorylase kinase deficiency, which is benign ~ A ~ The abnormal polysaccharide is poorly soluble and provokes a foreign-body reaction in tissue, so the disease is one of structural glycogen abnormality rather than of impaired glucose release.
E ~ Which activated nucleotide sugar donates glucose residues during glycogen synthesis? ~ ADP-glucose ~ UDP-glucose ~ GDP-glucose ~ CDP-glucose ~ B ~ Glycogen synthase transfers glucose from UDP-glucose to the growing alpha-1,4 chain, and glycogenin serves as the protein primer on which the first residues are laid down.
M ~ Which enzyme of the hexose monophosphate shunt requires thiamine pyrophosphate, and how is this used clinically? ~ Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, measured in haemolysis ~ Transketolase, whose erythrocyte activity assesses thiamine status ~ Transaldolase, measured in beriberi ~ 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, measured in pellagra ~ B ~ Activation of the enzyme by adding thiamine pyrophosphate in vitro gives the activity coefficient, a rise indicating deficiency, and the same vitamin is a cofactor for pyruvate and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase.
E ~ Which process explains the formation of glycated haemoglobin in a diabetic patient? ~ Enzymatic glycosylation by a glycosyltransferase ~ Non-enzymatic attachment of glucose to the terminal valine of the beta chain ~ Oxidation of the haem iron ~ Proteolysis of the beta chain ~ B ~ Because the reaction is non-enzymatic and irreversible over the life of the red cell, the level reflects average glycaemia over roughly 8 to 12 weeks and is falsely low when red cell survival is shortened.
H ~ A young man has a persistently positive urine reducing sugar test with normal blood glucose, no symptoms and no treatment needed. Which enzyme is deficient? ~ Aldolase B, causing fructose intolerance ~ Fructokinase, causing essential fructosuria ~ Galactokinase, causing galactosaemia ~ Sucrase-isomaltase ~ B ~ Fructose accumulates and is excreted harmlessly, whereas aldolase B deficiency traps fructose-1-phosphate and sequesters phosphate, producing hypoglycaemia, vomiting and liver failure after fructose ingestion.
E ~ Which linkages form the linear chains and the branch points of glycogen? ~ Alpha-1,4 in the chains and alpha-1,6 at the branch points ~ Beta-1,4 in the chains and beta-1,6 at branches ~ Alpha-1,6 in the chains and alpha-1,4 at branches ~ Alpha-1,4 throughout with no branches ~ A ~ Branching every 8 to 10 residues multiplies the non-reducing ends available to phosphorylase, which is what allows rapid mobilisation of glucose from the polymer.
M ~ Which pathway explains cataract and neuropathy developing in poorly controlled diabetes through osmotic injury? ~ The glucuronic acid pathway ~ The polyol pathway, in which aldose reductase converts glucose to sorbitol ~ The hexose monophosphate shunt ~ The uronic acid pathway ~ B ~ Sorbitol is trapped intracellularly because the tissues lack sorbitol dehydrogenase, drawing in water osmotically, and the reaction also consumes NADPH which weakens glutathione-dependent antioxidant defence.
@ Biochemistry ~ Enzymes: kinetics, regulation & clinical enzymology ~ BC_ENZ ~ Vasudevan Textbook of Biochemistry for Medical Students 9e, Ch. Enzymology
M ~ How does a pure non-competitive inhibitor alter the kinetic constants of an enzyme? ~ Vmax falls while Km is unchanged ~ Vmax is unchanged while Km rises ~ Both Vmax and Km rise ~ Vmax rises while Km falls ~ A ~ The inhibitor binds a site distinct from the active site so substrate cannot outcompete it, whereas a competitive inhibitor raises the apparent Km and can be overcome by more substrate.
M ~ What advantage does the Lineweaver-Burk double reciprocal plot offer over the direct Michaelis-Menten curve? ~ It gives a straight line so Vmax and Km can be read from the intercepts ~ It removes the need for substrate measurement ~ It measures enzyme concentration directly ~ It distinguishes isoenzymes ~ A ~ The intercept on the y axis gives 1 over Vmax and that on the x axis gives minus 1 over Km, and the pattern of intersecting lines is what distinguishes competitive from non-competitive inhibition.
H ~ Allopurinol is described as a suicide inhibitor of xanthine oxidase. What does this mean? ~ It binds reversibly at an allosteric site ~ It is converted by the enzyme into a product that then inactivates the enzyme irreversibly ~ It competes with the substrate but dissociates rapidly ~ It denatures the enzyme by altering pH ~ B ~ Oxypurinol formed by the enzyme remains tightly bound, so the inhibition is mechanism-based, and aspirin acting on cyclooxygenase by acetylation is the other standard example of irreversible inactivation.
M ~ A patient treated for myocardial infarction develops fresh chest pain on day 4. Which marker best identifies reinfarction and why? ~ Troponin I, because it stays raised for 10 days ~ CK-MB, because its shorter half-life allows a new rise to be detected against a falling baseline ~ Lactate dehydrogenase, because it peaks late ~ Myoglobin, because it is cardiac specific ~ B ~ Troponin remains the most sensitive and specific marker for the initial diagnosis, but because it is still elevated on day 4 a further rise is hard to interpret, so the marker that has already normalised is more informative.
E ~ A raised serum angiotensin-converting enzyme level supports which diagnosis? ~ Sarcoidosis ~ Acute pancreatitis ~ Paget disease of bone ~ Prostatic carcinoma ~ A ~ The enzyme is produced by the epithelioid cells of granulomas so it broadly reflects granuloma burden, but it is neither sensitive nor specific enough to diagnose or exclude the disease on its own.
E ~ What is the relationship between an apoenzyme, a cofactor and a holoenzyme? ~ The apoenzyme is the inactive protein, and with its cofactor it forms the catalytically active holoenzyme ~ The holoenzyme is the protein alone ~ The cofactor is the protein part ~ The apoenzyme is the active form ~ A ~ Tightly bound organic cofactors are called prosthetic groups, as with haem in catalase, whereas loosely associated ones such as NAD act as cosubstrates and are regenerated elsewhere.
M ~ Which mechanism of enzyme regulation acts within seconds and is reversed by a phosphatase? ~ Induction of gene transcription ~ Covalent modification by phosphorylation ~ Proteolytic activation of a zymogen ~ Feedback repression ~ B ~ Glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase are reciprocally controlled this way by the cyclic AMP cascade, which is how a hormonal signal at the cell surface is amplified into a rapid metabolic switch.
H ~ A patient remains apnoeic for hours after suxamethonium during anaesthesia. Which enzyme abnormality explains this and how is it assessed? ~ Deficiency of plasma pseudocholinesterase, assessed by the dibucaine number ~ Deficiency of acetylcholinesterase at the endplate ~ Deficiency of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase ~ Deficiency of catechol-O-methyltransferase ~ A ~ The dibucaine number reports the quality rather than the quantity of the enzyme, so it distinguishes an inherited atypical variant from an acquired reduction due to liver disease, malnutrition or pregnancy.
@ Biochemistry ~ Gene regulation & molecular techniques (PCR, blotting) ~ BC_GEN ~ Harper's Illustrated Biochemistry 32e, Ch. Molecular Genetics and Recombinant DNA
E ~ Which blotting technique is used to detect a specific DNA sequence? ~ Northern blotting ~ Southern blotting ~ Western blotting ~ South-western blotting ~ B ~ The mnemonic follows the target, with Northern for RNA and Western for protein, and all three depend on separation by electrophoresis, transfer to a membrane and detection with a labelled probe or antibody.
E ~ Which technique confirms a positive HIV screening test by detecting antibodies against specific viral proteins? ~ Southern blotting ~ Western blotting ~ Northern blotting ~ Polymerase chain reaction for antibody ~ B ~ Viral proteins separated by size are probed with the patient serum, so the pattern of bands rather than a single signal is what distinguishes a true positive from the cross-reactivity that causes false positive screening results.
M ~ Which modification of the polymerase chain reaction allows an RNA virus such as hepatitis C to be detected? ~ Nested PCR using two primer pairs ~ Reverse transcription PCR, in which RNA is first copied into complementary DNA ~ Multiplex PCR using several primer pairs ~ Real-time PCR with a fluorescent probe ~ B ~ Taq polymerase cannot use RNA as a template, so reverse transcriptase must generate complementary DNA first, and combining this with real-time detection gives the quantitative viral load used to monitor treatment.
M ~ Which principle underlies Sanger dideoxy sequencing? ~ Chain termination by incorporation of a dideoxynucleotide lacking the 3-prime hydroxyl group ~ Cleavage of DNA at specific bases by chemical reagents ~ Hybridisation to an array of oligonucleotides ~ Measurement of released pyrophosphate ~ A ~ The absent hydroxyl group prevents formation of the next phosphodiester bond, so a nested set of fragments of differing length is produced and their separation reveals the sequence.
H ~ How does attenuation regulate the tryptophan operon in addition to repression? ~ Ribosome stalling at tryptophan codons in the leader sequence alters formation of a terminator hairpin, so transcription stops when tryptophan is plentiful ~ The repressor binds the operator only when tryptophan is absent ~ The promoter is methylated when tryptophan is high ~ The messenger RNA is degraded by a specific nuclease ~ A ~ This second layer of control couples transcription to the actual charging of transfer RNA, giving a graded response, and it contrasts with the lac operon which is inducible rather than repressible.
M ~ Which technique detects a submicroscopic chromosomal deletion such as that of 22q11 in DiGeorge syndrome? ~ Conventional karyotyping ~ Fluorescence in situ hybridisation with a locus-specific probe ~ Northern blotting ~ Two-dimensional gel electrophoresis ~ B ~ Standard karyotyping resolves only changes of several megabases, whereas a labelled probe to the critical region shows a signal on one chromosome and none on the other, and chromosomal microarray gives a genome-wide equivalent.
H ~ How does a microRNA regulate gene expression? ~ It binds the promoter to block transcription initiation ~ It base-pairs with the 3-prime untranslated region of a target messenger RNA, causing translational repression or degradation ~ It methylates histones at the target locus ~ It is translated into a small regulatory peptide ~ B ~ Because a single species can regulate many transcripts, loss or overexpression of one microRNA can shift a whole programme, which is why some act functionally as oncogenes and others as tumour suppressors.
E ~ Why is sodium dodecyl sulphate included when separating proteins by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis? ~ It denatures the proteins and confers uniform negative charge so migration depends on size ~ It cross-links the proteins into complexes ~ It stains the protein bands ~ It maintains the native folded structure ~ A ~ Migration therefore estimates molecular weight against standards, whereas isoelectric focusing separates by charge instead and combining the two gives the two-dimensional maps used in proteomics.
