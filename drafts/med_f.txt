@ Medicine ~ Pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension & sleep apnoea ~ MED_PE ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 273 (Deep Venous Thrombosis and Pulmonary Thromboembolism)
E ~ Which anticoagulant is preferred for pulmonary embolism in a patient with active gastrointestinal malignancy? ~ Warfarin with a target INR of 2 to 3 ~ Low molecular weight heparin or a direct oral anticoagulant with careful bleeding assessment ~ Aspirin ~ Unfractionated heparin indefinitely ~ B ~ Cancer-associated thrombosis recurs more often on warfarin, so low molecular weight heparin was long standard; direct oral anticoagulants are now equivalent overall but carry more mucosal bleeding in luminal gastrointestinal and urothelial tumours.
E ~ The recommended duration of anticoagulation after a first pulmonary embolism provoked by major surgery is: ~ Three months ~ Six weeks ~ Twelve months ~ Lifelong ~ A ~ A transient major provoking factor that has resolved gives a low recurrence risk, so three months suffices; unprovoked or persistently provoked events warrant extended therapy with periodic reassessment.
E ~ Which finding on arterial blood gas is most typical of acute pulmonary embolism? ~ Hypoxaemia with hypocapnia and a raised alveolar-arterial gradient ~ Hypoxaemia with hypercapnia ~ Normal gases with a metabolic alkalosis ~ Isolated respiratory acidosis ~ A ~ Dead space ventilation and hyperventilation lower carbon dioxide while ventilation-perfusion mismatch widens the gradient; a normal gas result does not exclude embolism, which is why imaging is decided by clinical probability.
M ~ In suspected pulmonary embolism with renal impairment precluding contrast, the most appropriate imaging is: ~ Ventilation-perfusion scintigraphy ~ Repeat D-dimer ~ Chest radiograph alone ~ Coronary angiography ~ A ~ A perfusion or ventilation-perfusion scan avoids iodinated contrast and performs well when the chest radiograph is normal, which is usual in pulmonary embolism without coexisting lung disease.
M ~ Which echocardiographic finding identifies intermediate-risk (submassive) pulmonary embolism in a normotensive patient? ~ Left ventricular hypertrophy ~ Right ventricular dilatation and hypokinesis with septal flattening ~ Mitral valve prolapse ~ Pericardial effusion ~ B ~ Right ventricular strain plus a raised troponin defines intermediate-high risk, which warrants monitored anticoagulation with rescue thrombolysis if deterioration occurs rather than routine primary thrombolysis.
M ~ Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension should be suspected when: ~ Breathlessness and pulmonary hypertension persist beyond 3 months of adequate anticoagulation after pulmonary embolism ~ D-dimer remains detectable at one week ~ The initial embolism was small ~ There is a family history of asthma ~ A ~ Persisting organised thrombus with vascular remodelling affects a few per cent of survivors, is diagnosed by ventilation-perfusion scanning and pulmonary angiography, and is potentially curable by pulmonary endarterectomy.
M ~ Which class of drug is used in group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension but is harmful in pulmonary hypertension due to left heart disease? ~ Diuretics ~ Pulmonary vasodilators such as endothelin receptor antagonists and phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors ~ Beta blockers ~ Statins ~ B ~ Selective pulmonary vasodilatation increases flow into a congested left atrium, precipitating pulmonary oedema, so these agents are confined to group 1 disease after right heart catheterisation confirms the classification.
H ~ A patient with obstructive sleep apnoea develops daytime hypercapnia and a raised bicarbonate with a body mass index of 45. This indicates: ~ Obesity hypoventilation syndrome ~ Simple snoring ~ Central sleep apnoea ~ Narcolepsy ~ A ~ Awake hypercapnia with obesity and sleep-disordered breathing defines obesity hypoventilation, which carries much higher mortality than obstructive apnoea alone and often needs bilevel rather than continuous positive airway pressure.
H ~ Which pattern on polysomnography distinguishes central from obstructive sleep apnoea? ~ Cessation of airflow with continuing thoracoabdominal effort ~ Cessation of airflow with absent thoracoabdominal effort ~ Reduced airflow with snoring ~ Periodic limb movements ~ B ~ Central events reflect loss of respiratory drive so no effort is made, whereas obstructive events show paradoxical effort against a closed upper airway; central apnoea suggests heart failure, opioids or high altitude.
@ Medicine ~ Rheumatoid arthritis, SLE & connective tissue disease ~ MED_RA ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 358 (Rheumatoid Arthritis)
E ~ Which extra-articular manifestation of rheumatoid arthritis is the commonest cause of death attributable to the disease itself? ~ Rheumatoid nodules ~ Accelerated atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease ~ Scleritis ~ Amyloidosis ~ B ~ Chronic systemic inflammation roughly doubles cardiovascular risk independent of traditional factors, so risk-factor modification and tight disease control are part of standard rheumatoid care.
E ~ Before starting methotrexate, which baseline investigations are essential? ~ Full blood count, liver function and creatinine, with a chest radiograph ~ Serum uric acid only ~ Echocardiogram ~ Bone densitometry ~ A ~ Methotrexate causes myelosuppression, transaminitis and rarely pneumonitis, and folic acid supplementation with quarterly monitoring reduces toxicity; the baseline film helps interpret later respiratory symptoms.
E ~ Which joints are characteristically spared in rheumatoid arthritis? ~ Metacarpophalangeal joints ~ The distal interphalangeal joints and the thoracolumbar spine ~ Wrists ~ Metatarsophalangeal joints ~ B ~ Sparing of the distal interphalangeal joints separates rheumatoid arthritis from psoriatic arthritis and osteoarthritis, and only the cervical spine is involved, where atlantoaxial subluxation is a preoperative anaesthetic concern.
M ~ A patient with rheumatoid arthritis is to start a tumour necrosis factor inhibitor. Which screening is mandatory first? ~ Screening for latent tuberculosis and hepatitis B ~ Colonoscopy ~ Thyroid antibodies ~ Serum electrophoresis ~ A ~ Tumour necrosis factor is essential for granuloma integrity, so blockade reactivates latent tuberculosis; interferon-gamma release assay or tuberculin testing with a chest radiograph, plus hepatitis serology, precedes therapy.
M ~ Which pattern of renal involvement in systemic lupus erythematosus most requires urgent immunosuppression? ~ Class II mesangial proliferative ~ Class IV diffuse proliferative with active urinary sediment ~ Class I minimal mesangial ~ Class VI advanced sclerosing ~ B ~ Diffuse proliferative disease has the worst renal prognosis and responds to induction with mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide plus corticosteroids, whereas class VI represents burnt-out scarring where immunosuppression cannot help.
M ~ Which antibody in systemic lupus erythematosus is associated with neonatal lupus and congenital heart block? ~ Anti-Sm ~ Anti-Ro (SSA) ~ Anti-dsDNA ~ Anti-ribosomal P ~ B ~ Anti-Ro crosses the placenta and damages the fetal conduction system between 18 and 24 weeks, so affected pregnancies need serial fetal echocardiography and hydroxychloroquine reduces recurrence.
M ~ A patient with systemic sclerosis develops abrupt severe hypertension with acute kidney injury and a microangiopathic blood picture. The treatment of choice is: ~ Intravenous corticosteroids ~ An ACE inhibitor such as captopril, titrated rapidly ~ Cyclophosphamide ~ Plasma exchange ~ B ~ Scleroderma renal crisis is driven by renin release from ischaemic juxtaglomerular apparatus, and ACE inhibition transformed a once-fatal complication; corticosteroids actually increase the risk of precipitating it.
H ~ Which autoantibody in systemic sclerosis predicts the greatest risk of interstitial lung disease and diffuse cutaneous involvement? ~ Anti-centromere antibody ~ Anti-topoisomerase I (anti-Scl-70) antibody ~ Anti-RNA polymerase III antibody ~ Anti-U1 RNP antibody ~ B ~ Anti-Scl-70 marks diffuse disease with pulmonary fibrosis, anti-centromere marks limited disease with pulmonary arterial hypertension, and anti-RNA polymerase III marks a high risk of renal crisis and occult malignancy.
H ~ Mixed connective tissue disease is characterised by which antibody together with overlapping features of lupus, myositis and scleroderma? ~ Anti-U1 RNP antibody ~ Anti-Jo-1 antibody ~ Anti-Mi-2 antibody ~ Anti-histone antibody ~ A ~ High-titre anti-U1 RNP with Raynaud phenomenon, puffy hands, arthritis, myositis and oesophageal dysmotility defines the syndrome, whose principal cause of death is pulmonary arterial hypertension.
@ Medicine ~ Sepsis, fever of unknown origin & antimicrobial stewardship ~ MED_SEP ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 297 (Sepsis and Septic Shock)
E ~ In the initial resuscitation of septic shock, the recommended crystalloid volume within the first three hours is at least: ~ 10 mL/kg ~ 30 mL/kg ~ 60 mL/kg ~ 5 mL/kg ~ B ~ The Surviving Sepsis guidance recommends 30 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid for sepsis-induced hypoperfusion, with further fluid guided by dynamic measures of responsiveness rather than fixed targets.
E ~ Which biomarker trend is most useful for guiding safe de-escalation of antibiotics in sepsis? ~ Serial C-reactive protein ~ Serial procalcitonin ~ Serial lactate alone ~ Serial white cell count ~ B ~ Procalcitonin falls quickly when bacterial infection resolves and trials show that procalcitonin-guided stopping shortens antibiotic courses without increasing mortality, whereas C-reactive protein is slower and less specific.
E ~ Which is the most appropriate approach when blood cultures identify a single organism sensitive to a narrow-spectrum agent? ~ Continue the broad empirical combination to be safe ~ De-escalate to the narrowest effective agent for the shortest effective duration ~ Add a second agent for synergy in all cases ~ Switch to a newer broad-spectrum agent ~ B ~ De-escalation is the core of stewardship, reducing resistance, cost, Clostridioides difficile infection and drug toxicity without compromising outcome once the organism and its sensitivities are known.
M ~ A patient meets criteria for sepsis but the lactate is normal and blood pressure is maintained. The most important immediate action is: ~ Withhold antibiotics until cultures return ~ Obtain cultures and give appropriate antibiotics within one hour while seeking source control ~ Give vasopressors ~ Arrange elective imaging next day ~ B ~ Every hour of delay in effective antibiotics increases mortality, and identifying and draining or removing the source is at least as important as the drug choice.
M ~ Which vasopressor is added when noradrenaline alone fails to reach the mean arterial pressure target in septic shock? ~ Dopamine ~ Vasopressin ~ Phenylephrine ~ Dobutamine ~ B ~ Vasopressin acts on V1 receptors independently of adrenergic pathways, so it is noradrenaline-sparing; dopamine causes more tachyarrhythmia and dobutamine is an inotrope for persistent hypoperfusion despite adequate pressure.
M ~ Which category of fever of unknown origin should prompt evaluation for drug-induced fever? ~ Fever with a new rash and eosinophilia in a patient on multiple recently started drugs ~ Fever with weight loss over months ~ Fever with a heart murmur ~ Fever with hepatosplenomegaly ~ A ~ Drug fever often appears one to three weeks after starting the agent, may be accompanied by rash and eosinophilia, and characteristically settles within 72 hours of stopping the culprit, so a careful drug chart review is mandatory.
M ~ Which imaging modality has the highest yield in classical fever of unknown origin after initial tests are unrevealing? ~ Plain radiography ~ FDG positron emission tomography with CT ~ Barium studies ~ Skull radiography ~ B ~ Fluorodeoxyglucose uptake highlights occult infection, large-vessel vasculitis and malignancy in a single whole-body study and directs biopsy, giving a diagnostic contribution in roughly half of unexplained cases.
H ~ Which finding most strongly suggests adult-onset Still disease as a cause of fever of unknown origin? ~ Quotidian fever with an evanescent salmon-pink rash, arthritis and a very high serum ferritin ~ Continuous fever with rigors ~ Fever with lymphocytosis ~ Fever with a low erythrocyte sedimentation rate ~ A ~ A ferritin many times normal with a low glycosylated fraction, neutrophilic leucocytosis and the transient rash appearing with fever spikes is characteristic, and it is a diagnosis of exclusion requiring infection and malignancy to be ruled out.
H ~ In sepsis, why is a balanced crystalloid generally preferred to 0.9 percent saline for large-volume resuscitation? ~ It contains more sodium ~ It causes less hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis and possibly less renal injury ~ It expands plasma volume more than saline ~ It is bacteriostatic ~ B ~ The high chloride load of normal saline reduces renal blood flow and causes a hyperchloraemic acidosis, and trials such as SMART showed fewer major adverse kidney events with balanced solutions.
@ Medicine ~ Spondyloarthritis, gout & the vasculitides ~ MED_SPA ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 362 (The Spondyloarthritides)
E ~ Which feature best distinguishes inflammatory from mechanical back pain? ~ Pain worse on exertion and relieved by rest ~ Morning stiffness over 30 minutes with improvement on exercise and night pain in the second half of the night ~ Pain radiating below the knee ~ Onset after age 50 ~ B ~ Inflammatory back pain begins before 45, improves with movement and worsens with rest, and this pattern with sacroiliitis on imaging or HLA-B27 supports axial spondyloarthritis.
E ~ The first-line pharmacological treatment for axial spondyloarthritis is: ~ Regular NSAIDs with a structured exercise programme ~ Methotrexate ~ Oral corticosteroids ~ Hydroxychloroquine ~ A ~ Unlike peripheral arthritis, axial disease does not respond to conventional disease-modifying drugs; NSAIDs plus physiotherapy come first, and biologics targeting tumour necrosis factor or interleukin-17 follow if response is inadequate.
E ~ Which radiographic change is characteristic of established ankylosing spondylitis of the spine? ~ Osteophytes at the vertebral margins ~ Symmetrical syndesmophytes producing a bamboo spine with squaring of vertebral bodies ~ Vertebral collapse ~ Disc space calcification alone ~ B ~ Vertically oriented syndesmophytes arise from ossification of the annulus fibrosus, which is different from the horizontal, bulky osteophytes of spondylosis and from the chunky asymmetrical syndesmophytes of psoriatic disease.
M ~ Which drug lowers urate by inhibiting the URAT1 transporter and is contraindicated in urolithiasis? ~ Allopurinol ~ Probenecid ~ Febuxostat ~ Rasburicase ~ B ~ Uricosuric agents block tubular reabsorption so urinary urate rises, which risks stone formation; they also require adequate renal function and high fluid intake to be effective and safe.
M ~ Urate-lowering therapy started during an acute attack of gout should be accompanied by: ~ Nothing additional ~ Anti-inflammatory prophylaxis with colchicine or an NSAID for several months ~ Immediate cessation at the first flare ~ High-dose corticosteroids indefinitely ~ B ~ Mobilising crystals from deposits provokes flares in the first months, so prophylaxis improves adherence; treat-to-target dosing to a urate below 6 mg/dL is what actually dissolves tophi.
M ~ Which crystal is identified in pseudogout, and how does it appear under polarised light? ~ Needle-shaped, strongly negatively birefringent ~ Rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent calcium pyrophosphate ~ Bipyramidal calcium oxalate ~ Maltese cross cholesterol ~ B ~ Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate deposition produces chondrocalcinosis of menisci and the triangular fibrocartilage of the wrist, and attacks favour large joints in the elderly.
M ~ Asthma, eosinophilia above 10 percent and a necrotising vasculitis with p-ANCA suggest: ~ Granulomatosis with polyangiitis ~ Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis ~ Polyarteritis nodosa ~ Takayasu arteritis ~ B ~ The three phases of allergic rhinitis and asthma, then eosinophilic tissue infiltration, then vasculitis with mononeuritis multiplex and cardiac involvement characterise this ANCA-associated disease; cardiac disease drives mortality.
H ~ Which vasculitis characteristically causes recurrent oral and genital ulceration with uveitis and a positive pathergy test? ~ Behcet disease ~ Kawasaki disease ~ Buerger disease ~ Cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis ~ A ~ Behcet disease is a variable-vessel vasculitis associated with HLA-B51 that uniquely affects both arteries and veins, causing venous thrombosis and pulmonary artery aneurysms alongside the mucocutaneous and ocular features.
H ~ In giant cell arteritis, which treatment is added to corticosteroids to reduce cumulative steroid exposure and relapse? ~ Methotrexate alone ~ Tocilizumab, an interleukin-6 receptor antagonist ~ Rituximab ~ Cyclophosphamide ~ B ~ The GiACTA trial showed higher sustained remission and lower cumulative steroid dose with tocilizumab; note that it suppresses the erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein, so these can no longer be used to monitor disease activity.
@ Medicine ~ Thyroid disorders ~ MED_TD ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 375 (Disorders of the Thyroid Gland)
E ~ Which pattern of thyroid function tests indicates subclinical hypothyroidism? ~ Raised TSH with a normal free thyroxine ~ Raised TSH with a low free thyroxine ~ Low TSH with a raised free thyroxine ~ Low TSH with a low free thyroxine ~ A ~ The pituitary is the most sensitive detector of thyroid hormone deficiency, so TSH rises before thyroxine falls below the reference range; treatment is considered when TSH exceeds 10 or when antibodies, symptoms or pregnancy are present.
E ~ In a patient with suspected central hypothyroidism, which test result would be expected? ~ Low free thyroxine with a low or inappropriately normal TSH ~ Low free thyroxine with a high TSH ~ High free thyroxine with a low TSH ~ Normal free thyroxine with a high TSH ~ A ~ Pituitary or hypothalamic failure removes the drive to the thyroid, so TSH cannot rise appropriately; thyroxine must never be started before glucocorticoid replacement because it can precipitate adrenal crisis.
E ~ Which single test best distinguishes Graves disease from a toxic nodule when the clinical picture is unclear? ~ Serum thyroglobulin ~ Radioiodine uptake scan showing diffuse versus focal uptake, or TSH receptor antibody ~ Serum calcitonin ~ Fine needle aspiration ~ B ~ Diffuse homogeneous uptake with a positive TSH receptor antibody confirms Graves disease, whereas a solitary hot nodule suppressing the rest of the gland indicates a toxic adenoma, and the distinction changes definitive treatment.
M ~ A pregnant woman in the first trimester is found to have suppressed TSH with a mildly raised free thyroxine and no goitre or eye signs. The most likely explanation is: ~ Gestational transient thyrotoxicosis from human chorionic gonadotropin cross-stimulation ~ Graves disease ~ Toxic multinodular goitre ~ Factitious thyrotoxicosis ~ A ~ Human chorionic gonadotropin shares an alpha subunit with TSH and weakly stimulates its receptor, peaking at 10 to 12 weeks; the picture resolves spontaneously and needs no antithyroid drug.
M ~ Which feature of Graves ophthalmopathy is an indication for urgent high-dose corticosteroids and possible orbital decompression? ~ Lid lag ~ Optic neuropathy with reduced colour vision ~ Mild proptosis ~ Conjunctival injection ~ B ~ Compressive optic neuropathy at the orbital apex threatens permanent visual loss, so it is treated urgently; smoking cessation and selenium have roles in milder disease and radioiodine can worsen active eye disease.
M ~ Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis type 2 differs from type 1 in that it: ~ Results from iodine-induced excess synthesis in a nodular gland ~ Is a destructive thyroiditis with a low vascularity on Doppler, responding to corticosteroids ~ Always requires thyroidectomy ~ Occurs only in iodine-deficient areas ~ B ~ Type 1 is iodine-driven hyperfunction treated with thionamides, whereas type 2 is drug-induced destruction releasing preformed hormone and responds to steroids; distinguishing them changes management entirely.
M ~ A solitary thyroid nodule with fine needle aspiration reported as Bethesda category IV, a follicular neoplasm, should be managed by: ~ Repeat aspiration in six months ~ Diagnostic hemithyroidectomy or molecular testing ~ Radioiodine ~ Suppressive thyroxine ~ B ~ Cytology cannot distinguish follicular adenoma from carcinoma because that rests on capsular invasion, so lobectomy provides the histology; molecular marker panels can reduce unnecessary surgery.
H ~ Which finding on a thyroid nodule biopsy would indicate medullary carcinoma and prompt screening for phaeochromocytoma? ~ Papillae with nuclear grooves ~ Sheets of cells with amyloid stroma and positive calcitonin staining ~ Hurthle cells ~ Lymphocytic infiltration ~ B ~ Medullary carcinoma arises from parafollicular C cells and may be part of MEN 2, so RET testing and exclusion of phaeochromocytoma are mandatory before any neck surgery to avoid an intraoperative hypertensive crisis.
H ~ In a critically ill patient, a low free triiodothyronine with a normal or low TSH and a raised reverse triiodothyronine indicates: ~ Non-thyroidal illness (sick euthyroid) syndrome ~ Primary hypothyroidism ~ Thyrotoxicosis ~ Iodine deficiency ~ A ~ Illness shifts deiodination from the active to the inactive pathway; treating with thyroxine has not improved outcomes, so thyroid function is best rechecked after recovery rather than acted on during the acute illness.
@ Medicine ~ Tubulointerstitial disease, RTA & renal replacement therapy ~ MED_RTA ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 315 (Tubulointerstitial Diseases of the Kidney)
E ~ Which acid-base and potassium pattern characterises type 2 (proximal) renal tubular acidosis? ~ Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis with hypokalaemia and a bicarbonate threshold that is reset lower ~ High anion gap acidosis with hyperkalaemia ~ Metabolic alkalosis with hypokalaemia ~ Normal acid-base status ~ A ~ Failure of proximal bicarbonate reclamation causes bicarbonate wasting until plasma bicarbonate falls to the new lower threshold, after which the urine can be acidified, unlike distal disease where it cannot.
E ~ Which drug most characteristically causes chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis with a bland urinary sediment and slowly rising creatinine? ~ Lithium ~ Penicillin ~ Furosemide ~ Metformin ~ A ~ Lithium enters principal cells through the epithelial sodium channel and causes nephrogenic diabetes insipidus with chronic interstitial fibrosis and microcysts, so renal function and concentrating ability need long-term monitoring.
E ~ Which vascular access provides the best long-term survival for maintenance haemodialysis? ~ Tunnelled internal jugular catheter ~ Native arteriovenous fistula ~ Femoral temporary catheter ~ Arteriovenous graft ~ B ~ A native fistula has the lowest rates of infection and thrombosis and the best patency, which is why forearm veins are preserved from venepuncture in patients approaching end-stage renal disease.
M ~ A patient develops acute kidney injury with sterile pyuria, eosinophiluria and a rash a week after starting a proton pump inhibitor. The management is: ~ Increase fluid intake alone ~ Withdraw the offending drug, with corticosteroids if renal function does not recover ~ Start antibiotics ~ Begin urgent dialysis ~ B ~ Acute interstitial nephritis is a hypersensitivity reaction, so removing the trigger is essential; steroids are used when function fails to improve, and delayed diagnosis leads to interstitial fibrosis.
M ~ Which feature suggests that hyperkalaemia is due to type 4 rather than type 1 renal tubular acidosis? ~ A urine pH above 5.5 ~ Hyperkalaemia with a mild acidosis in a diabetic with hyporeninaemic hypoaldosteronism ~ Nephrocalcinosis ~ Recurrent calcium phosphate stones ~ B ~ Type 4 reflects aldosterone deficiency or resistance, so potassium is high and the acidosis mild; type 1 causes hypokalaemia with an inappropriately alkaline urine, nephrocalcinosis and stones.
M ~ Which complication of long-term haemodialysis is caused by beta-2 microglobulin deposition? ~ Dialysis-related amyloidosis with carpal tunnel syndrome and destructive arthropathy ~ Uraemic pericarditis ~ Aluminium bone disease ~ Dialysis disequilibrium ~ A ~ Beta-2 microglobulin is poorly cleared by conventional low-flux membranes, and its amyloid deposits in synovium, bone cysts and the carpal tunnel; high-flux membranes and transplantation reduce the risk.
M ~ Which finding indicates that chronic kidney disease has caused secondary hyperparathyroidism with high-turnover bone disease? ~ Low phosphate with low parathyroid hormone ~ High phosphate, low calcitriol and a markedly raised parathyroid hormone ~ Normal biochemistry with osteoporosis ~ High calcium with low parathyroid hormone ~ B ~ Reduced renal 1-alpha-hydroxylation lowers calcitriol while phosphate retention and FGF23 rise, together driving parathyroid hyperplasia; management is phosphate binders, active vitamin D analogues and calcimimetics.
H ~ Which condition causes a normal anion gap metabolic acidosis with a positive urinary anion gap and a urine pH above 5.5 despite systemic acidaemia? ~ Diarrhoeal bicarbonate loss ~ Distal (type 1) renal tubular acidosis ~ Proximal (type 2) renal tubular acidosis after bicarbonate depletion ~ Ureteric diversion ~ B ~ The failure of the alpha-intercalated cell hydrogen ATPase means ammonium excretion is impaired, so the urinary anion gap is positive and the urine cannot be acidified, in contrast to diarrhoea where ammonium excretion rises and the gap is negative.
