@ ENT ~ Neck spaces, deep neck infection & aerodigestive foreign bodies ~ ENT_NCK ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Deep Neck Infections
E ~ Which deep neck infection presents with bilateral submandibular and submental swelling with a raised woody tongue and airway threat? ~ Ludwig angina ~ Retropharyngeal abscess ~ Parapharyngeal abscess ~ Peritonsillar abscess ~ A ~ It usually arises from a lower molar tooth, involves the submandibular, submental and sublingual spaces, and airway management with drainage and antibiotics takes precedence over imaging.
E ~ Which space infection causes trismus with medial displacement of the tonsil and a risk of carotid sheath involvement? ~ Parapharyngeal abscess ~ Retropharyngeal abscess ~ Submandibular abscess ~ Masticator space abscess ~ A ~ Because the space contains the carotid sheath and cranial nerves IX to XII, complications include internal jugular thrombosis, carotid erosion and cranial neuropathy.
E ~ Which foreign body site is most common in a child who inhales a peanut, and why? ~ The right main bronchus, because it is wider, shorter and more vertical ~ The left main bronchus ~ The trachea ~ The larynx ~ A ~ Unilateral wheeze with reduced air entry and a normal radiograph does not exclude it, so a strong history warrants rigid bronchoscopy.
M ~ Which radiological sign indicates a retropharyngeal abscess in a child? ~ Widening of the prevertebral soft tissue shadow on a lateral neck radiograph ~ Steeple sign ~ Thumb sign ~ Sail sign ~ A ~ The steeple sign indicates croup and the thumb sign epiglottitis, and contrast computed tomography distinguishes cellulitis from a drainable collection.
M ~ Which complication of a deep neck infection presents with rigors, septic pulmonary emboli and internal jugular vein thrombosis? ~ Lemierre syndrome, usually due to Fusobacterium necrophorum ~ Ludwig angina ~ Grisel syndrome ~ Mediastinitis ~ A ~ It follows oropharyngeal infection in a young adult, is diagnosed by contrast imaging of the neck and chest, and is treated with prolonged antibiotics with anticoagulation in selected cases.
M ~ Which oesophageal site is the commonest for a swallowed coin to lodge in a child? ~ At the cricopharyngeus, the narrowest point about 15 cm from the incisors ~ At the aortic arch crossing ~ At the left main bronchus crossing ~ At the diaphragmatic hiatus ~ A ~ A coin lodged in the oesophagus appears as a full circle on an anteroposterior film whereas one in the trachea appears edge-on, which is a useful radiological distinction.
M ~ Which button battery ingestion feature makes it a surgical emergency rather than an observed foreign body? ~ Lodgement in the oesophagus, because electrolytic injury causes mucosal necrosis and perforation within hours ~ Location in the stomach ~ Passage beyond the duodenum ~ Any size below 15 mm ~ A ~ The double-ring or halo sign on radiograph identifies it, and endoscopic removal within 2 hours is the aim because delay leads to tracheo-oesophageal or aorto-oesophageal fistula.
H ~ Why does mediastinitis complicate a deep neck infection, and along which plane does it spread? ~ Infection tracks down the retropharyngeal and danger space to the posterior mediastinum, which is continuous with the neck ~ It spreads through the bloodstream only ~ It spreads via lymphatics to the mediastinum ~ It cannot spread below the clavicle ~ A ~ Because the danger space between the alar and prevertebral fascia extends to the diaphragm, descending necrotising mediastinitis carries a high mortality and requires aggressive thoracic drainage.
H ~ Why is a laryngeal foreign body more immediately life threatening than a bronchial one? ~ It obstructs the narrowest part of the airway completely and provokes laryngospasm, whereas a bronchial foreign body leaves one lung ventilated ~ It is always larger ~ It cannot be removed ~ It causes no symptoms ~ A ~ This explains why a complete laryngeal obstruction demands immediate back blows or abdominal thrusts, and cricothyroidotomy if these fail, rather than transfer for endoscopy.
@ ENT ~ Oral cavity, salivary gland & pharyngeal disease ~ ENT_ORL ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Diseases of Oral Cavity and Salivary Glands
E ~ Which salivary gland most often forms calculi, and why? ~ The submandibular gland, because its secretion is more mucinous and alkaline and the duct runs upward against gravity ~ The parotid gland ~ The sublingual gland ~ The minor salivary glands ~ A ~ Colicky swelling of the gland at mealtimes is characteristic, and most stones are radio-opaque so a plain film or ultrasound identifies them.
E ~ Which viral infection causes bilateral painful parotid swelling with a risk of orchitis and deafness? ~ Mumps ~ Cytomegalovirus ~ Coxsackie A ~ Epstein-Barr virus ~ A ~ Bilateral involvement distinguishes it from suppurative parotitis, which is unilateral with pus expressible from the duct and occurs in dehydrated or debilitated patients.
E ~ Which premalignant oral lesion carries a higher risk of malignant transformation than leukoplakia? ~ Erythroplakia ~ Leukoplakia ~ Oral submucous fibrosis ~ Lichen planus ~ A ~ A red velvety patch that cannot be scraped off has a transformation risk of up to about 30 percent, which is far higher than white patches, so early biopsy is essential.
M ~ Which condition presents with progressive trismus, blanching of the oral mucosa and fibrous bands in a habitual areca nut chewer? ~ Oral submucous fibrosis ~ Leukoplakia ~ Lichen planus ~ Candidiasis ~ A ~ It is potentially malignant, largely irreversible, and management combines habit cessation with intralesional steroid, physiotherapy and surgery for severe trismus, along with surveillance for carcinoma.
M ~ Which sign indicates that a parotid mass is malignant rather than benign? ~ Facial nerve palsy, fixity to skin or deep tissue, pain or cervical lymphadenopathy ~ Slow growth over years ~ A mobile discrete swelling ~ Absence of pain ~ A ~ Fine needle aspiration and imaging assist, but any facial weakness with a parotid mass is regarded as strongly suggestive of malignancy and changes the surgical plan.
M ~ Which surgical procedure is standard for a benign tumour of the superficial lobe of the parotid? ~ Superficial parotidectomy with facial nerve preservation ~ Enucleation of the tumour ~ Total conservative parotidectomy ~ Radical parotidectomy ~ A ~ Enucleation leaves pseudopodia behind and causes recurrence in pleomorphic adenoma, and Frey syndrome of gustatory sweating is a recognised late complication of the operation.
M ~ Which condition causes a dry mouth and dry eyes with bilateral parotid enlargement and a risk of lymphoma? ~ Sjogren syndrome ~ Sialolithiasis ~ Mumps ~ Sialadenosis ~ A ~ Antibodies to Ro and La with a labial gland biopsy showing focal lymphocytic sialadenitis support it, and the risk of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma justifies long-term surveillance.
H ~ Why does a squamous carcinoma of the posterior third of the tongue present later and with a worse prognosis than one of the anterior two thirds? ~ It is less visible and less painful early, and the region has rich bilateral lymphatic drainage so nodal spread occurs early ~ It grows more slowly ~ It has no lymphatic drainage ~ It is always detected on routine examination ~ A ~ This is why persistent unilateral otalgia, referred through the glossopharyngeal nerve, or a neck node without an obvious primary should prompt examination of the base of the tongue.
H ~ Why is a persistent unilateral serous otitis media in an adult from a high-incidence region an indication for nasopharyngeal biopsy? ~ Nasopharyngeal carcinoma can obstruct the eustachian tube and presents with a neck node and unilateral effusion before any nasal symptom ~ Effusions in adults are always benign ~ The tube is never obstructed by tumour ~ Biopsy is contraindicated ~ A ~ Its association with Epstein-Barr virus, salted fish consumption and genetic susceptibility, and its radiosensitivity, make early diagnosis particularly worthwhile.
@ ENT ~ Otitis media, CSOM, hearing loss ~ ENT_OMC ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Diseases of Middle Ear
E ~ Which organism most commonly causes acute suppurative otitis media in a child? ~ Streptococcus pneumoniae, followed by Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis ~ Pseudomonas aeruginosa ~ Staphylococcus aureus ~ Mycoplasma pneumoniae ~ A ~ Pseudomonas and Staphylococcus dominate chronic suppurative disease instead, which is why the antibiotic choice differs between acute and chronic infection.
E ~ Which stage of acute suppurative otitis media is characterised by relief of pain with the onset of discharge? ~ The stage of suppuration with perforation ~ The stage of hyperaemia ~ The stage of exudation ~ The stage of resolution ~ A ~ The bulging drum ruptures, releasing the pressure, and myringotomy achieves the same relief in a controlled way while preserving the drum.
M ~ Which finding indicates the need for myringotomy in acute otitis media? ~ A severely bulging drum with persistent severe pain, or a complication such as facial palsy ~ Any drum redness ~ Fever above 38 degrees ~ Mild conductive loss ~ A ~ An incision in the antero-inferior quadrant avoids the ossicles and the round window niche, and it heals without permanent perforation.
M ~ Which type of hearing loss follows chronic suppurative otitis media with extensive ossicular erosion, and what is the audiometric pattern? ~ Conductive loss with a large air-bone gap approaching 60 dB ~ Sensorineural loss with no gap ~ Mixed loss with a small gap ~ Normal audiogram ~ A ~ A gap larger than the maximum possible for a conductive lesion indicates a coexisting sensorineural component from toxin diffusion through the round window.
M ~ Which surgical procedure removes disease while preserving the posterior canal wall in cholesteatoma? ~ Canal wall up (intact canal wall) mastoidectomy, which requires careful follow-up for residual disease ~ Modified radical mastoidectomy ~ Radical mastoidectomy ~ Cortical mastoidectomy only ~ A ~ Canal wall down procedures give better disease clearance but leave a cavity requiring lifelong care, so the choice balances the risk of residual disease against cavity problems.
H ~ Why is a cholesteatoma described as skin in the wrong place rather than as a tumour? ~ It is a sac of keratinising squamous epithelium in the middle ear whose expansion and enzymatic activity erode bone, though it is not neoplastic ~ It is a benign neoplasm of squamous cells ~ It arises from cholesterol deposits ~ It is a granuloma ~ A ~ The name is a historical misnomer, and its danger comes from bone erosion producing intracranial and labyrinthine complications rather than from malignant potential.
@ ENT ~ Sinusitis, epistaxis, DNS ~ ENT_SED ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Diseases of Nose and Sinuses
E ~ Which vessel is the commonest source of severe posterior epistaxis in an older hypertensive patient? ~ The sphenopalatine artery, in Woodruff plexus ~ The anterior ethmoidal artery ~ The superior labial artery ~ The greater palatine artery ~ A ~ Anterior bleeding from Little area is commoner overall and easier to control, whereas posterior bleeding needs posterior packing, endoscopic sphenopalatine artery ligation or embolisation.
E ~ Which first-aid measure is correct for anterior epistaxis? ~ Firm pinching of the cartilaginous part of the nose with the patient sitting and leaning forward ~ Pinching the bony bridge lying supine ~ Tilting the head back ~ Packing the throat ~ A ~ Leaning forward prevents swallowing blood, which causes vomiting and obscures ongoing loss, and topical vasoconstrictor with cautery follows if pressure fails.
E ~ Which paranasal sinus is most often involved in sinusitis, and why? ~ The maxillary sinus, because its ostium lies high on the medial wall so gravity-dependent drainage is poor ~ The frontal sinus, because it is largest ~ The sphenoid sinus, because it is deepest ~ The ethmoid sinus, because it has no ostium ~ A ~ Pain that worsens on stooping, with tenderness over the cheek and postnasal discharge, is characteristic, and dental infection is a recognised unilateral cause.
M ~ Which complication of ethmoidal sinusitis is commonest in a child, and how is it staged? ~ Orbital cellulitis, staged by the Chandler classification from preseptal cellulitis to cavernous sinus thrombosis ~ Meningitis, staged by Glasgow score ~ Frontal osteomyelitis ~ Cavernous sinus thrombosis only ~ A ~ The thin lamina papyracea separates the ethmoids from the orbit, and proptosis, reduced eye movement or visual change indicate a postseptal collection requiring imaging and drainage.
M ~ Which imaging is used to assess chronic sinusitis before endoscopic sinus surgery? ~ Computed tomography of the paranasal sinuses in the coronal plane ~ Plain radiograph of the sinuses ~ Magnetic resonance imaging ~ Ultrasound ~ A ~ Computed tomography maps the bony anatomy including the ethmoid roof, orbital wall and optic nerve, whereas magnetic resonance is reserved for suspected intracranial or fungal extension.
M ~ Which condition causes a mucosal ulcer and septal perforation with a history of cocaine use or previous surgery? ~ Septal perforation from ischaemic necrosis of cartilage ~ Antrochoanal polyp ~ Inverted papilloma ~ Rhinophyma ~ A ~ Perforation causes whistling, crusting and epistaxis, and other causes include trauma, granulomatous disease, tuberculosis and prolonged topical steroid use with poor technique.
M ~ Which feature of a deviated nasal septum makes surgical correction appropriate rather than observation? ~ Symptomatic obstruction, recurrent sinusitis or epistaxis attributable to the deviation ~ Any visible deviation ~ Cosmetic concern alone in a child ~ Presence of a spur without symptoms ~ A ~ Septoplasty preserves and repositions cartilage rather than resecting it as in the older submucous resection, which reduces the risk of saddle deformity and septal perforation.
H ~ Why is a young adult with recurrent severe epistaxis and telangiectasia on the lips investigated for a systemic disorder? ~ Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia can involve pulmonary and cerebral arteriovenous malformations that carry serious risk ~ It is always local ~ Telangiectasia is normal ~ Epistaxis in youth is never significant ~ A ~ The autosomal dominant condition requires screening of the lungs and brain and of first-degree relatives, so recognising the pattern in the nose has consequences well beyond the nose.
H ~ Why is a persistent unilateral nasal obstruction with bleeding in an adult treated as suspicious even when examination looks benign? ~ Sinonasal malignancy and inverted papilloma present exactly this way and are easily missed without endoscopy and imaging ~ Unilateral symptoms are always benign ~ Bilateral symptoms are more sinister ~ Endoscopy is unhelpful ~ A ~ The maxillary antrum is the commonest site, and by the time facial swelling, epiphora, loose teeth or diplopia appear the disease is already advanced.
@ ENT ~ Tonsillitis, carcinoma larynx ~ ENT_TCL ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Diseases of Oropharynx and Larynx
E ~ Which indication for tonsillectomy is supported by the strongest evidence? ~ Recurrent documented tonsillitis meeting frequency criteria, or obstructive sleep-disordered breathing ~ A single episode of tonsillitis ~ Halitosis alone ~ Tonsillar asymmetry without other features ~ A ~ Sleep-disordered breathing with adenotonsillar hypertrophy in a child is now the commonest indication, and unilateral enlargement in an adult raises the question of lymphoma or carcinoma.
E ~ Which complication of tonsillectomy occurs within the first 24 hours and is usually due to inadequate haemostasis? ~ Primary or reactionary haemorrhage, requiring return to theatre ~ Secondary haemorrhage from infection ~ Velopharyngeal insufficiency ~ Referred otalgia ~ A ~ Secondary haemorrhage occurs at 5 to 10 days from slough separation with infection and is usually managed with antibiotics and observation, though it can be severe.
E ~ Which staging feature of laryngeal carcinoma most strongly influences the decision between larynx preservation and total laryngectomy? ~ Cartilage invasion and involvement of the pre-epiglottic or paraglottic space with cord fixation ~ Patient age ~ Tumour histology grade ~ Presence of hoarseness ~ A ~ Early tumours are treated by radiotherapy or transoral laser surgery with excellent voice preservation, whereas extensive cartilage invasion predicts poor response to organ preservation.
M ~ Which finding indicates a peritonsillar abscess rather than acute tonsillitis? ~ Unilateral severe pain with trismus, a muffled voice, uvular deviation and a bulging soft palate ~ Bilateral tonsillar exudate ~ Fever with cervical nodes ~ Odynophagia alone ~ A ~ Aspiration or incision and drainage with antibiotics is the treatment, and interval tonsillectomy is considered because of the recurrence risk.
M ~ Which condition must be excluded before tonsillectomy in a child with a bifid uvula or submucous cleft palate? ~ Velopharyngeal insufficiency, which tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy could unmask ~ Coagulopathy ~ Obstructive sleep apnoea ~ Recurrent otitis media ~ A ~ Removing the adenoid in such a child can produce hypernasal speech and nasal regurgitation, so a partial or superior adenoidectomy is used if surgery is needed at all.
M ~ Which route of spread makes supraglottic carcinoma present with nodal disease earlier than glottic carcinoma? ~ Rich bilateral lymphatic drainage of the supraglottis, in contrast to the sparse lymphatics of the true cords ~ Direct haematogenous spread ~ Perineural spread ~ Transcartilaginous spread ~ A ~ This anatomical difference explains both the earlier nodal disease and the need to treat the neck electively in supraglottic tumours even when it appears clinically negative.
H ~ Why does referred otalgia occur in carcinoma of the larynx or base of tongue? ~ Shared sensory innervation through the vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves refers pain to the ear via Arnold and Jacobson nerves ~ Direct tumour extension to the ear ~ Middle ear infection always coexists ~ It is psychogenic ~ A ~ This is why a patient with persistent unilateral otalgia and a normal ear examination must have the larynx and pharynx examined carefully.
@ ENT ~ Vertigo, Meniere's disease & facial nerve palsy ~ ENT_VER ~ Dhingra Diseases of Ear, Nose and Throat 8e, Ch. Vertigo and Facial Nerve
E ~ Which condition causes brief episodic vertigo lasting under a minute triggered by head position change, with a positive Dix-Hallpike test? ~ Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo from otolith debris in the posterior canal ~ Meniere disease ~ Vestibular neuritis ~ Vestibular schwannoma ~ A ~ The Epley repositioning manoeuvre resolves most cases, and the characteristic geotropic torsional nystagmus with latency and fatigability confirms the diagnosis.
E ~ Which tetrad characterises Meniere disease? ~ Episodic vertigo lasting minutes to hours, fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness ~ Continuous vertigo with facial palsy ~ Brief positional vertigo with normal hearing ~ Progressive hearing loss with no vertigo ~ A ~ Endolymphatic hydrops is the pathological basis, low frequencies are affected first, and management combines salt restriction with betahistine and diuretics, escalating to intratympanic therapy.
E ~ Which condition causes acute severe vertigo lasting days with normal hearing after a viral illness? ~ Vestibular neuritis ~ Labyrinthitis ~ Meniere disease ~ Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo ~ A ~ Labyrinthitis includes hearing loss because the cochlea is involved, and vestibular rehabilitation with a short course of vestibular sedative is the approach, avoiding prolonged suppressant use which delays compensation.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes a central from a peripheral cause of vertigo? ~ Vertical or direction-changing nystagmus that is not suppressed by fixation, with other brainstem or cerebellar signs ~ Horizontal nystagmus with a fast phase away from the lesion ~ Severe nausea ~ Tinnitus ~ A ~ The HINTS examination with head impulse, nystagmus and test of skew is more sensitive than early imaging for posterior circulation stroke in acute continuous vertigo.
M ~ Which grading system describes the severity of facial nerve palsy? ~ House-Brackmann grading from I to VI ~ Chandler classification ~ Ramsay classification ~ Fisch grading ~ A ~ Documenting the grade at presentation and during recovery is what allows treatment decisions and prognostication, and electroneuronography adds prognostic information in complete palsy.
M ~ Which treatment improves outcome in Bell palsy, and when must it be started? ~ Oral corticosteroids started within 72 hours of onset ~ Antivirals alone ~ Surgical decompression in all cases ~ Physiotherapy alone ~ A ~ Antivirals add little except possibly in severe palsy, and eye care with lubricants and taping to prevent exposure keratopathy is as important as the drug treatment.
M ~ Which condition causes facial palsy with vesicles in the external auditory canal and severe otalgia? ~ Ramsay Hunt syndrome from varicella zoster reactivation in the geniculate ganglion ~ Bell palsy ~ Acute otitis media with facial palsy ~ Lyme disease ~ A ~ It has a poorer prognosis than Bell palsy, and combined aciclovir with corticosteroid started early improves the chance of complete recovery.
H ~ Which site of facial nerve lesion is indicated by palsy with loss of taste, reduced lacrimation and hyperacusis? ~ A lesion at or proximal to the geniculate ganglion, above the greater petrosal branch ~ A lesion at the stylomastoid foramen ~ A lesion in the parotid gland ~ A lesion of the facial nucleus ~ A ~ Topodiagnostic testing follows the branches in order, with the nerve to stapedius affecting the acoustic reflex and the chorda tympani affecting taste and submandibular secretion.
H ~ Why does a vestibular schwannoma cause progressive unilateral hearing loss with disproportionate tinnitus but relatively little vertigo? ~ Slow growth allows central vestibular compensation, whereas cochlear nerve compression progressively impairs hearing ~ It does not involve the vestibular nerve ~ It grows too fast for compensation ~ Hearing is unaffected ~ A ~ Asymmetric sensorineural loss with poor speech discrimination is the classic presentation, and magnetic resonance imaging of the internal auditory meatus is the investigation of choice.
