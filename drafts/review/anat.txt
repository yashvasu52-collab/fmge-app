=== Anatomy / Inguinal canal, GIT relations
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_001 [E] The superficial (external) inguinal ring is a triangular deficiency in the aponeurosis of which muscle of the anterior abdominal wall?
   *A. External oblique
    B. Internal oblique
    C. Transversus abdominis
    D. Rectus abdominis
   key=A ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 2 — Abdomen & Pelvis (Anterior Abdominal Wall & Inguinal Canal)
   concept: The superficial inguinal ring is a triangular gap in the external oblique aponeurosis, lying just above and lateral to the pubic tubercle; its margins (medial and lateral crura) are bridged by intercrural fibres. The deep inguinal ring, in contrast, lies in the fascia transversalis. Internal oblique and transversus form the roof and the conjoint tendon, not the ring itself.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_002 [E] The deep (internal) inguinal ring, marking the entrance to the inguinal canal, is an opening in which layer of the abdominal wall?
    A. External oblique aponeurosis
    B. Aponeurosis of internal oblique
   *C. Fascia transversalis
    D. Transversus abdominis muscle
   key=C ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 5 — The Abdomen (Inguinal Canal)
   concept: The deep inguinal ring is an evagination of the fascia transversalis, situated about 1.25 cm above the midpoint of the inguinal ligament and lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels; it is the site through which an indirect inguinal hernia enters the canal. The superficial ring lies more medially in the external oblique aponeurosis, so the two rings do not overlie one another.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_003 [M] The anterior wall of the inguinal canal is formed throughout by the external oblique aponeurosis. In its lateral third, in front of the deep ring, it is additionally reinforced by which structure?
    A. Conjoint tendon
    B. Fascia transversalis
    C. Lacunar ligament
   *D. Fleshy fibres of the internal oblique
   key=D ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 2 — Abdomen & Pelvis (Inguinal Canal)
   concept: The anterior wall is the external oblique aponeurosis along the whole canal, reinforced laterally (in front of the deep ring) by the fleshy origin of the internal oblique. This puts a muscular reinforcement in front of the deep ring, while the conjoint tendon reinforces the posterior wall medially behind the superficial ring — so the two weak points (the rings) are each backed by a reinforcement on the opposite wall.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_004 [M] Assertion (A): The posterior wall of the inguinal canal is strongest in its medial part, opposite the superficial inguinal ring. Reason (R): The conjoint tendon reinforces the fascia transversalis in the medial part of the posterior wall. Select the correct option.
   *A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 2 — Abdomen & Pelvis (Inguinal Canal)
   concept: The posterior wall of the inguinal canal is the fascia transversalis throughout, reinforced medially by the conjoint tendon (falx inguinalis), formed from the internal oblique and transversus aponeuroses attaching to the pubic crest and pectineal line. This reinforcement lies exactly behind the superficial ring, protecting an otherwise weak point — so both statements are true and R correctly explains A. A direct hernia bulges through this wall medial to the inferior epigastric vessels.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_005 [M] The conjoint tendon (falx inguinalis), which reinforces the medial part of the posterior wall of the inguinal canal, is formed by the fusion of the aponeurotic fibres of which two muscles?
    A. External oblique and internal oblique
    B. Transversus abdominis and external oblique
   *C. Internal oblique and transversus abdominis
    D. Internal oblique and rectus abdominis
   key=C ref=Gray's Anatomy for Students, Chapter 4 — Abdomen (Anterior Abdominal Wall)
   concept: The conjoint tendon is formed by the lower aponeurotic fibres of the internal oblique and transversus abdominis, which arch over the spermatic cord and descend to attach to the pubic crest and the medial part of the pectineal line. It strengthens the posterior wall behind the superficial ring. The external oblique instead forms the anterior wall and the inguinal, lacunar and pectineal ligaments.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_006 [M] Which of the following structures runs within the inguinal canal but is NOT a content of the spermatic cord (lying outside its coverings)?
    A. Ductus (vas) deferens
    B. Testicular artery
    C. Pampiniform plexus of veins
   *D. Ilioinguinal nerve
   key=D ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 5 — The Abdomen (Spermatic Cord)
   concept: The ilioinguinal nerve enters the canal by piercing the internal oblique and runs on the outer surface of the cord, leaving through the superficial ring; it is therefore in the canal but not a true content of the cord. True cord contents include the ductus deferens, testicular artery, artery to the vas, cremasteric artery, pampiniform plexus, genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve, sympathetic fibres, lymphatics and the obliterated processus vaginalis.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_007 [H] A physician palpates the femoral artery pulsation at the mid-inguinal point. This point is defined as lying midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and which bony landmark?

   key=B ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 5 — The Abdomen (Surface Anatomy)
   concept: The mid-inguinal point is midway between the ASIS and the pubic symphysis and marks where the femoral artery enters the thigh. It must be distinguished from the midpoint of the inguinal ligament (midway between ASIS and pubic tubercle), which overlies the deep inguinal ring. Because the pubic tubercle lies lateral to the symphysis, these two surface points are close but not identical — a classic exam distinction.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_008 [M] During repair of a strangulated femoral hernia, the surgeon must carefully divide the sharp, crescentic medial boundary of the femoral ring. This structure is the:

   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 5 — The Abdomen (Femoral Canal & Hernia)
   concept: The femoral ring is bounded anteriorly by the inguinal ligament, posteriorly by the pectineal ligament, laterally by the femoral vein (via a thin septum), and medially by the sharp crescentic lacunar ligament of Gimbernat. Strangulation occurs at this unyielding medial edge, which is the structure incised to relieve it — with care taken for an abnormal (aberrant) obturator artery that may cross the lacunar ligament.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_009 [H] To distinguish a femoral from an inguinal hernia at the bedside, the clinician relates the neck of the sac to the pubic tubercle. The neck of a FEMORAL hernia characteristically lies:

   *C. Below and lateral to the pubic tubercle
    D. Directly over the pubic tubercle
   key=C ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 5 — The Abdomen (Hernias)
   concept: The neck of a femoral hernia emerges through the femoral ring, which lies below and lateral to the pubic tubercle, whereas an inguinal hernia exits through the superficial inguinal ring above and medial to the tubercle. This classic test uses the pubic tubercle as the reference point. Femoral hernias are commoner in women and have a high strangulation risk owing to the narrow, rigid femoral ring.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_010 [M] Below the arcuate line of Douglas, the aponeurotic posterior wall of the rectus sheath is absent. Here the posterior surface of the rectus abdominis is directly related to which structure?
    A. Peritoneum only

    D. Posterior lamina of the internal oblique aponeurosis
   key=B ref=Gray's Anatomy for Students, Chapter 4 — Abdomen (Rectus Sheath)
   concept: Below the arcuate line all three flat-muscle aponeuroses pass in front of the rectus, leaving no posterior aponeurotic sheath; the muscle then rests directly on the transversalis fascia and, deep to it, the extraperitoneal fat and peritoneum. Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_011 [H] The arcuate line (linea semicircularis of Douglas), which marks the lower limit of the aponeurotic posterior rectus sheath, is typically located at approximately which level?

   concept: The arcuate line lies roughly midway between the umbilicus and the pubic symphysis (a few centimetres below the umbilicus). Below it the rectus sheath has no posterior aponeurotic wall, and it is where the inferior epigastric vessels pass anterior to enter the sheath. The lateral edge of the rectus (linea semilunaris) is the recognised, though uncommon, site of a Spigelian hernia.
Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_012 [E] The anterior boundary of the epiploic foramen (of Winslow), which connects the greater and lesser sacs, is formed by the:
   *A. Free right border of the lesser omentum (hepatoduodenal ligament)

    D. First part of the duodenum
   key=A ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 2 — Abdomen & Pelvis (Peritoneum)
   concept: The epiploic (omental) foramen is bounded anteriorly by the free right border of the lesser omentum (hepatoduodenal ligament), which carries the portal triad — bile duct (right), hepatic artery proper (left) and portal vein (behind). Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_013 [M] Which structure forms the POSTERIOR boundary of the epiploic foramen of Winslow?
    A. Portal vein
    B. Bile duct
    C. Quadrate lobe of the liver
   *D. Inferior vena cava
   key=D ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 5 — The Abdomen (Peritoneal Cavity)
   concept: The posterior boundary of the epiploic foramen is the inferior vena cava, covered by peritoneum. Anteriorly lies the hepatoduodenal ligament with the portal triad, superiorly the caudate lobe of the liver, and inferiorly the first part of the duodenum with the horizontal course of the hepatic artery. Compressing the anterior boundary between finger and thumb (Pringle's manoeuvre) controls bleeding from the hepatic artery or portal vein.

Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_015 [H] The foregut is supplied by the coeliac trunk and the midgut by the superior mesenteric artery. The embryological foregut–midgut junction, marking this change in dominant arterial supply, lies at which point?

   key=D ref=Gray's Anatomy for Students, Chapter 4 — Abdomen (Development & Blood Supply of the Gut)

Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_016 [H] An elderly hypotensive patient develops ischaemic colitis. The most vulnerable 'watershed' zone lies at the splenic flexure, at the border between the territories of which two arteries?

   concept: The splenic (left colic) flexure is the junction of midgut supply (SMA, via the middle colic artery) and hindgut supply (IMA, via the left colic artery). A second watershed (Sudeck's point) lies at the rectosigmoid junction between the IMA/superior rectal and the internal iliac supply.

Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_020 [M] All of the following structures pass through the porta hepatis EXCEPT:

Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_021 [E] In the majority of individuals, the vermiform appendix most commonly occupies which position relative to the caecum?

Q_GEN_ANAT_ABDOMEN_025 [H] Assertion (A): A malignant tumour of the testis metastasises first to the superficial inguinal lymph nodes. === Anatomy / Mediastinum, heart & great vessels
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_001 [E] Which of the following structures lies within the middle mediastinum?

    C. Oesophagus
   *D. Heart enclosed within the pericardium
   key=D ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 1 — Thorax (Mediastinum)

Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_002 [M] The narrowest of the normal oesophageal constrictions, situated about 15 cm from the incisor teeth and the commonest cervical site of foreign-body arrest, is produced by the:

Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_003 [M] A 58-year-old man has central chest pain; ECG shows ST-segment elevation in leads II, III and aVF. Occlusion of which coronary artery best explains this inferior-wall infarct?
    A. Left anterior descending artery
    B. Left circumflex artery
   *C. Right coronary artery
    D. Left main coronary artery
   key=C ref=Moore's Clinically Oriented Anatomy — Thorax (Coronary Arteries)
   concept: In the common right-dominant heart, the right coronary artery gives the posterior interventricular (posterior descending) branch and supplies the diaphragmatic/inferior surface of the left ventricle, which is reflected in the inferior leads II, III and aVF. The RCA also usually supplies the SA and AV nodes, so inferior infarcts are often accompanied by bradyarrhythmias. The LAD supplies the anterior wall (V1–V4), the circumflex the lateral wall (I, aVL, V5–V6), and left main occlusion is typically catastrophic and widespread.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_004 [M] In the majority of individuals, the artery supplying the sinuatrial (SA) node arises from the:
    A. Left anterior descending artery
    B. Left circumflex artery
    C. Posterior interventricular artery
   *D. Right coronary artery
   key=D ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 1 — Thorax (Blood Supply of the Heart)
   concept: The sinuatrial nodal artery arises from the right coronary artery in about 60% of hearts (near its origin, running to the node at the junction of SVC and right atrium) and from the left circumflex artery in the remaining ~40%. Because the pacemaker's blood supply most often comes from the RCA, proximal RCA disease can disturb impulse generation. The posterior interventricular artery supplies the posterior septum, not the SA node, and the LAD supplies the anterior septum and anterior LV.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_005 [H] In a right-dominant coronary circulation, the atrioventricular (AV) nodal branch that arises at the crux of the heart is a branch of the:

   *B. Right coronary artery
    C. Left anterior descending artery
    D. Anterior interventricular artery
   key=B ref=Gray's Anatomy for Students — Thorax (Cardiac Vessels and Dominance)
   concept: Coronary dominance is defined by which artery crosses the crux (the point where the posterior atrioventricular and interventricular grooves meet) to give the posterior descending artery and the AV nodal branch. In the right-dominant pattern (about 85–90% of people) the right coronary artery reaches the crux and supplies the AV node; hence inferior/RCA infarcts can cause AV block. In the ~10% left-dominant hearts the circumflex takes over this role.

    D. Entire left atrium and the coronary sinus wall
   key=B ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 3 — The Thorax (Coronary Arteries)
   concept: The left anterior descending artery runs in the anterior interventricular groove and supplies the anterior two-thirds of the interventricular septum, the anterior wall of the left ventricle, the adjacent right ventricular wall and the cardiac apex; it also gives septal perforators to the proximal bundle branches. The posterior third of the septum is supplied by the posterior interventricular artery (usually from the RCA), so LAD occlusion classically produces large anteroseptal infarcts.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_007 [E] The apex of the heart, where the apex beat is normally palpated, is formed by which chamber?
    A. Right ventricle
    B. Left atrium
   *C. Left ventricle
    D. Right atrium
   key=C ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 1 — Thorax (External Features of the Heart)
   concept: The apex of the heart is formed entirely by the left ventricle and points downward, forward and to the left, lying deep to the left fifth intercostal space just medial to the midclavicular line, where the apex beat is felt. The right ventricle forms most of the anterior/sternocostal surface and inferior border, the right atrium forms the right border, and the left atrium forms the base (posterior surface), not the apex.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_008 [E] On a frontal (PA) chest radiograph, the right border of the cardiac shadow is formed by the:
   *A. Right atrium
    B. Right ventricle
    C. Left ventricle
    D. Ascending aorta
   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 3 — The Thorax (Borders and Surfaces of the Heart)
   concept: The right border of the heart is formed by the right atrium, which receives the SVC above and the IVC below. The inferior border is formed mainly by the right ventricle (with a small part of the left ventricle at the apex), and the left border by the left ventricle and the left auricle. The right atrium forming the right border is a standard landmark used when assessing cardiac silhouette and chamber enlargement on radiographs.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_009 [H] The coronary sinus, which drains most of the venous blood of the heart, opens into the:
   *A. Right atrium
    B. Right ventricle
    C. Left atrium
    D. Superior vena cava
   key=A ref=Gray's Anatomy for Students — Thorax (Cardiac Veins and Coronary Sinus)
   concept: The coronary sinus lies in the posterior part of the atrioventricular (coronary) groove and opens into the right atrium between the opening of the inferior vena cava and the right atrioventricular (tricuspid) orifice; its mouth is guarded by the valve of the coronary sinus (Thebesian valve). It receives the great, middle and small cardiac veins and the oblique vein of the left atrium, and returns the bulk of cardiac venous blood to the right atrium.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_010 [M] The sounds of the mitral valve are best auscultated at which surface site, even though the valve itself lies deep to the left fourth costal cartilage?
    A. Right second intercostal space at the sternal edge
    B. Left second intercostal space at the sternal edge
   *C. Left fifth intercostal space in the midclavicular line (apex)
    D. Lower left sternal border in the fourth–fifth intercostal space
   key=C ref=Moore's Clinically Oriented Anatomy — Thorax (Surface Anatomy and Auscultation of Heart Valves)
   concept: Auscultation areas do not overlie the valves but lie downstream in the direction of blood flow, so the valve sounds are carried to where the blood is projected. The mitral valve is heard best at the apex (left fifth intercostal space, midclavicular line), the tricuspid at the lower left sternal edge, the aortic in the right second intercostal space, and the pulmonary in the left second intercostal space. Thus the mitral area is at the apex despite the valve lying behind the left fourth costal cartilage.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_011 [H] Which cardiac valve lies most superiorly and is auscultated in the left second intercostal space?
    A. Aortic valve
    B. Mitral valve
    C. Tricuspid valve
   *D. Pulmonary valve
   key=D ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 1 — Thorax (Surface Marking of Heart Valves)
   concept: The pulmonary valve is the most superior and most anterior of the four valves, lying behind the medial end of the left third costal cartilage and the adjoining sternum; its sounds are heard in the left second intercostal space. In descending order of position the valves lie: pulmonary (highest), aortic (just below and to the right), then the mitral and tricuspid atrioventricular valves. The surface projections cluster along a line, so the listening areas are deliberately offset downstream.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_012 [E] The three branches of the arch of the aorta, in order from right (proximal) to left (distal), are:
    A. Left common carotid, brachiocephalic trunk, left subclavian
   *B. Brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid, left subclavian
    C. Brachiocephalic trunk, left subclavian, left common carotid
    D. Left subclavian, left common carotid, brachiocephalic trunk
   key=B ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 3 — The Thorax (Arch of the Aorta)
   concept: The arch of the aorta gives three branches from its convex upper surface, in order from proximal to distal: the brachiocephalic trunk (which divides into the right common carotid and right subclavian arteries), the left common carotid artery, and the left subclavian artery. There is no right-sided single trunk equivalent to a left brachiocephalic artery because the right common carotid and right subclavian arise together from the brachiocephalic trunk.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_013 [H] Assertion (A): An aneurysm of the arch of the aorta can produce hoarseness of the voice. Reason (R): The left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks below the arch around the ligamentum arteriosum before ascending to the larynx. Choose the most appropriate option.
   *A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=Moore's Clinically Oriented Anatomy — Thorax (Vagus and Recurrent Laryngeal Nerves)
   concept: The left recurrent laryngeal nerve arises from the left vagus in the thorax, loops beneath the arch of the aorta immediately behind the ligamentum arteriosum, then ascends in the tracheo-oesophageal groove to supply the intrinsic laryngeal muscles. An aortic arch aneurysm can stretch or compress this nerve, causing left vocal-cord palsy and hoarseness (Ortner cardiovocal syndrome), so R correctly explains A. The right recurrent laryngeal nerve instead hooks under the right subclavian artery.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_014 [M] The ligamentum arteriosum, the fibrous remnant of the ductus arteriosus, connects the:
    A. Superior vena cava to the right atrium
    B. Ascending aorta to the pulmonary trunk at the level of the valves
    C. Left atrium to the descending thoracic aorta
   *D. Root of the left pulmonary artery (pulmonary trunk) to the arch of the aorta
   key=D ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 1 — Thorax (Arch of Aorta and Ligamentum Arteriosum)
   concept: The ligamentum arteriosum is the post-natal remnant of the ductus arteriosus; it connects the beginning of the left pulmonary artery (or the bifurcation of the pulmonary trunk) to the inferior concave surface of the aortic arch, just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks around it. Failure of the ductus to close produces a patent ductus arteriosus with a left-to-right shunt and a continuous machinery murmur.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_015 [H] After ascending on the right side of the vertebral column, the azygos vein arches forward over the root of the right lung to drain into the:
    A. Right atrium
    B. Left brachiocephalic vein
   *C. Superior vena cava
    D. Inferior vena cava
   key=C ref=Gray's Anatomy for Students — Thorax (Azygos System of Veins)
   concept: The azygos vein ascends in the posterior mediastinum on the right of the vertebral bodies, and at about the T4 level it arches forward over the root of the right lung (the arch of the azygos) to open into the posterior aspect of the superior vena cava, just before the SVC pierces the pericardium. It receives the right posterior intercostal veins, the hemiazygos and accessory hemiazygos veins, and bronchial and oesophageal veins, and forms an important caval–caval collateral channel in IVC obstruction.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_016 [M] An aspirated peanut in a supine adult is more likely to lodge in the right main bronchus because, compared with the left, the right main bronchus is:
    A. Longer, narrower and more horizontal
   *B. Wider, shorter and more vertical (more in line with the trachea)
    C. The first branch of the trachea, arising above the carina
    D. Directly continuous with the lingular bronchus
   key=B ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 3 — The Thorax (Trachea and Bronchi)
   concept: The right main (principal) bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left, running more directly in continuity with the trachea below the carina; consequently inhaled foreign bodies and aspirated material pass more readily into it and into the right lung. The left main bronchus is narrower, longer and more horizontal as it passes below the arch of the aorta. The carina is the internal ridge at the tracheal bifurcation, and both main bronchi arise there, not above it.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_017 [H] The oesophageal hiatus of the diaphragm, at the level of the T10 vertebra, transmits the oesophagus together with which nerves?
    A. Greater splanchnic nerves
    B. Right and left phrenic nerves
    C. Sympathetic trunks
   *D. Anterior and posterior vagal trunks
   key=D ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 1 — Thorax (Diaphragm and its Openings)
   concept: The oesophageal hiatus lies at the T10 level within the muscular sling of the right crus of the diaphragm and transmits the oesophagus, the anterior and posterior vagal trunks (derived mainly from the left and right vagi respectively after the oesophageal plexus) and the oesophageal branches of the left gastric vessels. In contrast, the caval opening (T8) transmits the IVC and right phrenic branches, and the aortic hiatus (T12) transmits the aorta, thoracic duct and azygos vein.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_018 [M] The inferior vena cava pierces the central tendon of the diaphragm at the level of which vertebra?
   *A. T8
    B. T10
    C. T12
    D. T6
   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 3 — The Thorax (Diaphragm)
   concept: The caval opening for the inferior vena cava lies in the central tendon of the diaphragm at the T8 level and also transmits terminal branches of the right phrenic nerve; being in the tendon, its margins are attached to the vein, so descent of the diaphragm on inspiration widens it and aids venous return. The three main diaphragmatic apertures are remembered as T8 (caval), T10 (oesophageal) and T12 (aortic) — the mnemonic I ate (8) ten (10) eggs at twelve (12).
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_019 [E] The phrenic nerve, the sole motor supply to the diaphragm, arises from which spinal cord segments?
    A. C1, C2 and C3
   *B. C3, C4 and C5
    C. C5, C6 and C7
    D. T1, T2 and T3
   key=B ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 1 — Thorax (Phrenic Nerve)
   concept: The phrenic nerve arises from the ventral rami of C3, C4 and C5 (chiefly C4), summarised as C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive. It provides the entire motor supply to the diaphragm and carries sensory fibres from the fibrous pericardium, mediastinal parietal pleura and the central part of the diaphragmatic pleura and peritoneum. High cervical cord lesions above C3 therefore abolish diaphragmatic breathing, whereas lesions below C5 spare it.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_020 [M] Irritation of the central diaphragmatic peritoneum (for example by a subphrenic abscess) is classically referred to the tip of the shoulder because the phrenic nerve shares its segmental origin with the supraclavicular nerves, namely:
    A. C2 and C3
   *B. C3 and C4
    C. C5 and C6
    D. T1 and T2
   key=B ref=Moore's Clinically Oriented Anatomy — Thorax (Phrenic Nerve and Referred Pain)
   concept: The central part of the diaphragm receives sensory innervation from the phrenic nerve (C3–C5, predominantly C3–C4). The supraclavicular nerves supplying the skin over the shoulder tip also arise from C3–C4, so central diaphragmatic irritation is referred to the shoulder tip (as in Kehr sign with splenic rupture or subphrenic collections). The peripheral rim of the diaphragm is instead supplied by the lower intercostal nerves, so peripheral irritation is referred to the thoraco-abdominal wall.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_021 [M] During chest-drain insertion the instrument is passed immediately above the upper border of the lower rib of the space. This avoids the main intercostal neurovascular bundle, whose structures lie in the costal groove in which order from superior to inferior?
    A. Nerve, artery, vein
    B. Artery, vein, nerve
   *C. Vein, artery, nerve
    D. Vein, nerve, artery
   key=C ref=Snell's Clinical Anatomy by Regions, Chapter 3 — The Thorax (Intercostal Spaces and Neurovascular Bundle)
   concept: In the costal groove along the lower border of each upper rib the main intercostal neurovascular bundle is arranged from above downward as Vein, Artery, Nerve (mnemonic VAN). Because these lie tucked under the rib above the space, entry is made just above the upper border of the rib below the space (over the middle of the space), where only a smaller collateral bundle runs, minimising the risk of injuring the main vessels and nerve. The safe triangle for drains is bordered by pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi and the fifth intercostal space.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_022 [H] The transverse pericardial sinus is a passage within the pericardial cavity that lies posterior to the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk and anterior to the:
    A. Inferior vena cava and right ventricle
    B. Left ventricle and cardiac apex
   *C. Superior vena cava and the left atrium
    D. Descending thoracic aorta
   key=C ref=Gray's Anatomy for Students — Thorax (Pericardium and its Sinuses)
   concept: The transverse pericardial sinus is the space enclosed as the serous pericardium reflects around the arterial and venous groups of great vessels; it lies behind the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk (the arterial mesocardium) and in front of the superior vena cava and the atria (left atrium). A finger or ligature passed through this sinus can be used to clamp or ligate the aorta and pulmonary trunk together during cardiac surgery. The oblique pericardial sinus is a separate cul-de-sac behind the left atrium.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_023 [H] Assertion (A): A small but rapidly accumulating pericardial effusion may precipitate cardiac tamponade. Reason (R): The tough fibrous pericardium is relatively inextensible and cannot stretch acutely to accommodate the added volume. Choose the most appropriate option.
   *A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=Moore's Clinically Oriented Anatomy — Thorax (Pericardium and Cardiac Tamponade)
   concept: The fibrous pericardium is a strong, inelastic sac, so fluid entering the pericardial cavity rapidly (as in trauma or aortic rupture) causes a steep rise in intrapericardial pressure that compresses the thin-walled atria and ventricles, impairing venous return and diastolic filling — cardiac tamponade, with Beck triad of hypotension, muffled heart sounds and raised jugular venous pressure. Slowly forming effusions allow the pericardium to stretch and can reach large volumes before compromise, so R correctly explains A.
Q_GEN_ANAT_THORAX_024 [M] The thoracic duct, the largest lymphatic channel of the body, most commonly terminates by opening into the venous system at the junction of the:
    A. Right subclavian and right internal jugular veins
    B. Left brachiocephalic vein and the superior vena cava
    C. Azygos vein and the superior vena cava
   *D. Left subclavian and left internal jugular veins
   key=D ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy Vol 1 — Thorax (Thoracic Duct)
   concept: The thoracic duct begins at the cisterna chyli (about L1–L2), enters the thorax through the aortic hiatus (T12), ascends in the posterior mediastinum on the right, crosses to the left at about T5, and finally opens into the venous system at the junction of the left subclavian and left internal jugular veins. It drains lymph from the whole body except the right head and neck, right upper limb and right hemithorax, which are drained by the right lymphatic duct.

=== Anatomy / Pharyngeal arches, germ-layer derivatives
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_001 [E] The muscles of mastication (temporalis, masseter and the pterygoids), along with mylohyoid, anterior belly of digastric, tensor tympani and tensor veli palatini, are all derivatives of which pharyngeal arch?
   *A. First pharyngeal arch
    B. Second pharyngeal arch
    C. Third pharyngeal arch
    D. Fourth pharyngeal arch
   key=A ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Head and Neck (Pharyngeal/Branchial Apparatus)
   concept: The mesoderm of the first (mandibular) arch forms the muscles of mastication plus mylohyoid, anterior belly of digastric, tensor tympani and tensor veli palatini, all supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal (V3), the nerve of the first arch. Second-arch muscles are the muscles of facial expression (VII); the third arch gives only stylopharyngeus (IX); the fourth arch gives cricothyroid and pharyngeal constrictors (X).
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_002 [E] The epidermis of the skin, together with hair, nails and the sebaceous and sweat glands, is derived from which germ layer?
    A. Paraxial mesoderm
    B. Endoderm
   *C. Surface ectoderm
    D. Neural crest
   key=C ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Integumentary System / Third Week (Germ Layer Derivatives)
   concept: The epidermis and its appendages (hair, nails, sebaceous and sweat glands, mammary glands) arise from surface (non-neural) ectoderm; the dermis beneath it is mesodermal (dermatome of somites over the trunk). Endoderm forms gut epithelium and its glands, while neural crest supplies melanocytes that migrate into the epidermis but does not form the keratinocyte layer itself.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_003 [E] The epithelial lining of the gastrointestinal tract and the parenchyma of the liver, pancreas and lungs are derived from which germ layer?
    A. Ectoderm
    B. Paraxial mesoderm
    C. Neural crest
   *D. Endoderm
   key=D ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Digestive System / Germ Layer Derivatives
   concept: Endoderm forms the epithelial lining of the gut tube and the glandular/parenchymal cells of the liver, pancreas, thyroid, parathyroids, thymus and the respiratory epithelium of the lungs, as well as the lining of the bladder and urethra. The surrounding smooth muscle and connective tissue (stroma) of these organs are mesodermal (splanchnic/visceral mesoderm), and the extreme ends of the gut (mouth, anal canal) are ectodermal.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_004 [E] Which cranial nerve is the nerve of the third pharyngeal arch?
    A. Facial nerve (VII)
   *B. Glossopharyngeal nerve (IX)
    C. Vagus nerve (X)
    D. Mandibular nerve (V3)
   key=B ref=IB Singh Human Embryology — Pharyngeal Arches, Pouches and Clefts
   concept: Each pharyngeal arch carries its own cranial nerve: arch 1 = trigeminal (V3), arch 2 = facial (VII), arch 3 = glossopharyngeal (IX), and arches 4 and 6 = branches of the vagus (superior and recurrent laryngeal nerves respectively). The glossopharyngeal nerve therefore supplies the single third-arch muscle, stylopharyngeus, and provides sensation to the posterior third of the tongue.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_005 [E] Only one of the pharyngeal (branchial) clefts gives rise to a definitive adult structure. That first pharyngeal cleft forms which of the following?
   *A. External acoustic meatus
    B. Auditory (pharyngotympanic) tube
    C. Palatine tonsil
    D. Tympanic cavity
   key=A ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Head and Neck (Pharyngeal Clefts)
   concept: The ectodermal first pharyngeal cleft (groove) persists as the external acoustic meatus, and its floor contributes to the outer layer of the tympanic membrane. Clefts 2, 3 and 4 are normally buried and obliterated as the cervical sinus; their persistence causes a branchial cyst or fistula. The auditory tube and tympanic cavity are endodermal first-POUCH derivatives, and the palatine tonsil arises from the second pouch, not from a cleft.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_006 [M] Meckel's cartilage, the cartilage bar of the first pharyngeal arch, gives rise to which pair of middle-ear ossicles?
   *A. Malleus and incus
    B. Incus and stapes
    C. Stapes and styloid process
    D. Malleus and stapes
   key=A ref=IB Singh Human Embryology — Pharyngeal Arch Cartilages / Development of the Ear
   concept: The dorsal end of Meckel's (first-arch) cartilage ossifies into the malleus and incus and also forms the sphenomandibular ligament and anterior ligament of the malleus; the mandible itself develops by intramembranous ossification lateral to this cartilage. The stapes, along with the styloid process, stylohyoid ligament and lesser cornu of the hyoid, is a derivative of Reichert's (second-arch) cartilage, which is why it is not a Meckel's derivative.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_007 [M] During swallowing, a muscle that elevates the pharynx and larynx is supplied by the glossopharyngeal nerve and is the only muscle derived from the third pharyngeal arch. Which muscle is this?
    A. Stylohyoid
   *B. Stylopharyngeus
    C. Styloglossus
    D. Palatopharyngeus
   key=B ref=BD Chaurasia's Human Anatomy — Head and Neck / Development of the Pharyngeal Arches
   concept: Stylopharyngeus is the sole muscle of the third pharyngeal arch and is therefore supplied by that arch's nerve, the glossopharyngeal (IX). Stylohyoid is a second-arch muscle (facial nerve, VII); styloglossus is a tongue muscle derived from occipital myotomes and supplied by the hypoglossal (XII); palatopharyngeus is a fourth/sixth-arch derivative supplied by the vagus (X) via the pharyngeal plexus.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_008 [H] The cricothyroid muscle, a derivative of the fourth pharyngeal arch, is supplied by which nerve?
    A. Recurrent laryngeal nerve
    B. Internal laryngeal nerve
   *C. External laryngeal nerve
    D. Glossopharyngeal nerve
   key=C ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Head and Neck (Fourth and Sixth Arch Derivatives)
   concept: The fourth arch gives rise to cricothyroid (and the pharyngeal constrictors and levator veli palatini), supplied by the external laryngeal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve of the vagus. All the remaining intrinsic laryngeal muscles are sixth-arch derivatives supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve. The internal laryngeal nerve is purely sensory (above the vocal folds), so it supplies no muscle.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_009 [M] The endoderm of the first pharyngeal pouch forms the tubotympanic recess, which develops into which of the following?
    A. Palatine tonsil
   *B. Tympanic cavity and auditory tube
    C. Inferior parathyroid gland
    D. Thymus
   key=B ref=IB Singh Human Embryology — Pharyngeal Pouches / Development of the Middle Ear
   concept: The first pharyngeal pouch expands into the tubotympanic recess, whose distal (dilated) part becomes the tympanic (middle-ear) cavity and mastoid antrum while its proximal (narrow) part becomes the auditory (pharyngotympanic) tube. The palatine tonsil comes from the second pouch; the inferior parathyroid and thymus come from the third pouch; the superior parathyroid and ultimobranchial body come from the fourth pouch.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_010 [M] The surface epithelium and the crypts of the palatine tonsil are derived from the endoderm of which pharyngeal pouch?
    A. Middle ear cavity
    B. Thymus
   *C. Palatine tonsil
    D. Superior parathyroid gland
   key=C ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Head and Neck (Pharyngeal Pouches)
   concept: This question tests the second pharyngeal pouch, whose endoderm proliferates into buds that form the surface epithelium and lining of the tonsillar crypts of the palatine tonsil; the surrounding lymphoid tissue is later infiltrated by mesoderm-derived lymphocytes. The other listed structures belong to different pouches: middle ear = pouch 1, thymus = pouch 3, superior parathyroid = pouch 4, making palatine tonsil the correct second-pouch derivative.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_011 [H] The inferior parathyroid glands and the thymus both develop from the endoderm of which pharyngeal pouch?
    A. First pouch
    B. Second pouch
    C. Fourth pouch
   *D. Third pouch
   key=D ref=IB Singh Human Embryology — Pharyngeal Pouches (Parathyroid and Thymic Development)
   concept: The dorsal wing of the third pouch forms the inferior parathyroid gland and the ventral wing forms the thymus. As the thymus migrates caudally into the thorax it drags the inferior parathyroid with it, so the inferior parathyroids end up BELOW the superior parathyroids, which arise from the fourth pouch and migrate less. This crossing-over of migration explains the paradoxical final positions of the parathyroid glands.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_012 [H] The superior parathyroid glands and the ultimobranchial body develop from which pharyngeal pouch?
    A. Second pouch
    B. Third pouch
   *C. Fourth pouch
    D. First pouch
   key=C ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Head and Neck (Fourth Pouch Derivatives)
   concept: The dorsal wing of the fourth pouch forms the superior parathyroid gland, while its ventral wing forms the ultimobranchial (postbranchial) body, which is incorporated into the thyroid and gives rise to the calcitonin-secreting parafollicular C cells. Because the superior parathyroids migrate only a short distance, they remain above the inferior parathyroids despite arising from a more caudal pouch than the third-pouch inferior glands.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_013 [H] Assertion (A): The parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid gland secrete calcitonin. Reason (R): The parafollicular C cells are derived from the ultimobranchial body of the fourth pharyngeal pouch. Choose the best option.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
   *B. Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=B ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Head and Neck / Thyroid Gland (Ultimobranchial Body)
   concept: Both statements are individually true: parafollicular C cells do secrete calcitonin, and they are indeed carried into the thyroid within the ultimobranchial body of the fourth pouch (the C cells themselves being neural-crest cells that colonise that body). However, the embryonic ORIGIN of the C cells does not explain WHY they secrete calcitonin, so the reason is not the correct explanation of the assertion, making B the right choice.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_014 [M] A 20-year-old presents with a smooth, painless, fluctuant swelling at the anterior border of the upper third of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. It has been slowly enlarging and does not move on swallowing or tongue protrusion. Which lesion, arising from remnants of the cervical sinus, is most likely?
   *A. Branchial (cervical) cyst
    B. Thyroglossal duct cyst
    C. Cystic hygroma
    D. Sublingual dermoid cyst
   key=A ref=Langman's Medical Embryology — Head and Neck (Pharyngeal Cleft Anomalies)
   concept: A branchial cyst results from failure of the cervical sinus (formed by the buried second, third and fourth clefts) to obliterate, producing a fluctuant swelling classically at the junction of the upper and middle thirds of the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid; a persistent tract opening onto the skin is a branchial fistula. A thyroglossal cyst is midline and moves on tongue protrusion, a cystic hygroma is a soft transilluminant lymphatic malformation, and a dermoid lies in the midline floor of the mouth.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_015 [M] A 9-year-old child has a painless midline neck swelling near the level of the hyoid bone that moves upward when the tongue is protruded and when the child swallows. Which developmental anomaly does this describe?
    A. Branchial cyst
   *B. Thyroglossal duct cyst
    C. Enlarged submental lymph node
    D. Sublingual dermoid cyst
   key=B ref=IB Singh Human Embryology — Development of the Thyroid Gland (Thyroglossal Duct)
   concept: A thyroglossal duct cyst arises from persistence of the thyroglossal duct, the tract along which the thyroid descended from the foramen cecum to the neck. Because the duct is attached superiorly to the foramen cecum of the tongue and passes in close relation to the hyoid, the cyst characteristically moves upward on protrusion of the tongue (and on swallowing) — a sign that distinguishes it from a laterally placed branchial cyst, a fixed lymph node, or a floor-of-mouth dermoid.
Q_GEN_ANAT_EMBRYO_016 [M] The foramen cecum, located at the junction of the anterior two-thirds and posterior third of the tongue, marks the point of origin of which structure?
    A. Palatine tonsil
    B. Parathyroid gland
   *C. Thyroid gland

=== Anatomy / Spinal cord tracts & lesions
Q_GEN_ANAT_NEURO_TRACTS_001 [E] The dorsal (posterior) column–medial lemniscus pathway of the spinal cord is chiefly responsible for carrying which of the following sensory modalities?

   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Neuroanatomy — The Spinal Cord and Ascending Tracts
   concept: The dorsal columns (fasciculus gracilis and cuneatus) carry fine discriminative touch, vibration, two-point discrimination and conscious joint-position sense. Q_GEN_ANAT_NEURO_TRACTS_002 [E] The lateral spinothalamic tract is the principal ascending pathway for which of the following sensations?

    B. Vibration and conscious proprioception
    C. Fine discriminative (two-point) touch
    D. Motor commands to skeletal muscle
   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Neuroanatomy — The Spinal Cord and Ascending Tracts
   concept: The lateral spinothalamic tract conveys pain and temperature. First-order fibers enter and travel a short distance in the dorsolateral tract of Lissauer and synapse in the dorsal horn; second-order fibers cross in the anterior white commissure and ascend contralaterally to the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus. Because the fibers cross close to their level of entry, a cord lesion causes contralateral pain/temperature loss beginning one to two segments below.

   *A. Sacral fibres are laminated most laterally and are spared by a centrally expanding lesion
    B. Sacral fibres decussate only in the medulla
    C. Sacral sensation is carried entirely in the dorsal columns
    D. Sacral dermatomes have dual (bilateral) cord innervation
   key=A ref=Vishram Singh, Textbook of Clinical Neuroanatomy, 3rd ed., Ch. 6 (Spinal Cord)
   concept: In the lateral spinothalamic tract fibres are somatotopically laminated, with sacral segments lying most laterally (superficially) and cervical segments most medially. A centrally arising intramedullary tumour or syrinx therefore destroys the medial cervical/thoracic fibres first while the laterally placed sacral fibres are spared, producing 'sacral sparing.' An extramedullary compressive lesion tends instead to involve sacral sensation early.
Q_GEN_ANAT_NEURO_TRACTS_025 [M] The fasciculus cuneatus is present in the dorsal columns only above the mid-thoracic (approximately T6) level. Which information does it convey?
   *A. Fine touch, vibration and conscious proprioception from the upper limb, upper trunk and neck
    B. Pain and temperature from the lower limb
    C. Conscious proprioception from the lower limb and lower trunk
    D. Voluntary motor commands to the upper limb
   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Neuroanatomy, 8th ed., Ch. 4 (The Spinal Cord and the Ascending and Descending Tracts)
   concept: The dorsal columns comprise a medial fasciculus gracilis and a lateral fasciculus cuneatus. The cuneatus is added at about the T6 level and carries discriminative touch, vibration and conscious proprioception from the upper limb, upper trunk and neck; below T6 only the gracilis (lower limb and lower trunk) is present. Cuneatus fibres synapse in the nucleus cuneatus of the medulla.
Q_GEN_ANAT_NEURO_TRACTS_026 [H] A comatose patient after a midbrain haemorrhage shows extension and internal rotation of all four limbs (decerebrate posturing). Loss of which descending influence, leaving vestibulospinal and pontine reticulospinal drive to the extensors unopposed, best explains this posture?
   *A. Rubrospinal tract facilitation of flexor tone
    B. Lateral corticospinal tract control of the hands
    C. Dorsal spinocerebellar proprioceptive feedback
    D. Fasciculus gracilis sensory input
   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Neuroanatomy, 8th ed., Ch. 4 (The Spinal Cord and the Ascending and Descending Tracts)
   concept: Decerebrate rigidity (extension of all four limbs) follows a brainstem lesion below the level of the red nucleus. Removing the rubrospinal tract's facilitation of flexor muscle tone leaves the extensor drive of the lateral vestibulospinal and pontine reticulospinal tracts unopposed, so extensor tone dominates. A lesion above the red nucleus (e.g., internal capsule) spares rubrospinal flexor facilitation and instead produces decorticate posturing with arm flexion.
Q_GEN_ANAT_NEURO_TRACTS_027 [M] The largest neurons contributing axons to the corticospinal tract, the giant Betz cells, are found in which layer of the primary motor cortex?
   *A. Layer V (internal pyramidal layer)
    B. Layer I (molecular layer)
    C. Layer IV (internal granular layer)
    D. Layer VI (multiform layer)
   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Neuroanatomy, 8th ed., Ch. 4 (The Spinal Cord and the Ascending and Descending Tracts)
   concept: Only about 30% of corticospinal fibres arise from the primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4), with the rest from premotor and sensory areas. The giant Betz cells, the largest neurons of the primary motor cortex, lie in cortical layer V (the internal pyramidal layer) and contribute the fastest-conducting, largest-diameter corticospinal axons, though they form only a small fraction of the tract's total fibres.
Q_GEN_ANAT_NEURO_TRACTS_028 [H] A patient with a cervical syrinx develops a right-sided Horner's syndrome (partial ptosis, miosis and facial anhidrosis). Damage to which grey-matter structure, the origin of pre-ganglionic sympathetic fibres, accounts for this?
   *A. Intermediolateral cell column (lateral horn) at C8–T2
    B. Nucleus dorsalis of Clarke
    C. Substantia gelatinosa
    D. Anterior (ventral) horn motor neurons
   key=A ref=Snell's Clinical Neuroanatomy, 8th ed., Ch. 4 (The Spinal Cord and the Ascending and Descending Tracts)
   concept: Pre-ganglionic sympathetic neurons occupy the intermediolateral cell column (lateral horn) of spinal segments T1–L2, and those supplying the head and eye emerge from C8–T2 (the ciliospinal centre of Budge). A cervical syrinx expanding into this lateral horn interrupts the oculosympathetic outflow, producing an ipsilateral Horner's syndrome of partial ptosis, miosis and facial anhidrosis.
