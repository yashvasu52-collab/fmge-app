
=== Orthopedics / Bone tumours; CTEV, DDH
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_001 [E] Which of the following is the most common benign bone tumour?
    A. Osteoid osteoma
    B. Enchondroma
   *C. Osteochondroma (osteocartilaginous exostosis)
    D. Giant cell tumour
   key=C ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Bone Tumours (Benign Tumours)
   concept: Osteochondroma is the commonest benign bone tumour, arising from the metaphysis of long bones (typically around the knee) as a bony projection capped by cartilage that points away from the adjacent joint. Malignant change to chondrosarcoma is rare (~1%), suggested by a cartilage cap thicker than 2 cm or renewed growth after skeletal maturity. Osteoid osteoma, enchondroma and giant cell tumour are all less common.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_002 [M] A 19-year-old man complains of well-localised bone pain that is worst at night and is dramatically relieved by aspirin or other NSAIDs. Radiograph shows a small (<1 cm) radiolucent nidus surrounded by dense reactive sclerosis in the femoral cortex. The most likely diagnosis is:
   *A. Osteoid osteoma
    B. Osteoblastoma
    C. Brodie's abscess
    D. Stress fracture
   key=A ref=Apley's System of Orthopaedics and Fractures — Bone Tumours
   concept: Osteoid osteoma classically causes night pain relieved by NSAIDs because the nidus produces prostaglandins; the nidus is a small (<1 cm) radiolucent focus with surrounding reactive sclerosis, best localised on CT. Osteoblastoma is larger (>2 cm), favours the spine and has less night-pain/NSAID response. Brodie's abscess is subacute osteomyelitis, and a stress fracture shows a periosteal reaction along a stress line rather than a discrete nidus.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_003 [M] The combination of multiple enchondromas with soft-tissue haemangiomas is known as:
    A. Ollier disease
    B. McCune-Albright syndrome
    C. Gardner syndrome
   *D. Maffucci syndrome
   key=D ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Bone Tumours (Cartilaginous Tumours)
   concept: Maffucci syndrome is multiple enchondromatosis plus soft-tissue haemangiomas, whereas Ollier disease is multiple enchondromatosis without haemangiomas; both carry an increased risk of malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma, higher in Maffucci. McCune-Albright is polyostotic fibrous dysplasia with café-au-lait spots and endocrinopathy, and Gardner syndrome combines colonic polyposis with osteomas and desmoid tumours.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_004 [H] A 16-year-old presents with knee pain. Radiograph shows a well-defined lytic lesion confined to the epiphysis of the proximal tibia with fine internal calcification, and the growth plate is still open. Biopsy shows chondroid cells with 'chicken-wire' calcification. The diagnosis is:
    A. Giant cell tumour
   *B. Chondroblastoma
    C. Chondromyxoid fibroma
    D. Osteosarcoma
   key=B ref=Campbell's Operative Orthopaedics — Benign Bone Tumours
   concept: Chondroblastoma is a rare benign tumour arising in the epiphysis/apophysis of a skeletally immature patient (open physis), showing 'chicken-wire' calcification and S-100 positivity. Giant cell tumour is also epiphyseal but occurs after physeal closure in the mature skeleton. Chondromyxoid fibroma is metaphyseal, and osteosarcoma is an aggressive metaphyseal lesion with bone destruction and periosteal reaction, not a well-defined epiphyseal lesion.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_005 [E] Osteosarcoma most commonly arises at which anatomical location?
    A. Diaphysis of the femur
    B. Epiphysis of the proximal humerus
   *C. Metaphysis around the knee
    D. Vertebral body
   key=C ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Malignant Bone Tumours (Osteosarcoma)
   concept: Osteosarcoma most commonly arises in the metaphysis of long bones around the knee (distal femur > proximal tibia), followed by the proximal humerus, typically in adolescents. Radiological hallmarks are sunburst/sunray spiculation and Codman's triangle from aggressive periosteal reaction. The diaphysis is the classic site of Ewing sarcoma, the epiphysis suggests chondroblastoma or giant cell tumour, and vertebral bodies are common sites for metastases and myeloma.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_006 [M] The characteristic cytogenetic translocation of Ewing sarcoma is:
   *A. t(11;22)(q24;q12)
    B. t(9;22)(q34;q11)
    C. t(X;18)(p11;q11)
    D. t(2;13)(q35;q14)
   key=A ref=Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease — Bones and Joints (cross-referenced with Apley's)
   concept: Ewing sarcoma is defined by t(11;22)(q24;q12) producing the EWSR1-FLI1 fusion; it is a small round blue cell tumour that is CD99 (MIC2) positive with PAS-positive intracytoplasmic glycogen, arising in the diaphysis with an onion-skin (laminated) periosteal reaction. t(9;22) is the Philadelphia chromosome of CML/ALL, t(X;18) is seen in synovial sarcoma, and t(2;13) occurs in alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_007 [M] A 62-year-old man has back pain, anaemia and a markedly raised ESR. Skull radiograph shows multiple well-defined 'punched-out' lytic lesions without a sclerotic rim, and urine contains Bence-Jones protein. The most likely diagnosis is:
    A. Osteoporosis
   *B. Multiple myeloma
    C. Metastatic prostate carcinoma
    D. Primary hyperparathyroidism
   key=B ref=Apley's System of Orthopaedics and Fractures — Bone Tumours (Myeloma)
   concept: Multiple myeloma is a plasma-cell malignancy causing punched-out lytic lesions without a sclerotic rim, Bence-Jones (light-chain) proteinuria, an M-band, rouleaux formation, hypercalcaemia and renal failure; lesions are typically 'cold' on a technetium bone scan because there is no osteoblastic reaction. Prostatic metastases are characteristically osteoblastic, and hyperparathyroidism produces brown tumours and a salt-and-pepper skull rather than discrete punched-out lesions.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_008 [E] The most common malignant tumour of bone overall is:
    A. Osteosarcoma
    B. Ewing sarcoma
    C. Chondrosarcoma
   *D. Metastatic carcinoma
   key=D ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Metastatic Bone Tumours
   concept: Metastatic carcinoma is by far the commonest malignant tumour of bone; the primaries are recalled by 'BLT-KP' — Breast, Lung, Thyroid, Kidney and Prostate — and they favour the axial (red-marrow) skeleton. Osteosarcoma is the commonest primary malignant bone tumour if myeloma is excluded. Most metastases are osteolytic, while prostate carcinoma is characteristically osteoblastic.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_009 [H] Which primary malignancy most characteristically produces osteoblastic (sclerotic) bone metastases?
    A. Renal cell carcinoma
    B. Thyroid carcinoma
   *C. Prostate carcinoma
    D. Lung carcinoma
   key=C ref=Campbell's Operative Orthopaedics — Metastatic Disease of Bone
   concept: Prostate carcinoma classically causes osteoblastic (sclerotic) metastases, associated with a raised alkaline phosphatase and PSA, whereas breast cancer produces mixed lesions. Renal cell and thyroid carcinomas typically give expansile, vascular, osteolytic 'blow-out' metastases (which can be pulsatile), and lung carcinoma metastases are usually lytic. Recognising blastic versus lytic patterns helps direct the search for the primary tumour.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_010 [M] A 30-year-old woman has distal femoral pain. Radiograph shows an eccentric, expansile 'soap-bubble' lytic lesion at the epiphysis extending up to the subchondral bone, with no surrounding sclerotic rim, and the growth plate is fused. The most likely diagnosis is:
    A. Aneurysmal bone cyst
   *B. Giant cell tumour
    C. Osteosarcoma
    D. Chondrosarcoma
   key=B ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Giant Cell Tumour
   concept: Giant cell tumour (osteoclastoma) is benign but locally aggressive, occurring in the mature skeleton (after physeal closure, typically 20-40 years) at the epi-metaphyseal region extending to subchondral bone; it shows an eccentric lytic 'soap-bubble' appearance without a sclerotic margin. It has a high local recurrence after curettage and rarely gives benign lung metastases. Campanacci grading describes its radiological aggressiveness; ABC shows fluid-fluid levels and osteosarcoma shows aggressive bone destruction.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_011 [M] Fluid-fluid levels on MRI within an expansile, eccentric metaphyseal lytic lesion in an adolescent are most characteristic of:
   *A. Aneurysmal bone cyst
    B. Simple bone cyst
    C. Enchondroma
    D. Osteoid osteoma
   key=A ref=Apley's System of Orthopaedics and Fractures — Cystic Lesions of Bone
   concept: An aneurysmal bone cyst contains blood-filled spaces that produce fluid-fluid levels on MRI/CT due to sedimentation; it is an eccentric, expansile 'blown-out' metaphyseal lesion in patients under 20 and may be secondary to other lesions such as GCT, chondroblastoma or osteosarcoma. A simple bone cyst is centrally located, an enchondroma shows chondroid calcification, and osteoid osteoma has a sclerotic nidus. Because telangiectatic osteosarcoma can also show fluid-fluid levels, biopsy is still needed.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_012 [M] A 10-year-old boy sustains a pathological fracture through a well-defined central lytic lesion in the proximal humeral metaphysis. A small bony fragment is seen lying within the lesion (the 'fallen fragment sign'). The diagnosis is:
    A. Aneurysmal bone cyst
    B. Fibrous dysplasia
    C. Ewing sarcoma
   *D. Unicameral (simple) bone cyst
   key=D ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Simple Bone Cyst
   concept: A unicameral (simple) bone cyst is a fluid-filled cavity that is centrally located in the proximal humeral or femoral metaphysis in children and often presents as a pathological fracture; the 'fallen fragment/leaf sign' — a cortical fragment sinking to the dependent part of the fluid-filled cyst — is pathognomonic. An aneurysmal bone cyst is eccentric with fluid-fluid levels, and treatment of a simple cyst is by aspiration and steroid injection or curettage with bone grafting.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_013 [H] A young girl has precocious puberty, café-au-lait macules with irregular 'coast of Maine' borders, and a ground-glass lytic lesion of the proximal femur producing a 'shepherd's crook' deformity. This constellation is best described as:
    A. Neurofibromatosis type 1
    B. Ollier disease
    C. Osteogenesis imperfecta
   *D. McCune-Albright syndrome
   key=D ref=Campbell's Operative Orthopaedics — Fibrous Dysplasia
   concept: McCune-Albright syndrome combines polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait macules with irregular 'coast of Maine' borders and endocrinopathy (classically precocious puberty), caused by a post-zygotic GNAS1 mutation. Fibrous dysplasia gives a ground-glass appearance and a 'shepherd's crook' (coxa vara) deformity of the proximal femur. Neurofibromatosis type 1 has smooth 'coast of California' café-au-lait spots with neurofibromas, distinguishing it from this presentation.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_014 [H] An asymptomatic, well-defined eccentric metaphyseal lucent lesion with a sclerotic scalloped margin, found incidentally in a child and usually regressing spontaneously, is:
    A. Chondrosarcoma
    B. Osteosarcoma
   *C. Non-ossifying fibroma
    D. Brodie's abscess
   key=C ref=Apley's System of Orthopaedics and Fractures — Fibrous Lesions of Bone
   concept: Non-ossifying fibroma (fibroxanthoma), when small and cortex-based, is called a fibrous cortical defect; it is a very common, asymptomatic, incidental eccentric metaphyseal lucency with a sclerotic scalloped border in children and adolescents, and it typically ossifies and regresses spontaneously. It is a 'leave-me-alone/do-not-touch' lesion needing no treatment unless it is large and threatens pathological fracture. Chondrosarcoma and osteosarcoma are aggressive, and Brodie's abscess is subacute osteomyelitis.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_015 [H] Which statement about chondrosarcoma is correct?
    A. It typically affects children under 10 years of age
   *B. It is largely radio- and chemo-resistant and is treated by wide surgical excision
    C. It arises most often in the small bones of the hand
    D. Codman's triangle and sunburst spiculation are its radiological hallmarks
   key=B ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Chondrosarcoma
   concept: Chondrosarcoma is a malignant cartilage-forming tumour of older adults (40-60 years) affecting axial and flat bones such as the pelvis, proximal femur, ribs and shoulder girdle; it is relatively radio- and chemo-resistant, so wide en-bloc surgical excision is the mainstay of treatment. It is not a childhood tumour, enchondromas of the small hand bones rarely turn malignant, and sunburst/Codman's triangle are features of osteosarcoma rather than chondrosarcoma.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_016 [H] Which of the following is a fundamental principle of bone-tumour biopsy?
    A. Excisional biopsy is preferred for all suspected malignant tumours
    B. Biopsy should always precede clinical and radiological assessment
    C. The biopsy tract should be placed transversely, away from the definitive resection plane
   *D. The biopsy tract must be placed so that it can be excised en-bloc with the definitive resection
   key=D ref=Campbell's Operative Orthopaedics — Principles of Tumour Biopsy and Enneking Staging
   concept: Biopsy is the last step, performed after clinical evaluation and imaging and ideally at the definitive treating centre; the tract must be longitudinal and positioned so it can be removed en-bloc with the tumour at definitive surgery, because a poorly placed or transverse tract seeds tumour and can convert a limb-salvage case into an amputation. For suspected malignancy, incisional or core-needle biopsy is preferred over excisional biopsy. Enneking's surgical staging uses grade, site/compartment and metastasis.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_017 [E] The deformities of congenital talipes equinovarus (CTEV) are remembered by the mnemonic 'CAVE'. Which of the following is NOT a component of CTEV?
    A. Midfoot cavus
    B. Forefoot adductus
   *C. Hindfoot valgus
    D. Ankle equinus
   key=C ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Congenital Talipes Equinovarus (CTEV)
   concept: The four components of clubfoot (CTEV) are given by CAVE: Cavus of the midfoot, Adductus of the forefoot, Varus of the hindfoot and Equinus of the ankle. The hindfoot in CTEV is in VARUS, not valgus, so hindfoot valgus is not a component. In the Ponseti sequence the cavus is corrected first and the hindfoot varus and equinus are corrected last, equinus often requiring a tendo-Achilles tenotomy.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_018 [M] In the Ponseti method for idiopathic clubfoot, after serial weekly manipulation and casting the residual ankle equinus is most often corrected by:
   *A. Percutaneous tendo-Achilles tenotomy
    B. Extensive posteromedial soft-tissue release
    C. Triple arthrodesis
    D. Tibialis anterior tendon transfer
   key=A ref=Apley's System of Orthopaedics and Fractures — Clubfoot (Ponseti Method)
   concept: In the Ponseti technique, serial manipulation and casting correct the cavus, then adductus, then varus; a tight tendo-Achilles causing residual equinus is corrected by a percutaneous Achilles tenotomy in roughly 80-90% of cases, after which a foot-abduction (Denis Browne) brace is worn to prevent relapse. Extensive posteromedial soft-tissue release is now largely avoided because it causes stiffness, while triple arthrodesis and tendon transfers are reserved for older or relapsed feet.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_019 [H] Assertion (A): Idiopathic CTEV generally responds better to Ponseti casting than syndromic clubfoot. Reason (R): Syndromic clubfoot, as in arthrogryposis and myelomeningocele, is rigid, more resistant to correction and has a higher relapse rate. Choose the correct option:
   *A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=Campbell's Operative Orthopaedics — Congenital Clubfoot (Idiopathic vs Syndromic)
   concept: Idiopathic clubfoot is supple and responds well to Ponseti casting, whereas syndromic or teratologic clubfoot associated with arthrogryposis, spina bifida/myelomeningocele or diastrophic dysplasia is rigid, resistant to correction and relapses more often, frequently requiring surgery. Both the assertion and the reason are true, and the rigidity/resistance described in the reason is precisely why syndromic feet respond less well, so R correctly explains A.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_020 [M] During newborn hip screening, the examiner adducts the flexed hip and applies a posteriorly directed force, feeling the femoral head slip out of the acetabulum. This provocative manoeuvre is the:
    A. Ortolani test
   *B. Barlow test
    C. Galeazzi test
    D. Thomas test
   key=B ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (Clinical Tests)
   concept: The Barlow test is the provocative (dislocating) test: the flexed hip is adducted and pushed posteriorly, and a dislocatable head is felt to slip out of the acetabulum. The Ortolani test is the reduction test, in which abduction with an anterior lift relocates an already-dislocated hip with a palpable clunk. The Galeazzi sign detects unequal knee heights from femoral shortening/dislocation, and the Thomas test assesses a fixed flexion deformity of the hip.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_021 [M] For diagnosing developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) in an infant younger than 6 months, the investigation of choice is:
    A. Plain radiograph (AP pelvis)
    B. CT scan
    C. MRI
   *D. Ultrasonography (Graf method)
   key=D ref=Apley's System of Orthopaedics and Fractures — Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (Imaging)
   concept: Before about 4-6 months the femoral head is largely cartilaginous and its ossific nucleus has not appeared, so plain radiographs are unreliable; ultrasonography (Graf's method, using alpha and beta angles) is the imaging modality of choice and allows dynamic assessment. After roughly 6 months, once the ossific nucleus appears, plain radiographs become useful using Hilgenreiner's and Perkin's lines, Shenton's line and the acetabular index. CT/MRI are reserved for complex or post-reduction assessment.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_022 [H] A 3-year-old girl presents with a painless limp. Examination shows a positive Trendelenburg sign, limb shortening and an extra thigh skin crease, and neglected unilateral DDH is diagnosed. The most appropriate management is:
    A. Pavlik harness
    B. Observation until skeletal maturity
   *C. Open reduction with femoral and/or pelvic osteotomy
    D. Closed reduction with a hip spica cast alone
   key=C ref=Campbell's Operative Orthopaedics — Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (Late Presentation)
   concept: A Pavlik harness is effective only in early infancy (younger than about 6 months); in a walking child with neglected/established DDH, closed reduction usually fails, so open reduction combined with femoral shortening-derotation and/or a pelvic osteotomy (Salter or Pemberton) is required to achieve and maintain a concentric, stable hip. A positive Trendelenburg sign reflects abductor (gluteus medius) insufficiency because the dislocated head loses its normal fulcrum. Simple observation would leave the hip dysplastic and painful.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_023 [E] Achondroplasia, the commonest form of short-limb (rhizomelic) dwarfism, is caused by a mutation in which gene?
    A. COL1A1 gene
   *B. FGFR3 gene
    C. FBN1 gene
    D. GNAS1 gene
   key=B ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics — Skeletal Dysplasias (Achondroplasia)
   concept: Achondroplasia is an autosomal dominant condition (most cases arising from new mutations, with a paternal age effect) caused by a gain-of-function mutation in FGFR3 that impairs endochondral ossification, producing rhizomelic (proximal) limb shortening, a trident hand, frontal bossing, spinal canal stenosis, but a normal trunk and normal intelligence. COL1A1 mutations cause osteogenesis imperfecta, FBN1 causes Marfan syndrome, and GNAS1 is implicated in fibrous dysplasia/McCune-Albright syndrome.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_TUMOR_CONG_024 [M] Assertion (A): Osteogenesis imperfecta classically presents with recurrent fractures and blue sclerae. Reason (R): It results from a qualitative or quantitative defect in type I collagen. Choose the correct option:
   *A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=Apley's System of Orthopaedics and Fractures — Osteogenesis Imperfecta
   concept: Osteogenesis imperfecta ('brittle bone disease') arises from defective type I collagen (COL1A1/COL1A2 mutations), producing fragile bones with recurrent low-trauma fractures, blue sclerae (thin sclera revealing the underlying choroid), dentinogenesis imperfecta, conductive hearing loss, ligamentous laxity and wormian bones in the skull. Both the assertion and the reason are true, and the type I collagen defect directly explains both the fragility and the blue sclerae, so R is the correct explanation of A.
Q_S_005 [M] A 15-year-old presents with a painful swelling around the knee. X-ray of the lower femur shows a destructive metaphyseal lesion with a 'sunburst/sunray' spiculated pattern and a Codman's triangle. The most likely diagnosis is:
    A. Ewing's sarcoma
   *B. Osteosarcoma
    C. Giant cell tumour
    D. Osteochondroma
   key=B ref=Maheshwari, Essential Orthopaedics
   concept: Osteosarcoma occurs in adolescents at the metaphysis around the knee (lower femur/upper tibia), with 'sunburst' spiculation and Codman's triangle (elevated periosteum). Ewing's sarcoma shows an 'onion-peel' lamellated periosteal reaction and favours the diaphysis; giant cell tumour is epiphyseal/subarticular in the mature skeleton.
Q_S_006 [E] During newborn examination the paediatrician flexes and abducts the hip and feels a palpable 'clunk' as the femoral head relocates into the acetabulum, suggesting developmental dysplasia of the hip. This manoeuvre is known as:
    A. Barlow's test
   *B. Ortolani's test
    C. Trendelenburg test
    D. Thomas test
   key=B ref=Maheshwari, Essential Orthopaedics
   concept: Ortolani's test relocates a dislocated hip (abduction with a palpable clunk of reduction), while Barlow's test is the provocative dislocation test (adduction plus posterior pressure). Both screen for DDH in neonates; ultrasound is the imaging of choice under 6 months of age.

=== Orthopedics / Osteomyelitis & TB spine
Q_GEN_ORTHO_INFECTION_001 [E] Which single organism is the commonest cause of acute haematogenous osteomyelitis across all age groups?
    A. Streptococcus pyogenes
   *B. Staphylococcus aureus
    C. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
    D. Salmonella typhi
   key=B ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics, 6th ed — Bone and Joint Infections (Acute Osteomyelitis)
   concept: Staphylococcus aureus is by far the most frequent organism in acute haematogenous osteomyelitis at all ages because of its adhesins that bind bone matrix and its ability to survive intracellularly. Streptococcus pyogenes causes a minority of cases; Pseudomonas is linked to IV drug use and puncture wounds; Salmonella is characteristic of sickle-cell disease. Knowing S. aureus predominates guides empirical anti-staphylococcal antibiotic choice before cultures return.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_INFECTION_002 [E] A 7-year-old known to have sickle-cell disease develops fever with pain and swelling of the forearm and is diagnosed with osteomyelitis. Which organism is characteristically associated with osteomyelitis in this setting?
    A. Escherichia coli
    B. Group B Streptococcus
   *C. Salmonella species
    D. Neisseria gonorrhoeae
   key=C ref=Apley & Solomon's System of Orthopaedics and Trauma, 10th ed — Acute Haematogenous Osteomyelitis
   concept: Salmonella osteomyelitis is classically over-represented in sickle-cell disease, attributed to micro-infarction of gut and bone that seeds Salmonella and to functional hyposplenism impairing clearance of encapsulated organisms. Although Staphylococcus aureus is still numerically common even in sickle-cell patients, Salmonella is the association exams test. E. coli and Group B Strep suit neonates, and gonococcus causes septic arthritis in sexually active adults, not this presentation.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_INFECTION_003 [M] Assertion (A): In neonatal osteomyelitis, spread of infection into the adjacent joint (secondary septic arthritis) is rare. Reason (R): In neonates and infants, some metaphyseal blood vessels cross the physis (growth plate) to reach the epiphysis. Choose the correct option.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true, but R is false
   *D. A is false, but R is true
   key=D ref=Apley & Solomon's System of Orthopaedics and Trauma, 10th ed — Osteomyelitis in Infants
   concept: The assertion is false: in neonates and infants joint spread is actually common, not rare. The reason is true — before about 18 months some metaphyseal vessels perforate the physis into the epiphysis, so infection readily reaches the joint, causing secondary septic arthritis and growth-plate damage. In older children the physis acts as a barrier, confining infection to the metaphysis. Because a false assertion pairs with a true reason, the answer is 'A false, R true'.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_INFECTION_004 [M] A 26-year-old intravenous drug user presents with pain, swelling and warmth of the sternoclavicular joint with adjacent bone involvement. Besides Staphylococcus aureus, which organism is classically associated with bone and joint infection at unusual sites in this population?
   *A. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
    B. Salmonella enteritidis
    C. Group B Streptococcus
    D. Coagulase-negative Staphylococcus
   key=A ref=Campbell's Operative Orthopaedics, 14th ed — Osteomyelitis (special settings)
   concept: Intravenous drug users classically develop infection at unusual axial sites (sternoclavicular, sacroiliac, vertebral) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the organism characteristically over-represented in this group along with S. aureus. Salmonella suggests sickle-cell disease, Group B Streptococcus suits neonates, and coagulase-negative staphylococci (S. epidermidis) characterise prosthetic/implant infection via biofilm. Recognising the IVDU-Pseudomonas link influences empirical anti-pseudomonal cover and imaging of axial joints.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_INFECTION_005 [M] Acute haematogenous osteomyelitis in children characteristically begins in the metaphysis of long bones. Which feature best explains this predilection?
    A. The metaphysis has the richest anastomotic arterial supply, promoting rapid bacterial clearance
   *B. Sluggish blood flow in the hairpin loops of non-anastomosing metaphyseal capillaries favours bacterial lodgement
    C. The growth plate is the most vascular part of the bone and acts as the primary nidus
    D. Metaphyseal bone is covered by unusually thick periosteum that traps organisms
   key=B ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics, 6th ed — Pathology of Acute Osteomyelitis
   concept: In the child's metaphysis, nutrient-artery branches form hairpin loops that empty into wide, non-anastomosing venous sinusoids where blood flow is sluggish and phagocytic activity is relatively low; this stasis lets circulating bacteria settle and multiply. It is a low-flow, poorly-defended zone, not a rich anastomotic bed (so A is wrong). The physis is relatively avascular and acts as a barrier rather than a nidus (C wrong), and periosteal thickness does not explain metaphyseal seeding (D wrong).
Q_GEN_ORTHO_INFECTION_006 [E] In chronic osteomyelitis, a fragment of dead, avascular bone that has separated from surrounding living bone is termed:
    A. Involucrum
    B. Cloaca
   *C. Sequestrum
    D. Brodie's abscess
   key=C ref=Maheshwari's Essential Orthopaedics, 6th ed — Chronic Osteomyelitis (terminology)
   concept: A sequestrum is a piece of necrotic, avascular bone that has separated from viable bone; because it is dead and lacks blood supply, antibiotics cannot penetrate it and it perpetuates infection until surgically removed (sequestrectomy). By contrast, an involucrum is the new living bone laid down by periosteum around it, a cloaca is the opening through the involucrum through which pus escapes, and a Brodie's abscess is a walled-off subacute focus. Radiologically a sequestrum appears denser than the surrounding osteopenic living bone.
Q_GEN_ORTHO_INFECTION_007 [M] The layer of new, living bone laid down by the stripped and elevated periosteum, which comes to surround infected or dead bone in chronic osteomyelitis, is called:
    A. Sequestrum
    B. Cloaca
    C. Sinus tract
   *D. Involucrum
   key=D ref=Apley & Solomon's System of Orthopaedics and Trauma, 10th ed — Chronic Osteomyelitis
   concept: The involucrum is the sheath of new living bone formed by the periosteum, which is lifted off by subperiosteal pus; it envelops the infected/dead bone and can eventually reconstitute the shaft. It is perforated by openings called cloacae through which pus and sequestra discharge to a skin sinus. The involucrum is living and vascular, opposite to the dead sequestrum it surrounds; distinguishing the two is central to planning saucerisation and sequestrectomy in chronic osteomyelitis.
