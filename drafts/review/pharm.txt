
=== Pharmacology / ADRs, enzyme inducers/inhibitors
Q_c1_028 [E] A 16-year-old on long-term antiepileptic therapy develops gum hypertrophy, hirsutism and coarsening of the facial features. Which drug is responsible?
    A. Sodium valproate
   *B. Phenytoin
    C. Carbamazepine
    D. Ethosuximide
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi 8e, Ch. Antiepileptic Drugs
   concept: Phenytoin characteristically causes gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, coarse facies, megaloblastic anemia (folate interference), and cerebellar signs at toxic levels. It follows zero-order kinetics near therapeutic doses.

=== Pharmacology / Adrenergic agonists/antagonists
Q_c1_032 [H] During preoperative preparation of a patient with pheochromocytoma, which principle must be followed to avoid a hypertensive crisis?
    A. Start a beta-blocker first, then add an alpha-blocker
   *B. Achieve alpha-blockade (e.g. phenoxybenzamine) BEFORE giving any beta-blocker
    C. Use a beta-blocker alone
    D. No pharmacologic blockade is required
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi 8e, Ch. Adrenergic Antagonists
   concept: In pheochromocytoma, alpha-blockade must precede beta-blockade. Giving a beta-blocker first leaves alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed, precipitating a severe hypertensive crisis. Phenoxybenzamine (irreversible alpha-blocker) is used first.

=== Pharmacology / Anti-TB drugs & ADRs
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_001 [M] Isoniazid is a prodrug activated by the mycobacterial catalase-peroxidase enzyme. Once activated, its bactericidal effect is due to inhibition of the synthesis of which cell-wall component of Mycobacterium tuberculosis?
   *A. Mycolic acid
    B. Arabinogalactan
    C. Peptidoglycan cross-links
    D. Lipoarabinomannan
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antitubercular Drugs (Isoniazid); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Antimycobacterial Drugs
   concept: Isoniazid is activated by catalase-peroxidase (KatG); the active species inhibits InhA (enoyl-ACP reductase) and KasA, blocking synthesis of mycolic acids — the long-chain fatty acids essential to the mycobacterial cell wall — which is why it is bactericidal against actively dividing bacilli. Arabinogalactan synthesis is blocked by ethambutol (arabinosyl transferase), peptidoglycan is the target of beta-lactams and cycloserine, and lipoarabinomannan is not the isoniazid target.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_002 [M] A 34-year-old man on maintenance phenytoin for epilepsy is started on standard antitubercular therapy. Two weeks later he develops nystagmus, ataxia, slurred speech, and a raised serum phenytoin level. Which antitubercular drug most likely precipitated this?
    A. Rifampicin
   *B. Isoniazid
    C. Ethambutol
    D. Pyrazinamide
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Isoniazid (drug interactions); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics — Chemotherapy of Tuberculosis
   concept: Isoniazid inhibits several cytochrome P450 isoenzymes (CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4, CYP2E1) and thereby slows the metabolism of phenytoin and carbamazepine, raising their plasma levels and causing toxicity (nystagmus, ataxia, sedation), an effect greatest in slow acetylators. Rifampicin does the opposite — it is a potent enzyme inducer that LOWERS phenytoin levels — while ethambutol and pyrazinamide are not clinically significant CYP inhibitors of phenytoin.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_003 [M] A patient on antitubercular therapy develops arthralgia, malaise, and a rash; investigations show positive antinuclear antibody (ANA) and anti-histone antibodies, suggesting a drug-induced lupus-like syndrome. Which drug is the most likely cause?
    A. Ethambutol
    B. Streptomycin
   *C. Isoniazid
    D. Pyrazinamide
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Isoniazid (adverse effects); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Antimycobacterial Drugs
   concept: Isoniazid is a classic cause of a drug-induced lupus (SLE-like) syndrome, characterized by arthralgia, positive ANA, and anti-histone antibodies, and is more frequent in slow acetylators; procainamide and hydralazine are the other textbook culprits. Ethambutol (optic neuritis), streptomycin (ototoxicity/nephrotoxicity), and pyrazinamide (hyperuricemia/hepatotoxicity) do not produce a lupus-like autoimmune syndrome, making isoniazid the answer.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_004 [H] The dose-independent hepatocellular injury caused by isoniazid is attributed chiefly to which mechanism?
    A. Accumulation of pyrazinoic acid in hepatocytes
    B. Formation of an insoluble iron-drug chelate
    C. Inhibition of mitochondrial ATP synthase
   *D. Generation of toxic acetylhydrazine and hydrazine metabolites
   key=D ref=Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics — Chemotherapy of Tuberculosis (isoniazid toxicity); KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed
   concept: Isoniazid hepatotoxicity is attributed to reactive metabolites — acetylhydrazine and hydrazine — generated during acetylation and hydrolysis, which cause hepatocellular necrosis; risk rises with age, chronic alcohol use, pre-existing liver disease, and concurrent rifampicin (an inducer). Pyrazinoic acid is the active metabolite of pyrazinamide, iron chelation is not an isoniazid mechanism, and ATP synthase inhibition is the mechanism of bedaquiline, not a cause of hepatic injury.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_005 [E] Pyridoxine is co-administered with isoniazid to prevent an adverse effect. By which mechanism does isoniazid create the underlying vitamin deficiency?
   *A. It increases pyridoxine excretion and forms inactive hydrazones with pyridoxal phosphate
    B. It irreversibly inhibits hepatic CYP3A4
    C. It chelates calcium in the gut lumen
    D. It induces folate deficiency by blocking dihydrofolate reductase
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Isoniazid (pyridoxine prophylaxis); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Isoniazid increases urinary excretion of pyridoxine and forms hydrazone complexes with pyridoxal phosphate, producing a functional vitamin B6 deficiency that manifests as peripheral neuropathy; prophylactic pyridoxine (about 10 mg/day) prevents this, especially in diabetics, alcoholics, pregnant women, the malnourished, and slow acetylators. Isoniazid inhibits (does not induce) CYP enzymes, does not chelate calcium, and does not act on dihydrofolate reductase (that is trimethoprim/methotrexate).
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_006 [E] Rifampicin exerts its bactericidal action against Mycobacterium tuberculosis by inhibiting which bacterial enzyme?
    A. DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II)
   *B. DNA-dependent RNA polymerase
    C. Dihydrofolate reductase
    D. Arabinosyl transferase
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Rifampicin (mechanism); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics
   concept: Rifampicin binds the beta-subunit of bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase (encoded by rpoB), blocking initiation of RNA synthesis (transcription); it is bactericidal against both intracellular and extracellular organisms, and resistance results from rpoB point mutations. DNA gyrase is inhibited by fluoroquinolones, dihydrofolate reductase by trimethoprim/pyrimethamine, and arabinosyl transferase by ethambutol — none of which is rifampicin's target.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_007 [M] A woman taking a combined oral contraceptive pill is started on rifampicin-containing therapy and becomes pregnant despite reporting good compliance. What is the mechanism of this contraceptive failure?
    A. Rifampicin displaces estrogen from plasma-protein binding sites
    B. Rifampicin inhibits intestinal absorption of the contraceptive
   *C. Rifampicin induces hepatic CYP3A4, increasing metabolism of estrogen and progestin
    D. Rifampicin acts as a progesterone-receptor antagonist
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Rifampicin (enzyme induction, interactions); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Rifampicin is among the most potent inducers of hepatic and intestinal CYP3A4 (and P-glycoprotein), accelerating metabolism of estrogens and progestins and reducing contraceptive efficacy, so additional or alternative contraception must be advised. The same induction lowers levels of warfarin, HIV protease inhibitors, sulfonylureas, ciclosporin, and many other drugs. It does not act by protein displacement, absorption blockade, or receptor antagonism.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_008 [E] A patient on antitubercular therapy is alarmed by orange-red discoloration of his urine, tears, and sweat. Which drug is responsible and what is the appropriate advice?
    A. Ethambutol; stop the drug immediately
    B. Pyrazinamide; this indicates hepatotoxicity
    C. Isoniazid; this indicates ongoing hemolysis
   *D. Rifampicin; it is harmless but may permanently stain soft contact lenses
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Rifampicin (adverse effects); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Rifampicin and its metabolites impart a harmless orange-red colour to urine, sweat, tears, sputum, and saliva; patients should be reassured but warned that soft contact lenses can be permanently stained. It is not a marker of toxicity, so the drug is continued. Ethambutol, pyrazinamide, and isoniazid do not cause this discoloration, and none of the alarming interpretations in the other options is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_009 [H] Assertion (A): Rifampicin can cause a flu-like syndrome with fever, chills, and occasionally thrombocytopenia. Reason (R): These immunoallergic, antibody-mediated reactions occur more often with intermittent or high-dose rifampicin than with regular daily dosing. Select the correct option.
   *A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Rifampicin (intermittent-dose reactions); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics
   concept: Rifampicin's flu-like syndrome (fever, chills, myalgia) and the less common thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, and acute interstitial nephritis are immunoallergic reactions mediated by anti-rifampicin antibodies; these develop far more readily with intermittent, irregular, or high-dose therapy than with steady daily dosing. Both statements are therefore true and causally linked, so R correctly explains A, giving option A.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_010 [H] An HIV-positive patient on a ritonavir-boosted protease-inhibitor regimen requires treatment for tuberculosis. Which rifamycin is preferred to minimize drug interactions, and why?
    A. Rifampicin, because it is the least inducing of the rifamycins
   *B. Rifabutin, because it is a weaker inducer of CYP3A4 than rifampicin
    C. Rifapentine, because it has no effect on CYP450 enzymes
    D. Rifampicin at double dose, to overcome its own induction
   key=B ref=Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics — Rifamycins (rifabutin in HIV); KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed
   concept: Rifabutin is a weaker inducer of CYP3A4 than rifampicin, so it causes less reduction in protease-inhibitor plasma levels and is preferred (often with dose adjustment) in patients on PI-based antiretroviral therapy. Rifampicin is the MOST potent inducer of the rifamycins, not the least; rifapentine is a long-acting rifamycin that still induces CYP3A4; and simply doubling rifampicin does not resolve the interaction and risks toxicity.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_011 [M] Pyrazinamide is a prodrug that is especially effective against semi-dormant intracellular bacilli. Its activation and antibacterial activity depend on which of the following?
    A. Activation by KatG, with activity confined to alkaline pH
    B. Direct inhibition of DNA-dependent RNA polymerase
   *C. Conversion to pyrazinoic acid by pyrazinamidase, with activity at acidic pH
    D. Irreversible binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Pyrazinamide (mechanism, sterilizing action); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Pyrazinamide is converted to its active form pyrazinoic acid by mycobacterial pyrazinamidase/nicotinamidase (encoded by pncA), and is most active in the acidic environment inside macrophages and inflamed tissue, where it kills semi-dormant intracellular bacilli; this sterilizing action allowed shortening of therapy to 6 months, and resistance arises from pncA mutations. KatG activates isoniazid, RNA polymerase is rifampicin's target, and the 30S subunit is the aminoglycoside target.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_012 [H] Assertion (A): Pyrazinamide therapy increases the renal excretion of uric acid. Reason (R): The active metabolite pyrazinoic acid inhibits renal tubular secretion of urate. Select the correct option.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
   *D. A is false but R is true
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Pyrazinamide (hyperuricemia); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics
   concept: Pyrazinamide characteristically causes hyperuricemia precisely because its active metabolite pyrazinoic acid INHIBITS renal tubular secretion of urate, thereby DECREASING (not increasing) uric acid excretion — so the Assertion is false while the Reason is true, giving option D. The retained urate may be asymptomatic or precipitate gout/arthralgia; humans lack functional uricase, and pyrazinamide neither induces xanthine oxidase nor increases purine synthesis.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_013 [M] Ethambutol is a bacteriostatic antitubercular agent added mainly to prevent emergence of resistance. It acts by inhibiting which target?
   *A. Arabinosyl transferase, blocking arabinogalactan synthesis
    B. Mycolic acid synthesis
    C. Mycobacterial ATP synthase
    D. Folic acid synthesis
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Ethambutol (mechanism); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Ethambutol inhibits arabinosyl transferases (encoded by the embCAB operon; resistance via embB mutations), blocking polymerization of arabinose into the arabinogalactan of the mycobacterial cell wall; it is bacteriostatic and is combined with other drugs chiefly to retard resistance. Mycolic acid synthesis is isoniazid's target, ATP synthase is bedaquiline's, and folate synthesis is inhibited by dapsone/sulfonamides — not by ethambutol.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_014 [M] Which statement about ethambutol dosing and monitoring is correct?
    A. It is chiefly hepatically metabolized, so no dose change is needed in renal impairment
   *B. It is largely excreted unchanged by the kidney, so the dose is reduced in renal impairment, and visual acuity and red-green colour vision must be monitored
    C. It is absolutely contraindicated in all adults over 60 years of age
    D. Its optic toxicity is dose-independent and always irreversible
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Ethambutol (excretion, ocular monitoring); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Ethambutol is largely excreted unchanged by the kidney, so the dose must be reduced (or the interval extended) in renal impairment to avoid accumulation and optic neuritis. Its dose-related retrobulbar/optic neuritis is usually reversible if detected early, so baseline and periodic visual acuity and red-green colour discrimination testing are recommended, and it is avoided in young children who cannot report visual changes. It is not primarily hepatic, not contraindicated by age alone, and toxicity is dose-related, not dose-independent.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_015 [E] Streptomycin and amikacin act against mycobacteria by binding to which target, producing which effect?
    A. The 50S ribosomal subunit, halting peptide-bond formation
    B. DNA gyrase, causing DNA strand breaks
   *C. The 30S ribosomal subunit, causing misreading of mRNA
    D. DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, blocking transcription
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Streptomycin/Aminoglycosides (mechanism); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Aminoglycosides (streptomycin, amikacin, kanamycin) bind irreversibly to the 30S ribosomal subunit, causing misreading of the genetic code and inhibition of protein synthesis; they are bactericidal and act mainly on extracellular organisms. Streptomycin resistance is due to rpsL (S12 protein) or rrs (16S rRNA) mutations. Linezolid and macrolides act on the 50S subunit, fluoroquinolones inhibit DNA gyrase, and rifampicin inhibits RNA polymerase.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_016 [M] Assertion (A): Streptomycin is considered safe for use throughout pregnancy. Reason (R): Aminoglycosides cross the placenta and can damage the fetal eighth cranial nerve, causing congenital deafness. Select the correct option.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
   *D. A is false but R is true
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Streptomycin (toxicity, pregnancy); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics
   concept: Streptomycin is CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy, so the Assertion is false; it is not safe. The Reason is true — aminoglycosides cross the placenta and are ototoxic to the fetus, damaging cranial nerve VIII and causing irreversible congenital deafness. Because A is false while R is true, the answer is D. In adults, streptomycin additionally causes vestibular greater than cochlear toxicity and dose-related nephrotoxicity.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_017 [H] Bedaquiline, used in multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, has a novel mechanism of action and one notable cardiac adverse effect. Which pairing is correct?
   *A. Inhibits mycobacterial ATP synthase; QT-interval prolongation
    B. Inhibits DNA gyrase; tendon rupture
    C. Inhibits DNA-dependent RNA polymerase; hepatotoxicity
    D. Inhibits the 30S ribosomal subunit; ototoxicity
   key=A ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Antimycobacterial Drugs (bedaquiline); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics
   concept: Bedaquiline is a diarylquinoline that inhibits the mycobacterial ATP synthase (atpE), depleting cellular ATP; its principal safety concerns are QT-interval prolongation — so it is used cautiously with other QT-prolonging agents such as moxifloxacin and clofazimine, with ECG monitoring — and hepatotoxicity, and it has a very long terminal half-life. The other mechanism/adverse-effect pairings describe fluoroquinolones, rifampicin, and aminoglycosides respectively.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_018 [H] A patient on prolonged linezolid therapy for extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis develops progressive numbness of the feet and pancytopenia. Which additional caution is specifically relevant to linezolid?
    A. It is a potent inducer of CYP3A4
   *B. It is a reversible non-selective monoamine oxidase inhibitor, risking serotonin syndrome or hypertensive crisis with serotonergic drugs or tyramine-rich foods
    C. It chelates with dietary calcium, reducing absorption
    D. It causes harmless orange discoloration of the urine
   key=B ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Oxazolidinones (linezolid); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics
   concept: Linezolid (an oxazolidinone that binds the 23S rRNA of the 50S subunit and blocks formation of the initiation complex) causes dose- and duration-dependent myelosuppression (thrombocytopenia, anemia, pancytopenia) and peripheral and optic neuropathy with prolonged use; it is also a reversible non-selective MAO inhibitor, so combining it with SSRIs or tyramine-rich foods risks serotonin syndrome or hypertensive crisis. It is not a CYP inducer, does not chelate calcium (that is tetracyclines/fluoroquinolones), and does not colour urine (that is rifampicin).
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_019 [M] A second-line antitubercular drug frequently causes neuropsychiatric toxicity (seizures, psychosis, depression) and is routinely given with pyridoxine. By which mechanism does it act?
    A. It inhibits mycolic acid synthesis after KatG activation
    B. It binds the 50S ribosomal subunit
   *C. It inhibits alanine racemase and D-alanyl-D-alanine ligase, blocking cell-wall synthesis
    D. It inhibits arabinosyl transferase
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Cycloserine (mechanism, CNS toxicity); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Cycloserine is a structural analogue of D-alanine that inhibits alanine racemase and D-alanyl-D-alanine ligase, blocking peptidoglycan (cell-wall) synthesis. Its dose-limiting toxicity is neuropsychiatric — headache, tremor, psychosis, depression, suicidal ideation, and seizures — so pyridoxine is co-administered and it is avoided in patients with epilepsy, psychiatric illness, or alcoholism. The other mechanisms describe isoniazid, macrolides/linezolid, and ethambutol respectively.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_020 [M] In multidrug-resistant tuberculosis regimens a later-generation fluoroquinolone such as moxifloxacin or levofloxacin is a core drug. Which mechanism-of-action and class adverse-effect pairing is correct?
    A. Inhibits RNA polymerase; harmless red urine
    B. Inhibits ATP synthase; optic neuritis
    C. Inhibits folic acid synthesis; oxidative hemolysis
   *D. Inhibits DNA gyrase/topoisomerase; tendinopathy and QT-interval prolongation
   key=D ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Fluoroquinolones and Antimycobacterial Drugs; KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed
   concept: Fluoroquinolones inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV and are core second-line/MDR-TB agents; recognized class adverse effects include tendinitis and tendon rupture, QT-interval prolongation (moxifloxacin greater than levofloxacin), dysglycemia, and CNS effects. Moxifloxacin's QT effect is especially important when combined with bedaquiline or clofazimine. The distractor mechanisms describe rifampicin, bedaquiline, and dapsone/sulfonamides respectively.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_021 [M] Dapsone, a mainstay of multidrug therapy for leprosy, is bacteriostatic against Mycobacterium leprae because it inhibits which target?
   *A. Dihydropteroate synthase, blocking folic acid synthesis
    B. DNA-dependent RNA polymerase
    C. Arabinosyl transferase
    D. Mycolic acid synthesis
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antileprotic Drugs (Dapsone); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Dapsone is a sulfone that inhibits dihydropteroate synthase, blocking folic acid synthesis in M. leprae — the same folate pathway targeted by sulfonamides — and is bacteriostatic; it is combined with rifampicin (bactericidal, via RNA polymerase) and clofazimine in WHO multidrug therapy. RNA polymerase inhibition is rifampicin's mechanism, arabinosyl transferase is ethambutol's, and mycolic acid synthesis is isoniazid's — none is dapsone's target.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_022 [H] A patient started on dapsone for leprosy develops fatigue, dark urine, and a falling hemoglobin, and is subsequently found to be G6PD-deficient. Which pair of adverse effects best characterizes dapsone?
    A. Aplastic anemia and agranulocytosis only
   *B. Dose-related hemolysis and methemoglobinemia
    C. Megaloblastic anemia due to vitamin B12 deficiency
    D. Iron-deficiency anemia from chronic blood loss
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Dapsone (adverse effects, G6PD); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics
   concept: Dapsone characteristically causes dose-related hemolytic anemia — severe in G6PD deficiency — and methemoglobinemia, both driven by its oxidant hydroxylamine metabolite, so screening for G6PD deficiency is advised before therapy. It can also cause the 'dapsone syndrome' (fever, rash, hepatitis, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia). It does not cause megaloblastic or iron-deficiency anemia, and agranulocytosis, although reported, is uncommon and is not the oxidant mechanism producing this presentation.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_023 [E] A patient on multidrug therapy for lepromatous leprosy notices progressive reddish-brown to black discoloration of the skin. Which drug is responsible?
    A. Dapsone
    B. Rifampicin
   *C. Clofazimine
    D. Ethionamide
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antileprotic Drugs (Clofazimine); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology
   concept: Clofazimine, a riminophenazine dye that binds mycobacterial DNA and also has an anti-inflammatory action useful in erythema nodosum leprosum, causes dose-dependent reddish-brown to black discoloration of the skin, along with conjunctival, urinary, and secretion pigmentation, plus dryness/ichthyosis and gastrointestinal disturbance. Rifampicin colours secretions orange-red (not the skin black), dapsone causes hemolysis, and ethionamide causes gastrointestinal upset — none produces this characteristic pigmentation.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CHEMO_TB_024 [H] A second-line antitubercular drug that is structurally related to isoniazid causes marked gastrointestinal intolerance and, with prolonged use, hypothyroidism with goitre as well as hepatotoxicity. Which drug is this?
    A. Para-aminosalicylic acid (PAS)
    B. Capreomycin
    C. Cycloserine
   *D. Ethionamide
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Ethionamide (structure, adverse effects); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics
   concept: Ethionamide is a thioamide structurally related to isoniazid; like isoniazid it inhibits mycolic acid synthesis (InhA) after activation by EthA. Its major adverse effects are severe gastrointestinal intolerance (nausea, vomiting, metallic taste), hepatotoxicity, and, on prolonged use, hypothyroidism with goitre (it inhibits thyroid hormone synthesis) plus peripheral neuropathy (pyridoxine is given). PAS also causes GI upset and hypothyroidism but is not an isoniazid structural analogue; capreomycin is an ototoxic/nephrotoxic polypeptide; cycloserine causes neuropsychiatric toxicity.
Q_c1_046 [M] A tuberculosis patient reports decreasing visual acuity and difficulty distinguishing red from green. Which drug is responsible and requires monthly visual monitoring?
    A. Isoniazid
    B. Rifampicin
   *C. Ethambutol
    D. Streptomycin

=== Pharmacology / Antibiotics: classes, MOA, resistance, DoC
Q_c1_042 [E] A patient on gentamicin for gram-negative sepsis develops a rising serum creatinine and new hearing loss. These two dose-related toxicities are characteristic of which antibiotic class?
   *A. Aminoglycosides
    B. Macrolides
    C. Penicillins
    D. Tetracyclines
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi 8e, Ch. Aminoglycoside Antibiotics
   concept: Aminoglycosides characteristically cause nephrotoxicity (usually reversible) and ototoxicity (often irreversible), plus neuromuscular blockade. They are concentration-dependent bactericidals, favoring once-daily dosing with monitoring.
Q_c1_044 [M] A hospitalized patient grows methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) in blood cultures. The drug of choice is vancomycin, which acts by?
    A. Inhibiting DNA gyrase
   *B. Binding the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus to block peptidoglycan cell-wall synthesis
    C. Inhibiting the 30S ribosomal subunit
    D. Blocking folate synthesis
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi 8e, Ch. Antibacterial Drugs
   concept: Vancomycin (a glycopeptide) binds the terminal D-Ala-D-Ala of peptidoglycan precursors, inhibiting cell-wall synthesis; it is the drug of choice for serious MRSA infection. Rapid infusion releases histamine causing 'red-man' syndrome.

=== Pharmacology / Antiepileptics
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_AED_001 [E] Phenytoin and carbamazepine control focal and generalized tonic-clonic seizures largely through a common molecular action. Which mechanism do they principally share?
    A. Enhancement of GABA-A receptor chloride currents
   *B. Use-dependent (frequency-dependent) blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels, prolonging their inactivated state
    C. Blockade of T-type calcium channels in thalamic relay neurons
    D. Competitive antagonism of the NMDA glutamate receptor
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antiepileptic Drugs (mechanisms)
   concept: Phenytoin and carbamazepine stabilise the inactivated conformation of voltage-gated Na channels in a use-dependent manner, selectively suppressing high-frequency repetitive firing while sparing normal transmission, which controls focal and tonic-clonic seizures. Option A describes benzodiazepines/barbiturates, option C describes ethosuximide acting on thalamic T-type Ca channels underlying absence seizures, and option D describes drugs such as felbamate/perampanel-type glutamate modulation, none of which is the shared primary action here.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_AED_002 [E] Levetiracetam is a broad-spectrum antiepileptic whose novel target is distinct from that of the older sodium- and calcium-channel drugs. Its principal molecular target is:
   *A. The synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A), modulating vesicular neurotransmitter release
    B. Irreversible inhibition of GABA transaminase
    C. The alpha2-delta subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels
    D. Prolongation of GABA-A chloride channel opening duration
   key=A ref=Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics, 13th ed — Pharmacotherapy of the Epilepsies
   concept: Levetiracetam binds the synaptic vesicle protein SV2A, altering calcium-dependent vesicular neurotransmitter release and dampening abnormal synchronised discharge; this unique mechanism explains its broad spectrum and relative lack of enzyme interactions. This coefficient controls SPEED, not potency (that is the oil:gas coefficient / MAC). Highly soluble agents like methoxyflurane and ether give slow induction. Metabolism and muscle relaxation are unrelated to blood solubility, so only B is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_004 [H] During induction, delivering a high concentration of nitrous oxide together with a volatile agent such as sevoflurane accelerates the rise of the volatile agent's alveolar concentration. This phenomenon is termed the:
    A. Concentration effect
   *B. Second gas effect
    C. Diffusion hypoxia
    D. Bohr effect
   key=B ref=Morgan & Mikhail's Clinical Anesthesiology, 6th ed — Inhalation Anesthetics (pharmacokinetics)
   concept: The second gas effect: rapid, large-volume uptake of the high-concentration 'first gas' (nitrous oxide) into pulmonary blood shrinks alveolar gas volume, which concentrates the remaining 'second gas' (the volatile agent) and augments its inspiratory inflow — speeding its alveolar rise. It is a consequence of the concentration effect (which describes only the first gas itself). Diffusion hypoxia is the reverse process at emergence, and the Bohr effect concerns haemoglobin–oxygen affinity, so B is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_005 [M] At the end of a prolonged nitrous oxide anaesthetic, the patient is at risk of diffusion hypoxia. The most appropriate measure to prevent this is to:
    A. Continue nitrous oxide at a low flow rate
    B. Extubate immediately and leave the patient breathing room air
   *C. Administer 100% oxygen for the first few minutes after discontinuing nitrous oxide
    D. Hyperventilate the patient with added carbon dioxide
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — General Anaesthetics (Nitrous oxide)
   concept: When nitrous oxide is stopped, its large stored volume rapidly diffuses from blood back into the alveoli, diluting alveolar oxygen and CO2 and producing transient dilutional (diffusion) hypoxia. Delivering 100% oxygen for 3–5 minutes offsets this dilution and maintains arterial oxygenation. Continuing N2O or leaving the patient in room air worsens hypoxia, and adding CO2 is not indicated. Hence C is the correct preventive step; this risk is greatest after high-concentration, prolonged N2O use.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_006 [H] Prolonged or repeated exposure to nitrous oxide can cause megaloblastic anaemia and myeloneuropathy. This results from irreversible oxidation of the cobalt atom of vitamin B12, which inactivates:
   *A. Methionine synthase
    B. Thymidylate synthase
    C. Dihydrofolate reductase
    D. Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase
   key=A ref=Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics, 13th ed — General Anesthetics (Nitrous oxide toxicity)
   concept: Nitrous oxide oxidises the cobalt of cobalamin (Co+ to Co3+), inactivating methionine synthase, the B12-dependent enzyme that converts homocysteine to methionine and regenerates tetrahydrofolate from methyl-THF. The resulting 'folate trap' impairs DNA synthesis, causing megaloblastic bone-marrow changes, and chronic exposure produces subacute combined degeneration of the cord. Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase is the other B12 enzyme but is not the mediator here; DHFR and thymidylate synthase are folate enzymes not directly oxidised by N2O — so A is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_007 [M] Assertion (A): The dose of adrenaline infiltrated into the surgical field should be strictly limited during halothane anaesthesia. Reason (R): Halothane sensitises the myocardium to the arrhythmogenic effects of catecholamines. Select the correct option.
   *A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — General Anaesthetics (Halothane, adverse effects)
   concept: Halothane (more than other halogenated agents) sensitises the myocardium to catecholamines, so exogenous adrenaline can precipitate multifocal ventricular ectopics or ventricular tachycardia — this is exactly why the infiltrated adrenaline dose is capped during halothane. Both statements are true and the Reason directly and correctly explains the Assertion, so the answer is A. Halothane itself is a myocardial depressant, not a beta-agonist. Isoflurane and sevoflurane sensitise far less and are preferred when adrenaline is required.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_008 [H] Thiopentone (thiopental) is ABSOLUTELY contraindicated as an induction agent in a patient with which of the following conditions?
   *A. Acute intermittent porphyria
    B. Well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus
    C. Mild iron-deficiency anaemia
    D. Primary osteoarthritis of the knee
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — General Anaesthetics (Thiopentone sodium)
   concept: Barbiturates induce hepatic delta-aminolaevulinic acid (ALA) synthase, boosting porphyrin-precursor synthesis and precipitating an acute, potentially fatal attack of acute intermittent porphyria (and variegate porphyria); thiopentone is therefore absolutely contraindicated, and propofol is the preferred alternative. This hazard is distinct from thiopentone's ultrashort action (due to redistribution) and its tendency to trigger laryngospasm/bronchospasm. Diabetes, anaemia and osteoarthritis are not contraindications, so A is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_009 [M] Which of the following is a characteristic property of propofol that distinguishes it from thiopentone?
    A. It precipitates acute intermittent porphyria
    B. It has a long elimination half-life with marked cumulation
    C. It provides profound intra-operative analgesia
   *D. It possesses intrinsic anti-emetic activity
   key=D ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — General Anesthetics (Propofol)
   concept: Propofol has intrinsic anti-emetic action (useful against postoperative nausea and vomiting) and gives a smooth, rapid, clear-headed recovery, making it the induction agent of choice for day-care surgery and for maintenance by infusion (TIVA). It does NOT precipitate porphyria (indeed it is the safe substitute for thiopentone), is rapidly cleared without cumulation, and, like most IV anaesthetics, has essentially no analgesic effect. Its drawbacks are injection pain, dose-dependent hypotension/apnoea, egg-soy caution and propofol infusion syndrome — so D is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_010 [H] Propofol infusion syndrome, associated with prolonged high-dose propofol infusion (particularly in critically ill or paediatric patients), is characterised by:
   *A. Metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia, hyperlipidaemia and cardiac failure
    B. Hypernatraemia with central pontine myelinolysis
    C. Cholestatic jaundice with intense pruritus
    D. Aplastic anaemia and agranulocytosis
   key=A ref=Morgan & Mikhail's Clinical Anesthesiology, 6th ed — Intravenous Anesthetics (Propofol)
   concept: Propofol infusion syndrome (PRIS) occurs with high-dose (>4 mg/kg/h), prolonged infusions and reflects impaired mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation, producing severe metabolic (lactic) acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia, hyperlipidaemia, hepatomegaly and refractory bradycardia/cardiac failure that can be fatal. Treatment is to stop propofol immediately and give supportive/haemodynamic care. The other options describe unrelated toxicities not caused by propofol, so A is the correct constellation.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_011 [M] Ketamine is generally avoided in a patient with raised intracranial pressure or an open-globe (penetrating) eye injury because it:
    A. Causes profound systemic hypotension
   *B. Increases cerebral blood flow, intracranial pressure and intra-ocular pressure
    C. Produces marked, early respiratory depression
    D. Reduces cerebral metabolic rate and cerebral blood flow
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — General Anaesthetics (Ketamine)
   concept: Ketamine, a dissociative NMDA-receptor antagonist, is sympathomimetic — it raises blood pressure, heart rate, cerebral blood flow, ICP and intra-ocular pressure; hence caution in raised ICP and open-globe injuries. Because it supports blood pressure and relatively preserves airway reflexes and respiration, it is valuable in hypovolaemic shock and in asthmatics (bronchodilator). Its principal drawbacks are emergence delirium/hallucinations (reduced by benzodiazepines) and hypersalivation. Only B correctly states the ICP/IOP-raising effect.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_012 [E] Ketamine produces 'dissociative anaesthesia' primarily through antagonism at which receptor?
    A. GABA-A receptor
    B. Mu (μ) opioid receptor
   *C. NMDA subtype of glutamate receptor
    D. Alpha-2 adrenergic receptor
   key=C ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — General Anesthetics (Ketamine)
   concept: Ketamine is a non-competitive antagonist at the NMDA subtype of the glutamate receptor, producing a cataleptic 'dissociative' state — profound analgesia and amnesia with open eyes, nystagmus and preserved reflexes. GABA-A potentiation is the mechanism of thiopentone, propofol and benzodiazepines; mu-opioid agonism underlies morphine/fentanyl; alpha-2 agonism is the mechanism of dexmedetomidine and clonidine. Therefore NMDA-receptor blockade (C) is the correct answer for ketamine.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_013 [H] Etomidate is valued for its cardiovascular stability at induction, but even a single bolus can cause which of the following adverse effects?
    A. Malignant hyperthermia
    B. Prolonged skeletal muscle paralysis
    C. Massive histamine release with bronchospasm
   *D. Adrenocortical suppression (inhibition of steroidogenesis)
   key=D ref=Morgan & Mikhail's Clinical Anesthesiology, 6th ed — Intravenous Anesthetics (Etomidate)
   concept: Etomidate reversibly inhibits 11-beta-hydroxylase, suppressing cortisol and aldosterone synthesis; even a single induction dose blunts the adrenal stress response, and continuous infusion increased mortality in ICU patients — so it is used cautiously in sepsis. Its advantage is haemodynamic stability with minimal histamine release, making it useful in cardiac and shocked patients; other drawbacks are injection pain, myoclonus and postoperative nausea/vomiting. It is not a malignant-hyperthermia trigger and does not cause paralysis, so D is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_014 [M] A young, previously healthy patient given suxamethonium (succinylcholine) for a brief procedure fails to resume spontaneous breathing and remains paralysed for over an hour. The most likely cause is:
    A. Malignant hyperthermia
    B. An excessive dose of neostigmine
   *C. Atypical or deficient plasma pseudocholinesterase
    D. Acute hyperkalaemia
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Skeletal Muscle Relaxants (Succinylcholine)
   concept: Suxamethonium is normally hydrolysed within minutes by plasma pseudocholinesterase (butyrylcholinesterase). In patients with a genetically atypical or quantitatively deficient enzyme, hydrolysis is markedly slowed, producing prolonged apnoea ('scoline apnoea'), managed simply by continued mechanical ventilation until recovery. The dibucaine number identifies the atypical enzyme. Neostigmine would worsen (not cause) this by inhibiting cholinesterase. This scenario deliberately excludes the separate hyperkalaemia and malignant-hyperthermia hazards, so C is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_015 [H] Assertion (A): Atracurium is a suitable neuromuscular blocker in a patient with combined severe hepatic and renal failure. Reason (R): Atracurium is eliminated by Hofmann degradation, a process independent of hepatic and renal function. Select the correct option.
   *A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Skeletal Muscle Relaxants (non-depolarizing blockers)
   concept: Atracurium (and cisatracurium) undergo Hofmann elimination — spontaneous, non-enzymatic breakdown at physiological pH and temperature — plus ester hydrolysis, so clearance does not depend on the liver or kidney, making them ideal in organ failure. Both statements are true and the Reason correctly explains the Assertion, so the answer is A. A degradation product, laudanosine, can rarely cause CNS excitation. By contrast, vecuronium is largely biliary, pancuronium mainly renal and rocuronium chiefly hepatic, all accumulating in organ failure.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_016 [M] At the end of surgery, residual non-depolarizing neuromuscular block is reversed with neostigmine. Neostigmine is deliberately co-administered with glycopyrrolate (or atropine) in order to:
    A. Potentiate the neuromuscular blockade
   *B. Prevent the muscarinic effects of neostigmine such as bradycardia and excess secretions
    C. Prolong the action of the muscle relaxant
    D. Reverse the central sedative effects of the general anaesthetic
   key=B ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — Skeletal Muscle Relaxants (reversal of blockade)
   concept: Neostigmine inhibits acetylcholinesterase, raising synaptic acetylcholine to out-compete the non-depolarizing (competitive) blocker at the nicotinic neuromuscular junction. However, the extra acetylcholine also floods muscarinic receptors, causing bradycardia, bronchospasm, salivation and gut hypermotility. An antimuscarinic — glycopyrrolate (preferred, as it does not cross the blood–brain barrier) or atropine — is given alongside to block these unwanted muscarinic effects while leaving the nicotinic reversal intact. It neither potentiates nor prolongs the block, so B is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_017 [M] Sugammadex reverses neuromuscular blockade produced by rocuronium (and vecuronium) by which mechanism?
    A. Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase at the neuromuscular junction
    B. Blocking presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels
    C. Depolarizing the motor end-plate
   *D. Encapsulating (chelating) the aminosteroid relaxant molecule in plasma
   key=D ref=Morgan & Mikhail's Clinical Anesthesiology, 6th ed — Neuromuscular Blocking Agents (Sugammadex)
   concept: Sugammadex is a modified gamma-cyclodextrin that directly encapsulates aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers (rocuronium greater than vecuronium) in the plasma, forming an inactive complex; this creates a concentration gradient that draws the drug away from the neuromuscular junction, giving rapid, complete reversal even of profound block, without the muscarinic side-effects of neostigmine. It does NOT act on acetylcholinesterase and is ineffective against benzylisoquinolinium agents such as atracurium — so D is the correct mechanism.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_018 [E] The analgesic action and most of the clinically important effects of morphine are mediated predominantly through agonism at which opioid receptor?
   *A. Mu (μ) receptor
    B. Kappa (κ) receptor
    C. Delta (δ) receptor
    D. Nociceptin (ORL-1) receptor
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Opioid Analgesics and Antagonists
   concept: Morphine is a relatively selective agonist at the mu (μ)-opioid receptor; μ activation mediates supraspinal and spinal analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, miosis, constipation and physical dependence. Kappa contributes spinal analgesia with dysphoria and less respiratory depression, delta modulates analgesia and mood, and the ORL-1/nociceptin receptor is a related but distinct system. All opioid receptors are Gi/Go-coupled, reducing cAMP, closing calcium and opening potassium channels to depress neuronal excitability — so A is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_019 [H] Assertion (A): Morphine should be used cautiously in a patient with chronic kidney disease. Reason (R): Morphine-3-glucuronide is a potent mu-opioid agonist that accumulates in renal failure and causes prolonged respiratory depression. Select the correct option.
    A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
   *C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=C ref=Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics, 13th ed — Opioids (morphine metabolism)
   concept: Morphine is indeed hazardous in renal failure — the Assertion is TRUE — but the Reason is FALSE because it names the wrong metabolite: morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G) is analgesically inactive (and may be neuroexcitatory), whereas it is morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G), a potent renally-excreted mu-agonist, that accumulates and causes prolonged sedation and respiratory depression. Since A is true but R is false, the answer is C; fentanyl is often preferred in significant renal impairment.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_020 [H] Which of the following statements regarding pethidine (meperidine) is TRUE?
    A. Its metabolite morphine-6-glucuronide is responsible for its analgesia
   *B. Its metabolite norpethidine can accumulate to cause CNS excitation and seizures, and it is contraindicated with MAO inhibitors
    C. It characteristically produces marked miosis and bradycardia identical to morphine
    D. It is a pure opioid antagonist used to treat opioid overdose
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Opioid Analgesics (Pethidine)
   concept: Pethidine is metabolised to norpethidine (normeperidine), a neurotoxic metabolite that accumulates — especially in renal impairment or with repeated dosing — causing tremor, myoclonus and seizures that are NOT reversed by naloxone. Combined with a monoamine-oxidase inhibitor it can trigger a life-threatening excitatory/serotonergic reaction (hyperthermia, rigidity, coma). Unlike morphine, pethidine has antimuscarinic action, so it causes less miosis (even mydriasis) and can produce tachycardia. It is an agonist, not an antagonist, making B the only true statement.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_021 [M] Codeine is a relatively weak analgesic by itself; its analgesic effect depends largely on hepatic O-demethylation to morphine, catalysed by which enzyme?
    A. CYP3A4
    B. Plasma pseudocholinesterase
    C. CYP1A2
   *D. CYP2D6
   key=D ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — Opioid Analgesics & Antagonists (codeine, CYP2D6)
   concept: Codeine is essentially a prodrug that is O-demethylated to morphine by CYP2D6. Poor metabolizers (deficient CYP2D6) obtain little analgesia, whereas ultra-rapid metabolizers generate excess morphine and risk toxicity and respiratory depression — a recognised danger in young children and in breast-fed infants of ultra-rapid-metabolizer mothers. CYP2D6 inhibitors such as fluoxetine and quinidine blunt codeine's effect. CYP3A4 forms inactive norcodeine, while pseudocholinesterase and CYP1A2 are not the key pathway, so D is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_022 [M] A patient maintained on high-dose full-agonist opioid therapy is given buprenorphine and unexpectedly develops acute withdrawal symptoms. This is best explained by the fact that buprenorphine is a:
    A. Pure mu-opioid antagonist like naloxone
    B. Selective kappa-receptor agonist
    C. Non-opioid NMDA-receptor antagonist
   *D. Partial mu-opioid agonist with very high receptor affinity
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Opioid Analgesics (Buprenorphine)
   concept: Buprenorphine is a partial agonist at the mu-receptor — very high binding affinity but low intrinsic activity — and a kappa antagonist. Given to a patient on a full agonist, its high affinity displaces that full agonist while delivering only submaximal mu activation, so the net opioid effect falls and withdrawal is precipitated. Its partial agonism also produces a ceiling on respiratory depression, and its tight binding makes overdose relatively resistant to naloxone. It is neither a pure antagonist nor an NMDA blocker, so D is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_023 [M] Tramadol produces analgesia by a dual mechanism. In addition to weak agonism at the mu-opioid receptor, it also:
    A. Blocks NMDA glutamate receptors
    B. Irreversibly inhibits cyclo-oxygenase
   *C. Inhibits neuronal reuptake of serotonin and noradrenaline
    D. Antagonises GABA-A receptors
   key=C ref=Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics, 13th ed — Opioids (Tramadol)
   concept: Tramadol is a weak mu-agonist (its M1 metabolite, formed by CYP2D6, is the more active opioid moiety) that additionally inhibits neuronal reuptake of serotonin and noradrenaline, enhancing descending inhibitory pain pathways. This monoaminergic action explains why it lowers seizure threshold and, particularly when combined with SSRIs, SNRIs or MAOIs, can precipitate serotonin syndrome. It causes comparatively less respiratory depression and constipation than classical opioids. It does not act via COX, NMDA or GABA-A, so C is correct.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CNS_ANES_024 [E] Regarding the mechanism of action of local anaesthetics, which statement is correct?
    A. They open voltage-gated potassium channels to hyperpolarise the axon
    B. They activate GABA-A receptors on the peripheral nerve
    C. They block calcium channels at the motor end-plate
   *D. They block voltage-gated sodium channels, binding chiefly from the axoplasmic (inner) side of the membrane
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Local Anaesthetics (mechanism of action)
   concept: Local anaesthetics are weak bases: the unionized lipid-soluble form penetrates the axonal membrane, then the ionized cationic form binds the inner (intracellular) gate of the voltage-gated sodium channel, preferentially in its open/inactivated state — giving use- (frequency-) dependent block that prevents the action-potential upstroke. This explains why inflamed acidic tissue (more ionized, less penetration) reduces efficacy, and why small, rapidly firing fibres are blocked first. They do not act on potassium channels, GABA-A or end-plate calcium channels, so D is correct.

=== Pharmacology / Heart failure, antiarrhythmics, antianginals
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_001 [E] A 58-year-old man with HFrEF started on enalapril develops a persistent dry, tickling cough that resolves within days of stopping the drug. The most likely mechanism is:
   *A. Accumulation of bradykinin and substance P in the airways
    B. Direct chemical irritation of the bronchial epithelium
    C. A rise in circulating angiotensin II acting on cough receptors
    D. Mast-cell histamine release triggering bronchospasm
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Drugs Affecting Renin-Angiotensin System / Drugs for CHF
   concept: ACE (kininase II) normally degrades bradykinin, so ACE inhibition raises bradykinin and substance P, which sensitise airway sensory nerves and produce the classic non-productive cough (and, rarely, angioedema). It is a class effect of all ACE inhibitors and reverses on stopping. Angiotensin II actually falls, not rises, so C is wrong; the cough is not from direct irritation (B) or histamine/allergy (D). Switching to an ARB, which does not affect bradykinin, avoids the cough.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_002 [M] In the angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) sacubitril/valsartan, the sacubitril component acts by:
    A. Blocking the angiotensin II AT1 receptor
    B. Inhibiting angiotensin-converting enzyme
   *C. Inhibiting neprilysin, thereby raising natriuretic peptide levels
    D. Directly stimulating soluble guanylate cyclase
   key=C ref=Katzung, Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — Drugs Used in Heart Failure
   concept: Sacubitril is a prodrug whose active metabolite inhibits neprilysin, the enzyme that degrades natriuretic peptides (ANP, BNP, CNP); higher natriuretic peptide levels promote natriuresis, vasodilation and reduced sympathetic/RAAS activity. The AT1 blockade is provided by valsartan, so A describes the partner drug, not sacubitril. It is not an ACE inhibitor (B), and it does not directly stimulate guanylate cyclase (D). PARADIGM-HF showed ARNI reduced cardiovascular death versus enalapril; ARNI must not be combined with an ACE inhibitor (angioedema risk).
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_003 [M] Ivabradine, used in chronic HFrEF with sinus rhythm and an elevated heart rate, reduces heart rate by:
    A. Blocking L-type calcium channels in the AV node
   *B. Selectively inhibiting the funny (If) current in the SA node
    C. Antagonising beta-1 adrenergic receptors
    D. Inhibiting the cardiac Na+/K+ ATPase
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Drugs for CHF / Antianginal Drugs
   concept: Ivabradine selectively blocks the hyperpolarisation-activated funny (If) current carried by HCN channels in the sinoatrial node, slowing the slope of spontaneous diastolic depolarisation and hence the rate of pacemaker firing. It is a pure heart-rate-lowering drug with no effect on contractility, blood pressure or AV conduction, which distinguishes it from beta-blockers (C) and non-dihydropyridine calcium blockers (A). It requires sinus rhythm to work and is added when heart rate stays >=70/min despite maximal beta-blockade. It does not touch the Na+/K+ ATPase (D, the digoxin target).
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_004 [M] Which class of oral antidiabetic drugs has been shown to reduce cardiovascular death and heart-failure hospitalisation in HFrEF, even in patients without diabetes?
    A. Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors
    B. DPP-4 inhibitors
    C. Sulfonylureas
   *D. SGLT2 inhibitors
   key=D ref=Katzung, Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — Drugs Used in Heart Failure
   concept: SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) reduced the composite of cardiovascular death and worsening heart failure in trials such as DAPA-HF and EMPEROR-Reduced, benefits seen regardless of diabetic status; proposed mechanisms include osmotic diuresis/natriuresis, reduced preload and afterload, and favourable cardiac metabolic/energetic effects. They are now a pillar of guideline-directed HFrEF therapy. DPP-4 inhibitors are neutral (saxagliptin even increased HF hospitalisation), and sulfonylureas and alpha-glucosidase inhibitors have no HF mortality benefit, so A, B and C are wrong.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_005 [E] Which beta-blocker has proven mortality benefit in HFrEF and is one of the agents recommended for chronic heart failure?
   *A. Carvedilol
    B. Atenolol
    C. Propranolol
    D. Esmolol
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Drugs for CHF
   concept: Only three beta-blockers have documented mortality benefit in HFrEF: carvedilol, bisoprolol and metoprolol succinate (extended release); this is a drug-specific, not a class-wide, effect and they must be started at low dose and up-titrated in stable patients. Carvedilol additionally blocks alpha-1 receptors, giving vasodilation. Atenolol and propranolol were not established in the landmark HF trials, and esmolol is an ultra-short-acting IV agent used for acute rate control, not chronic HF. Beta-blockers are avoided during acute decompensation.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_006 [M] A man with HFrEF on spironolactone develops painful gynecomastia. The best mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist to switch him to, with fewer antiandrogen effects, is:
    A. Amiloride
   *B. Eplerenone
    C. Triamterene
    D. Furosemide
   key=B ref=Katzung, Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — Diuretic Agents / Drugs Used in Heart Failure
   concept: Eplerenone is a selective aldosterone (mineralocorticoid) receptor antagonist that, unlike spironolactone, has minimal affinity for androgen and progesterone receptors, so it causes far less gynecomastia, breast tenderness and menstrual irregularity while retaining the mortality benefit of MRAs in HFrEF. Amiloride and triamterene are potassium-sparing diuretics that block the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC), not aldosterone receptors, so they are not MRAs. Furosemide is a loop diuretic. All MRAs and K+-sparing agents require monitoring for hyperkalemia.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_007 [H] A patient with HFrEF cannot tolerate ACE inhibitors or ARBs because of renal dysfunction with hyperkalemia. Which drug combination has additionally been shown to improve survival, particularly in self-identified Black patients?
    A. Amlodipine plus atenolol
    B. Digoxin plus furosemide
   *C. Hydralazine plus isosorbide dinitrate
    D. Verapamil plus nitroglycerin
   key=C ref=Katzung, Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — Drugs Used in Heart Failure
   concept: The combination of hydralazine (an arteriolar vasodilator reducing afterload) with isosorbide dinitrate (a venodilator/nitric-oxide donor reducing preload) improved survival in HFrEF and showed particular benefit in self-identified Black patients (A-HeFT trial). It is the standard alternative when ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNI are contraindicated by renal impairment or hyperkalemia because it does not raise potassium. Digoxin plus furosemide (B) relieves symptoms but does not extend survival; amlodipine/atenolol (A) and verapamil (D, a negative inotrope contraindicated in HFrEF) do not.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_008 [M] Milrinone, used as an inotrope in acute decompensated heart failure, increases myocardial contractility primarily by:
    A. Stimulating beta-1 adrenergic receptors
    B. Inhibiting the Na+/K+ ATPase
    C. Blocking phosphodiesterase-5
   *D. Inhibiting phosphodiesterase-3, raising intracellular cAMP
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Drugs for CHF
   concept: Milrinone is a selective phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor; by blocking the breakdown of cAMP in cardiac and vascular smooth muscle it increases intracellular cAMP and calcium in myocytes (positive inotropy) while causing vasodilation (inodilator effect, lowering preload and afterload). Because it acts downstream of the beta receptor, it works even when beta receptors are down-regulated or the patient is on a beta-blocker, unlike dobutamine (A). It does not inhibit Na+/K+ ATPase (B, the digoxin target) or PDE-5 (C, the sildenafil target). It can cause arrhythmias and hypotension.
Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_009 [E] Dobutamine used in cardiogenic shock acts predominantly as a(n):

    C. Beta-2 selective agonist
    D. Dopamine D1 receptor agonist

   concept: Dobutamine is a synthetic catecholamine that predominantly stimulates beta-1 adrenergic receptors, producing a positive inotropic effect with relatively modest increases in heart rate, making it useful for short-term inotropic support in acute decompensated heart failure and cardiogenic shock. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_010 [M] Which statement about digoxin is CORRECT?
    A. It has a wide therapeutic index, so plasma monitoring is unnecessary

    D. It characteristically shortens the PR interval on the ECG
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Drugs for CHF (Cardiac Glycosides)
   concept: Severe, life-threatening digoxin toxicity (refractory arrhythmias, marked hyperkalemia) is treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments (digoxin immune Fab, e.g. Digibind/DigiFab), which bind and neutralise the drug. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_011 [H] Which drug used in HFrEF provides symptomatic relief but has NOT been shown to reduce mortality?
    A. Bisoprolol
    B. Sacubitril/valsartan
   *C. Furosemide
    D. Spironolactone
   key=C ref=Katzung, Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, 15th ed — Drugs Used in Heart Failure
   concept: Loop diuretics such as furosemide relieve congestion (dyspnea, edema) rapidly and are essential for symptom control, but no trial has shown they reduce mortality in HFrEF; they treat symptoms, not disease progression. The drugs with proven mortality benefit are the neurohormonal blockers: ACE inhibitors/ARBs, ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan), evidence-based beta-blockers (bisoprolol), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone) and SGLT2 inhibitors. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_012 [M] A patient develops sustained ventricular tachycardia soon after an acute myocardial infarction. Which intravenous class Ib antiarrhythmic preferentially blocks sodium channels in depolarised, ischaemic ventricular tissue?

    C. Adenosine
   *D. Lidocaine

   concept: Lidocaine is a class Ib agent that binds sodium channels with fast on-off kinetics and shows use-dependence, so it preferentially blocks channels in rapidly firing or depolarised/ischaemic tissue while sparing normal myocardium; this makes it effective for ventricular arrhythmias, especially post-MI, with little effect on atrial tissue. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_014 [E] A young patient presents with paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (AV nodal reentry) unresponsive to vagal manoeuvres. The drug of choice for acute termination is:

    C. Digoxin
    D. Quinidine

   concept: Adenosine is the drug of choice for acute termination of AV-nodal reentrant supraventricular tachycardia. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_015 [M] A patient on a QT-prolonging drug develops polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (torsades de pointes). The drug of choice, given even when serum magnesium is normal, is:

   *C. Intravenous magnesium sulfate
    D. Procainamide
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antiarrhythmic Drugs
   concept: Intravenous magnesium sulfate is the drug of choice for torsades de pointes and works to suppress the early afterdepolarisations that trigger it even when the serum magnesium level is normal. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_016 [H] Which antiarrhythmic possesses BOTH class III (potassium-channel-blocking) and class II (beta-blocking) actions and is notably associated with QT prolongation and torsades de pointes?

   concept: Sotalol is a non-selective beta-blocker (class II) that also blocks the rapid delayed-rectifier potassium current IKr (class III), prolonging repolarisation and the QT interval; this dual action underlies both its efficacy in atrial and ventricular arrhythmias and its dose-dependent risk of torsades de pointes, especially with hypokalemia or renal impairment (it is renally cleared). Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_017 [M] A patient taking quinidine for an arrhythmia develops tinnitus, headache, blurred vision, and diarrhoea. This constellation of adverse effects is known as:

Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_018 [M] Long-term therapy with procainamide is most characteristically associated with which adverse effect?

Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_019 [M] Assertion (A): Verapamil should not be combined with a beta-blocker in most patients. Reason (R): Verapamil characteristically produces marked reflex tachycardia that beta-blockers are unable to control.

   concept: The assertion is true: verapamil (a non-dihydropyridine calcium blocker) depresses SA/AV nodal conduction and myocardial contractility, so combining it with a beta-blocker risks severe bradycardia, high-grade AV block, asystole and heart failure. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_020 [H] A patient with recent-onset atrial flutter needs rapid pharmacological cardioversion. Which intravenous class III agent is used specifically for this, carrying a risk of torsades de pointes?

Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_021 [H] Amiodarone is added to a patient who is stable on a maintenance dose of digoxin. What is the expected interaction and appropriate response?

Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_022 [E] The principal anti-anginal action of organic nitrates at usual therapeutic doses is:

    D. Blockade of cardiac beta-1 adrenergic receptors
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi, Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antianginal Drugs
   concept: Organic nitrates are converted to nitric oxide, which activates guanylate cyclase and raises cGMP, relaxing vascular smooth muscle. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_024 [H] Ranolazine relieves chronic stable angina mainly by:

   concept: Ranolazine is a novel anti-anginal that inhibits the late inward sodium current in cardiac myocytes; reducing the sodium influx lessens sodium-dependent calcium overload, improving diastolic relaxation and myocardial oxygen efficiency without meaningfully lowering heart rate or blood pressure. Q_GEN_PHAR_CVS_HF_ARR_025 [H] Nicorandil, used in angina, produces coronary and systemic vasodilation through a dual mechanism of:

Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_003 [H] A 22-year-old with type 1 diabetes on bedtime NPH insulin has persistent fasting morning hyperglycaemia. A 3 a.m. capillary blood glucose is 45 mg/dL. What is the most appropriate next step?

   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Insulin; Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Pancreatic Hormones
   concept: The low 3 a.m. glucose indicates the Somogyi effect: nocturnal hypoglycaemia triggers counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, cortisol, GH, catecholamines) causing rebound morning hyperglycaemia. Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_004 [E] The most common acute adverse effect encountered during insulin therapy is:

Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_006 [E] Sulfonylureas such as glimepiride and glibenclamide lower blood glucose primarily by:

   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Meglitinide analogues; Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Pancreatic Hormones
   concept: Repaglinide is a meglitinide (glinide) insulin secretagogue that, like sulfonylureas, closes beta-cell K-ATP channels but at a distinct binding site, giving a fast onset and short duration; it is taken before each meal and skipped if the meal is skipped, which suits erratic eating and limits inter-meal hypoglycaemia. Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_014 [M] Long-term metformin therapy is associated with malabsorption of which vitamin, potentially leading to megaloblastic anaemia and worsening peripheral neuropathy?
   *A. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)
    B. Vitamin C
    C. Vitamin D
    D. Vitamin K
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Biguanides (Metformin); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics — Endocrine Pancreas
   concept: Metformin interferes with calcium-dependent vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum, so prolonged use can cause B12 deficiency with megaloblastic anaemia and aggravation of diabetic peripheral neuropathy; periodic B12 monitoring is advised in long-term users. Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_015 [E] The drug of choice for long-term replacement therapy in hypothyroidism is:
    A. Liothyronine (T3)
   *B. Levothyroxine (T4)
    C. Carbimazole
    D. Propylthiouracil
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Thyroid Hormones and Antithyroid Drugs; Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Thyroid & Antithyroid Drugs
   concept: Levothyroxine (synthetic T4) is the drug of choice for hypothyroidism because it has a long half-life (about 7 days) allowing stable once-daily dosing, and is peripherally converted to the active T3 at physiologically regulated rates, giving smooth, steady hormone levels. Liothyronine (T3) has a short half-life and causes fluctuating levels, reserved for special situations like myxoedema coma. Carbimazole and propylthiouracil are antithyroid drugs used in hyperthyroidism, not replacement.
Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_016 [M] A woman in the first trimester of pregnancy develops Graves' hyperthyroidism requiring drug therapy. The preferred antithyroid agent during this period is:
    A. Carbimazole
    B. Methimazole
    C. Radioactive iodine (131-I)
   *D. Propylthiouracil
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antithyroid Drugs; Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Thyroid & Antithyroid Drugs
   concept: Propylthiouracil is preferred in the first trimester because methimazole/carbimazole are associated with teratogenic effects such as aplasia cutis, choanal and oesophageal atresia (methimazole embryopathy). Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_017 [M] Carbimazole and methimazole inhibit thyroid hormone synthesis mainly by:
   *A. Inhibiting thyroid peroxidase, thereby blocking iodide organification and iodotyrosine coupling
    B. Blocking peripheral conversion of T4 to T3
    C. Emitting radiation that destroys thyroid follicular cells
    D. Competitively inhibiting the sodium-iodide symporter
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antithyroid Drugs; Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics — Thyroid and Antithyroid Drugs
   concept: The thionamides carbimazole (a prodrug converted to methimazole) and methimazole inhibit thyroid peroxidase, blocking oxidation and organification of iodide and the coupling of iodotyrosines (MIT and DIT) to form T3 and T4, thereby reducing new hormone synthesis without affecting release of stored hormone (hence a delayed clinical effect). Only propylthiouracil additionally blocks peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion. Radiation destruction describes 131-I, and symporter inhibition describes perchlorate/thiocyanate.
Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_018 [H] A patient who started carbimazole two weeks ago presents with high fever and severe sore throat. The most important life-threatening adverse effect that must be urgently excluded is:
    A. Hepatotoxicity
    B. Aplastic anaemia
    C. Cholestatic jaundice
   *D. Agranulocytosis
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Antithyroid Drugs (adverse effects); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Thyroid & Antithyroid Drugs
   concept: Agranulocytosis is the most serious, though rare, idiosyncratic adverse effect of thionamides; it usually appears within the first 2-3 months and presents with fever and sore throat. Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_019 [M] Assertion (A): Propranolol is a useful adjunct in the management of thyroid storm. Reason (R): Propranolol acts by inhibiting thyroid peroxidase and thereby blocking new thyroid hormone synthesis. Choose the correct option.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
   *C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Thyroid storm management; Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Thyroid & Antithyroid Drugs
   concept: The assertion is true: propranolol rapidly controls the adrenergic features of thyroid storm (tachycardia, tremor, anxiety) and, at high doses, also inhibits peripheral conversion of T4 to the more active T3. Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_020 [H] Lugol's iodine given for about 10 days before thyroidectomy in Graves' disease is beneficial chiefly because it:
    A. Increases thyroid hormone release
    B. Stimulates peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion
   *C. Transiently inhibits hormone synthesis and release and reduces gland vascularity (Wolff-Chaikoff effect)
    D. Permanently ablates the gland
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Iodine and Iodides (preoperative preparation); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Thyroid & Antithyroid Drugs
   concept: High-dose iodide (Lugol's solution) acutely inhibits its own organification and hormone release and reduces thyroid size and vascularity via the Wolff-Chaikoff effect, making the gland firm and less vascular for safer surgery. Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_021 [M] Radioactive iodine (131-I) used to treat hyperthyroidism produces its therapeutic effect by:
    A. Inhibiting thyroid peroxidase
    B. Blocking peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion
    C. Competitively inhibiting iodide uptake at the symporter
   *D. Emitting beta particles that destroy thyroid follicular cells
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Radioactive Iodine; Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Thyroid & Antithyroid Drugs
   concept: 131-I is concentrated in the thyroid via the sodium-iodide symporter and emits beta particles that penetrate only 0.5-2 mm, selectively destroying follicular cells over weeks with little damage to surrounding tissue; hypothyroidism is a common late outcome requiring levothyroxine. It is contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation. Peroxidase inhibition describes thionamides, T4-to-T3 block describes propranolol/PTU, and symporter competition describes perchlorate.
Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_022 [H] Which corticosteroid possesses the highest anti-inflammatory (glucocorticoid) potency together with negligible mineralocorticoid (salt-retaining) activity?
    A. Hydrocortisone
    B. Prednisolone
   *C. Dexamethasone
    D. Fludrocortisone
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Corticosteroids (relative potencies); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Adrenocorticosteroids
   concept: Dexamethasone (and betamethasone) have the highest glucocorticoid/anti-inflammatory potency (about 25-30 times hydrocortisone) with essentially no mineralocorticoid activity, making them ideal when fluid retention must be avoided (e.g. cerebral oedema). Hydrocortisone has equal glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid activity, prednisolone is intermediate (about 4 times) with slight salt retention, and fludrocortisone is a potent mineralocorticoid used for its salt-retaining, not anti-inflammatory, action.
Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_023 [M] The anti-inflammatory action of glucocorticoids is mediated chiefly by:
    A. Directly and irreversibly inhibiting cyclo-oxygenase
   *B. Inducing annexin-1 (lipocortin-1), which inhibits phospholipase A2 and reduces prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis
    C. Antagonising histamine H1 receptors
    D. Blocking cysteinyl-leukotriene receptors
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Corticosteroids (mechanism); Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics — ACTH and Adrenal Steroids
   concept: Glucocorticoids bind cytoplasmic glucocorticoid receptors that translocate to the nucleus and alter gene transcription; a key effect is induction of annexin-1 (lipocortin-1), which inhibits phospholipase A2, cutting the release of arachidonic acid and hence both prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Irreversible COX inhibition describes aspirin, H1 blockade describes antihistamines, and leukotriene-receptor blockade describes montelukast.
Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_024 [E] Assertion (A): Long-term high-dose glucocorticoid therapy must be tapered gradually rather than stopped abruptly. Reason (R): Chronic glucocorticoid use causes osteoporosis. Choose the correct option.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
   *B. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Corticosteroids (HPA suppression, withdrawal); Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Adrenocorticosteroids
   concept: Both statements are individually true, but osteoporosis is not the reason for tapering. Chronic glucocorticoids suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and cause adrenal atrophy; abrupt withdrawal can therefore precipitate an acute adrenal (addisonian) crisis, which is why the dose is tapered to allow HPA recovery. Q_GEN_PHAR_ENDO_025 [H] Which agent used in Cushing syndrome acts as a competitive antagonist at the glucocorticoid receptor (and is also a progesterone-receptor antagonist)?
    A. Metyrapone
    B. Ketoconazole
    C. Fludrocortisone
   *D. Mifepristone
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology, 8th ed — Corticosteroid antagonists/inhibitors; Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Adrenocorticosteroids & Antagonists
   concept: Mifepristone (RU-486) is a competitive antagonist at the glucocorticoid receptor (and the progesterone receptor), and is used to control hyperglycaemia in inoperable Cushing syndrome. Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_001 [M] A patient presents with severe salicylate (aspirin, a weak acid, pKa ~3.5) poisoning. Which measure best enhances renal elimination of the drug?
    A. Acidification of urine with ammonium chloride
   *B. Alkalinisation of urine with intravenous sodium bicarbonate
    C. Restricting fluids to concentrate the urine
    D. Giving repeated doses of oral activated charcoal
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — General Pharmacology / Pharmacokinetics
   concept: By the pH-partition principle, a weak acid (HA <-> H+ + A-) becomes more ionised in alkaline urine. Sodium bicarbonate raises urine pH, so aspirin exists mainly as the ionised A- form which cannot be reabsorbed across the tubule and is 'ion-trapped' and excreted. Acidifying urine would favour excretion of weak BASES, not acids; fluid restriction reduces clearance; activated charcoal acts in the gut, not the kidney.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_002 [E] Assertion (A): A drug with an apparent volume of distribution (Vd) of 500 L in a 70 kg adult is extensively distributed into and bound by tissues. Reason (R): The apparent Vd of a drug can never exceed the volume of total body water.
    A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
   *C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Distribution / Volume of Distribution
   concept: Apparent Vd = Dose / plasma concentration; it is a proportionality constant, not a real anatomical volume. A value (500 L) far exceeding total body water (~42 L) means the drug is largely sequestered in tissues so little remains in plasma (e.g. digoxin ~500 L, chloroquine even more). Thus A is TRUE. R is FALSE because Vd frequently exceeds total body water for highly tissue-bound drugs; it is not capped at 42 L.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_003 [M] A patient stable on warfarin develops bleeding after a sulphonamide is added. The most likely pharmacokinetic mechanism is:
    A. Sulphonamide reduces warfarin absorption from the gut
   *B. Sulphonamide displaces warfarin from plasma albumin, raising free (active) warfarin
    C. Sulphonamide induces hepatic metabolism of warfarin
    D. Sulphonamide increases renal clearance of warfarin
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Plasma Protein Binding / Drug Interactions
   concept: Warfarin is ~99% bound to plasma albumin; only the free fraction is pharmacologically active. Sulphonamides are also highly albumin-bound acidic drugs and competitively DISPLACE warfarin from binding sites, transiently raising free warfarin and precipitating bleeding (a protein-binding drug interaction). Options A, C and D would each LOWER, not raise, the anticoagulant effect, so they cannot explain the bleeding.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_004 [M] A drug is given as a 400 mg intravenous bolus. The plasma concentration extrapolated back to time zero (C0) is 20 mg/L. What is the apparent volume of distribution?
    A. 5 L
   *B. 20 L
    C. 80 L
    D. 8000 L
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Pharmacokinetic Calculations / Volume of Distribution
   concept: Apparent Vd = Dose / C0. Here Vd = 400 mg / 20 mg/L = 20 L. Dividing amount (mg) by concentration (mg/L) correctly yields litres. 5 L (=100/20) and 80 L are arithmetic errors; 8000 L results from multiplying (400x20) instead of dividing. A 20 L Vd is roughly total body water, indicating distribution beyond plasma into interstitial and intracellular fluid.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_005 [H] A drug has an apparent volume of distribution of 40 L and an elimination rate constant (kel) of 0.15 per hour. What is its total body clearance?
    A. 0.15 L/hr
    B. 2.67 L/hr
   *C. 6 L/hr
    D. 267 L/hr
   key=C ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Pharmacokinetics (Clearance)
   concept: Clearance = kel x Vd = 0.15/hr x 40 L = 6 L/hr. Clearance is the volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time and links the first-order rate constant to the distribution volume. 2.67 L/hr wrongly divides Vd by kel; 267 L/hr wrongly divides kel into Vd (40/0.15); 0.15 L/hr confuses the rate constant with clearance. The correct product gives 6 L/hr.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_006 [H] A drug has a volume of distribution of 30 L and a total clearance of 6 L/hr. What is its elimination half-life?
    A. 0.7 hr
    B. 5 hr
    C. 6.9 hr
   *D. 3.5 hr
   key=D ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Pharmacokinetics (Half-life)
   concept: Half-life t1/2 = 0.693 x Vd / CL = 0.693 x (30/6) = 0.693 x 5 = 3.465, i.e. about 3.5 hr. The kel is CL/Vd = 6/30 = 0.2/hr, and 0.693/0.2 = 3.46 hr confirms the answer. 5 hr omits the 0.693 factor; 0.7 hr uses 0.693 alone; 6.9 hr mistakenly multiplies 0.693 by 10. Correct half-life is ~3.5 hr.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_007 [M] A drug (bioavailability F = 1, given IV) has a clearance of 3 L/hr. To maintain a target steady-state concentration of 4 mg/L with a dosing interval of 6 hours, what maintenance dose should be given each interval?
   *A. 72 mg
    B. 12 mg
    C. 24 mg
    D. 0.5 mg
   key=A ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Maintenance Dosing
   concept: Maintenance dose per interval = CL x Css x tau / F = 3 L/hr x 4 mg/L x 6 hr / 1 = 72 mg every 6 hours. Dimensionally (L/hr)(mg/L)(hr) = mg. 12 mg is the hourly infusion RATE (CL x Css) but not the per-interval dose; 24 mg and 0.5 mg are arithmetic errors. At steady state the amount administered per interval must replace the amount cleared, giving 72 mg.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_008 [M] A drug with an elimination half-life of 12 hours is started on a fixed maintenance regimen without a loading dose. Approximately how long will it take to reach steady-state (plateau) plasma concentration?
    A. 12 hours
    B. 24 hours
   *C. About 2 to 2.5 days (4-5 half-lives)
    D. About 2 weeks
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Kinetics of Repeated Dosing / Plateau Principle
   concept: Steady state is reached in about 4-5 half-lives regardless of dose or dosing rate: 4 x 12 = 48 hr (~94%) and 5 x 12 = 60 hr (~97%), i.e. roughly 2-2.5 days. One half-life (12 hr) gives only 50% and two (24 hr) only 75% of plateau, so those are too early; 2 weeks is far beyond plateau. Only a loading dose speeds attainment of the target level; the time to plateau itself depends solely on t1/2.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_009 [E] Assertion (A): A small increase in the dose of phenytoin can cause a disproportionately large rise in its plasma concentration. Reason (R): At therapeutic doses phenytoin is eliminated by zero-order (capacity-limited/saturation) kinetics.
   *A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Order of Reaction / Non-linear Kinetics
   concept: Phenytoin's metabolising enzymes saturate within the therapeutic range, so elimination shifts from first-order to ZERO-order (a constant amount, not a constant fraction, is removed per unit time). Once saturated, further dose increments are cleared no faster, so plasma levels climb steeply and unpredictably, risking toxicity (nystagmus, ataxia). Thus both statements are true AND the zero-order (saturation) kinetics is precisely why small dose rises cause large level rises, making R the correct explanation of A.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_010 [E] Glyceryl trinitrate (nitroglycerin) is administered sublingually rather than orally for an acute angina attack chiefly because the sublingual route:
    A. Provides a slow, sustained release of the drug
   *B. Avoids extensive hepatic first-pass metabolism, allowing rapid systemic effect
    C. Protects the drug from destruction by gastric acid only
    D. Increases plasma protein binding of the drug
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Routes of Administration / First-pass Metabolism
   concept: Drug absorbed from the sublingual mucosa drains into the superior vena cava and enters the systemic circulation directly, BYPASSING the portal vein and hepatic first-pass metabolism. Orally swallowed nitroglycerin is almost completely inactivated on first pass through the liver, giving very low bioavailability. The sublingual route therefore gives a rapid onset (1-2 min), ideal for acute angina; it is not a slow-release route and does not act by altering protein binding.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_011 [M] A woman on combined oral contraceptive pills is started on rifampicin for tuberculosis and later has an unintended pregnancy. The most likely mechanism of contraceptive failure is:
   *A. Rifampicin induces hepatic CYP450 enzymes, increasing metabolism of the contraceptive steroids
    B. Rifampicin inhibits hepatic CYP450 enzymes, raising steroid levels
    C. Rifampicin displaces the steroids from plasma proteins
    D. Rifampicin reduces gastric emptying and steroid absorption
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Enzyme Induction / Drug Interactions
   concept: Rifampicin is a potent INDUCER of hepatic microsomal CYP450 enzymes (and P-glycoprotein). Enzyme induction accelerates the metabolism of oestrogen and progestin, lowering their plasma levels below the contraceptive threshold and causing failure. Induction is a slow process (days to weeks) as new enzyme protein is synthesised. Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_012 [M] Which of the following drugs INHIBITS hepatic microsomal enzymes and can raise plasma levels of co-administered drugs?
    A. Rifampicin
    B. Phenobarbitone
   *C. Erythromycin
    D. Carbamazepine
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Enzyme Inhibition / Drug Interactions
   concept: Erythromycin (like clarithromycin, cimetidine, ketoconazole, ciprofloxacin and grapefruit juice) is a CYP450 INHIBITOR; it slows metabolism of substrates, raising their plasma levels and risking toxicity (e.g. with theophylline or warfarin). Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_013 [M] Which of the following is a prodrug that is pharmacologically inactive until converted to its active form in the body?
   *A. Enalapril
    B. Atenolol
    C. Captopril
    D. Propranolol
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Biotransformation / Prodrugs
   concept: Enalapril is an ester prodrug that is hydrolysed by hepatic esterases to the active ACE inhibitor enalaprilat, which is itself poorly absorbed orally. Captopril, by contrast, is an intrinsically active ACE inhibitor (needs no activation). Atenolol and propranolol are active beta-blockers given as such. Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_014 [H] A young patient remains apnoeic and paralysed for several hours after a single dose of succinylcholine given for a routine procedure. The most likely underlying cause is:
    A. CYP2D6 poor-metaboliser genotype
    B. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency
    C. Slow acetylator (NAT2) status
   *D. Deficient or atypical plasma pseudocholinesterase (butyrylcholinesterase)

   concept: Succinylcholine is normally hydrolysed rapidly by plasma pseudocholinesterase (butyrylcholinesterase), giving a brief action (~5 min). Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_015 [E] Which of the following biotransformation reactions is a Phase II (conjugation) reaction?
    A. Aromatic hydroxylation
    B. N-dealkylation
    C. Nitro reduction
   *D. Glucuronidation
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Metabolism (Phase I vs Phase II)
   concept: Phase II reactions are synthetic CONJUGATIONS in which an endogenous group is attached to the drug or its Phase I metabolite, usually yielding a more water-soluble, readily excreted product; glucuronidation is the commonest, along with sulfation, acetylation, methylation, glycine and glutathione conjugation. Aromatic hydroxylation and N-dealkylation are Phase I OXIDATIONS (CYP450), and nitro reduction is a Phase I reduction, so all three are Phase I, not Phase II.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_016 [H] A broad-spectrum antibiotic reduces the efficacy of oral oestrogen-containing contraceptives even though the antibiotic is not an enzyme inducer. The best explanation is that the antibiotic:
    A. Displaces oestrogen from plasma albumin
   *B. Disrupts gut flora, interrupting the enterohepatic circulation of oestrogen
    C. Chelates the oestrogen in the stomach
    D. Increases glomerular filtration of oestrogen
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Excretion / Enterohepatic Circulation
   concept: Oestrogens are conjugated (glucuronidated/sulfated) in the liver and excreted in bile; gut bacteria then DECONJUGATE them in the intestine so that free oestrogen is reabsorbed - the enterohepatic circulation, which sustains plasma levels. A broad-spectrum antibiotic kills these bacteria, so less oestrogen is reabsorbed and more is lost in faeces, lowering levels and risking contraceptive failure. This is distinct from CYP induction (as with rifampicin); options A, C and D are not the recognised mechanism.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_017 [E] Which of the following drugs most requires therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) because of its narrow therapeutic index?
    A. Amoxicillin
   *B. Lithium
    C. Paracetamol at therapeutic doses
    D. Ranitidine
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Therapeutic Drug Monitoring / Therapeutic Index
   concept: Lithium has a very narrow therapeutic index (therapeutic range ~0.6-1.2 mEq/L, toxicity just above), so plasma levels must be monitored to avoid tremor, seizures and renal toxicity. Amoxicillin, ranitidine and therapeutic-dose paracetamol have wide safety margins and are not routinely monitored, making lithium the clear answer.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_018 [H] In an animal study a drug has a median lethal dose (LD50) of 300 mg/kg and a median effective dose (ED50) of 15 mg/kg. What is its therapeutic index?
    A. 0.05
    B. 5
   *C. 20
    D. 45
   key=C ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Therapeutic Index / Quantal Dose-Response
   concept: Therapeutic index = LD50 / ED50 = 300 / 15 = 20. A larger value denotes a wider margin of safety between the effective and lethal doses. 0.05 inverts the ratio (ED50/LD50); 5 and 45 are arithmetic errors. Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_019 [M] Two analgesics reach the SAME maximal pain relief, but drug A does so at 10 mg whereas drug B needs 100 mg. Assertion (A): The more potent drug is always the clinically superior analgesic. Reason (R): Potency reflects the dose needed for an effect (position on the dose axis / ED50), whereas efficacy is the maximal effect achievable.
    A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
   *D. A is false but R is true
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Potency vs Efficacy / Dose-Response
   concept: Drug A is more POTENT (lower dose/ED50) but both drugs have EQUAL EFFICACY because they reach the same maximal effect (Emax). Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_020 [H] Which statement best describes an inverse agonist?
   *A. It binds the receptor and produces an effect OPPOSITE to that of the agonist by reducing the receptor's constitutive (basal) activity
    B. It binds the receptor but produces no effect of its own, only blocking the agonist
    C. It binds and produces a submaximal effect, acting as an antagonist in the presence of a full agonist
    D. It binds a site distinct from the agonist to enhance agonist binding
   key=A ref=Katzung Basic & Clinical Pharmacology — Receptor Theory / Agonists and Antagonists
   concept: An inverse agonist binds the same receptor as the agonist but stabilises the INACTIVE conformation, reducing the receptor's spontaneous (constitutive) basal activity and thus producing an effect opposite to the agonist (e.g. certain beta-carbolines at the GABA-A benzodiazepine site are anxiogenic/proconvulsant). Option B describes a pure (neutral) antagonist, option C a partial agonist, and option D a positive allosteric modulator - each distinct from an inverse agonist.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_021 [M] Insulin produces its cellular effects primarily by acting on which type of receptor?
    A. A G-protein coupled receptor linked to cAMP
    B. A ligand-gated ion channel
   *C. A cell-surface receptor with intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity
    D. An intracellular nuclear (gene-transcription) receptor
   key=C ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Receptor Types / Signal Transduction
   concept: Insulin binds a transmembrane receptor whose intracellular domain has intrinsic TYROSINE KINASE activity; ligand binding triggers autophosphorylation and phosphorylation of insulin-receptor substrates, initiating downstream signalling (a response in seconds-to-minutes). The four superfamilies of receptors are: ligand-gated ion channels (fastest, e.g. nicotinic), G-protein coupled receptors (cAMP/IP3-DAG), enzyme-linked receptors such as receptor tyrosine kinases (insulin, growth factors), and intracellular nuclear receptors (steroids, slowest); insulin belongs to the tyrosine-kinase class.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_022 [H] A hormone activates a cell-surface receptor coupled to a stimulatory Gs protein, which then activates adenylyl cyclase. The second messenger whose intracellular level rises is:
   *A. Cyclic AMP (cAMP)
    B. Inositol trisphosphate (IP3)
    C. Diacylglycerol (DAG)
    D. Cyclic GMP (cGMP)
   key=A ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Second Messengers / GPCR Signalling
   concept: A Gs-coupled receptor stimulates adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP); cAMP then activates protein kinase A (e.g. beta-adrenergic and glucagon signalling). By contrast, Gq-coupled receptors activate phospholipase C to generate IP3 (releases Ca2+) and DAG (activates PKC), while cGMP is produced by guanylyl cyclase (nitric-oxide / ANP pathways). Nuclear receptors act via gene transcription and use no cytoplasmic second messenger, so cAMP is the correct answer here.
Q_GEN_PHAR_GEN_PKPD_023 [H] When ephedrine is injected repeatedly at short intervals, each successive dose produces a progressively smaller pressor response within minutes to hours. This rapidly developing loss of response is best termed:
    A. Idiosyncrasy
    B. Cumulation
    C. Drug allergy
   *D. Tachyphylaxis
   key=D ref=KD Tripathi Essentials of Medical Pharmacology — Tachyphylaxis / Tolerance / Drug Response
   concept: Tachyphylaxis is a RAPID (within minutes-hours) fall in response on repeated dosing. For ephedrine, an indirectly acting sympathomimetic, it results from progressive depletion of releasable noradrenaline stores plus receptor desensitisation. It differs from tolerance, which develops slowly over days-weeks. Idiosyncrasy is a genetically-determined abnormal response, cumulation is accumulation of a slowly-eliminated drug causing toxicity, and allergy is an immune-mediated reaction - none fits this rapid, dosing-related decline.
Q_c1_025 [E] Which statement correctly describes ZERO-order kinetics of drug elimination?
    A. A constant FRACTION of drug is eliminated per unit time
   *B. A constant AMOUNT of drug is eliminated per unit time
    C. Half-life is constant and independent of dose
    D. It applies to most drugs at ordinary therapeutic doses
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi 8e, Ch. Pharmacokinetics
   concept: Zero-order (saturation) kinetics eliminates a constant amount of drug per unit time because metabolizing enzymes are saturated; half-life is NOT constant. Classic examples are phenytoin, ethanol, and high-dose aspirin. First-order kinetics (most drugs) eliminates a constant fraction.
Q_c1_026 [E] A drug undergoes extensive hepatic first-pass metabolism when taken orally. What is its bioavailability by the intravenous route?
    A. Reduced below the oral value
   *B. 100% by definition
    C. Unchanged from the oral route
    D. Unpredictable and always variable
   key=B ref=KD Tripathi 8e, Ch. Pharmacokinetics
   concept: Intravenous administration bypasses absorption and first-pass metabolism, so bioavailability is 100% (the reference standard). Drugs with high first-pass effect (propranolol, GTN, lignocaine, morphine) have low ORAL bioavailability.
