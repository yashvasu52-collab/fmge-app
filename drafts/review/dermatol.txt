
=== Dermatology / Leprosy (types, lepra reactions)
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_001 [E] Leprosy (Hansen's disease) is caused by which of the following organisms?
    A. Mycobacterium tuberculosis
   *B. Mycobacterium leprae
    C. Mycobacterium ulcerans
    D. Mycobacterium marinum
   key=B ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Leprosy (Hansen's Disease)
   concept: Leprosy is caused by Mycobacterium leprae, an obligate intracellular acid-fast bacillus and the first bacterium identified as a cause of human disease (Hansen, 1873). It cannot be grown on artificial media (propagated in the mouse foot-pad and nine-banded armadillo), has the longest generation time of any bacterium (~12-14 days), and is thermophile-averse, favouring cool superficial skin and peripheral nerves. Distractors: M. ulcerans causes Buruli ulcer, M. marinum causes fish-tank/swimming-pool granuloma, and M. tuberculosis causes tuberculosis.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_002 [E] Because M. leprae prefers cooler tissues, leprosy lesions characteristically SPARE which of the following relatively warm body areas?
    A. Ear lobes
    B. Dorsum of the hands
   *C. Axilla
    D. Extensor surface of the forearm
   key=C ref=Neena Khanna — Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD, Leprosy
   concept: M. leprae grows optimally at cooler temperatures (~27-30°C), so lesions favour cool, superficial, acral sites: ear lobes, dorsa of hands and feet, extensor limbs, face, and nerves where they run superficially over bone. Warm, well-perfused, covered or midline regions are characteristically spared — the axillae, groin, perineum, hairy scalp, and the warm midline of the back (sometimes called 'immune zones'). Recognising this temperature-dependent distribution helps separate leprosy from other hypopigmenting dermatoses.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_003 [M] In tuberculoid (TT) leprosy on the Ridley-Jopling spectrum, the host immune response is BEST characterised as:
   *A. Strong Th1 cell-mediated response with well-formed epithelioid granulomas and a low (paucibacillary) load
    B. Strong Th2 humoral response with foamy macrophages and a high bacillary load
    C. Complete absence of cell-mediated immunity with abundant bacilli in skin
    D. Predominant IgE-driven response with a heavy eosinophilic infiltrate
   key=A ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Immunology of Leprosy / Ridley-Jopling Spectrum
   concept: The Ridley-Jopling spectrum is governed by cell-mediated immunity. The tuberculoid (TT) pole shows a vigorous Th1 response (IFN-γ, IL-2) producing well-formed epithelioid-cell granulomas with Langhans giant cells and dense lymphocytes, few or no demonstrable bacilli (paucibacillary), and a positive lepromin test. The lepromatous (LL) pole is the mirror image — Th2 dominance, foamy (Virchow) macrophages stuffed with bacilli, and a negative lepromin test. Leprosy is not IgE/eosinophil driven, excluding option D.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_004 [M] A skin biopsy in lepromatous leprosy (LL) would MOST characteristically show:
    A. Well-formed epithelioid granulomas with only rare bacilli
    B. Caseating necrosis surrounded by Langhans giant cells
    C. A non-specific perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate only
   *D. Sheets of foamy macrophages (Virchow/lepra cells) laden with acid-fast bacilli, separated from the epidermis by a clear grenz zone
   key=D ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Histopathology of Leprosy
   concept: LL leprosy reflects failed cell-mediated immunity (Th2 skew): macrophages ingest but cannot kill M. leprae, becoming foamy 'Virchow/lepra cells' packed with bacilli in clumps (globi), giving a very high bacillary index. A clear subepidermal band of uninvolved dermis (the 'grenz zone') separates the infiltrate from a flattened, atrophic epidermis. Option A describes the tuberculoid pole; caseation (option B) is a feature of tuberculosis, not leprosy; option C is too non-specific for the classic LL picture.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_005 [H] Which statement about the lepromin (Mitsuda) test is CORRECT?
    A. It is a diagnostic test that is positive in all forms of leprosy
   *B. It is positive in tuberculoid (TT) leprosy and negative in lepromatous (LL) leprosy, reflecting cell-mediated immunity, and is used for classification/prognosis rather than diagnosis
    C. It is positive in lepromatous and negative in tuberculoid leprosy
    D. It becomes positive only during a type 2 (ENL) reaction
   key=B ref=Neena Khanna — Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD, Lepromin Test
   concept: The lepromin test measures the host's cell-mediated immunity to M. leprae antigen, not the presence of infection, so it is NOT diagnostic — even healthy immunocompetent contacts can be positive. It is strongly positive at the tuberculoid (TT) pole (intact CMI, granuloma formation) and negative at the lepromatous (LL) pole (immunological anergy), grading progressively across BT-BB-BL. The late Mitsuda reaction (read at ~21-28 days) is used for classification and prognosis, making option B correct and A/C/D wrong.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_006 [E] According to WHO, diagnosis of leprosy rests on at least one of three cardinal signs. Which of the following is NOT one of these cardinal signs?
    A. A hypopigmented or reddish skin patch with definite loss of sensation
    B. A thickened or enlarged peripheral nerve with loss of sensory/motor function
   *C. A positive lepromin (Mitsuda) test
    D. Demonstration of acid-fast bacilli in a slit-skin smear
   key=C ref=WHO Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment and Prevention of Leprosy (2018)
   concept: WHO defines the diagnosis of leprosy by the presence of at least one of THREE cardinal signs: (1) a hypopigmented or reddish patch with definite sensory loss; (2) a thickened or enlarged peripheral nerve with loss of sensation and/or weakness of the muscles it supplies; and (3) presence of acid-fast bacilli in a slit-skin smear. The lepromin test measures host immunity, not infection, so it is used for classification and prognosis but is explicitly NOT a diagnostic cardinal sign — making option C the exception.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_007 [M] A leprosy patient has a thickened ulnar nerve at the elbow, sensory loss over the little and medial half of the ring finger, and hyperextension at the MCP joints with flexion at the IP joints of the 4th and 5th fingers. This deformity is:
   *A. Ulnar (partial) claw hand
    B. Ape-thumb deformity from median nerve palsy
    C. Wrist drop from radial nerve palsy
    D. Foot drop from common peroneal palsy
   key=A ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Nerve Involvement and Deformities in Leprosy
   concept: The ulnar nerve is the most commonly involved nerve trunk in leprosy; damage where it lies superficially at the elbow causes sensory loss over the little and medial ring finger and wasting/weakness of the intrinsic hand muscles (interossei, medial two lumbricals, hypothenar). Loss of the medial lumbricals produces the 'partial claw hand' — MCP hyperextension with IP flexion of the 4th and 5th fingers. A combined ulnar+median palsy extends clawing to all fingers ('complete claw'); radial palsy gives wrist drop; peroneal gives foot drop.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_008 [M] A leprosy patient develops inability to dorsiflex and evert the foot, a high-stepping gait, and sensory loss over the dorsum of the foot. The nerve MOST likely involved is the:
    A. Posterior tibial nerve
    B. Sural nerve
    C. Femoral nerve
   *D. Common peroneal (lateral popliteal) nerve
   key=D ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Peripheral Nerve Involvement in Leprosy
   concept: The common peroneal (lateral popliteal) nerve is frequently thickened in leprosy as it winds superficially around the neck of the fibula — a cool, exposed site. Its involvement weakens dorsiflexion and eversion, producing foot drop with a compensatory high-stepping gait and sensory loss over the dorsum of the foot and lateral leg. The posterior tibial nerve supplies the sole, so its damage instead causes plantar anaesthesia, clawing of toes, and trophic plantar ulcers. The sural nerve is purely sensory; the femoral nerve is not a leprosy predilection site.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_009 [H] Which peripheral nerve, commonly thickened in leprosy, is a PURELY SENSORY nerve that can often be seen and rolled beneath the skin as it crosses the sternocleidomastoid?
    A. Facial nerve
   *B. Greater auricular nerve
    C. Ulnar nerve
    D. Radial cutaneous nerve
   key=B ref=Neena Khanna — Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD, Nerve Examination in Leprosy
   concept: Nerves commonly thickened in leprosy include the ulnar, median, common peroneal, posterior tibial, radial cutaneous, facial and greater auricular nerves. The greater auricular nerve is a purely sensory branch of the cervical plexus (C2-C3) that runs superficially across the sternocleidomastoid — when enlarged it can be both seen and palpably rolled under the skin, a classic bedside sign. The facial nerve is motor (its zygomatic branch damage causes lagophthalmos); the ulnar and radial cutaneous are mixed/limb nerves and are not the answer here.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_010 [M] A leprosy patient develops a painless, deep, punched-out ulcer over a metatarsal head on the sole. The PRIMARY underlying mechanism is:
    A. Ischaemia due to a small-vessel vasculitis
    B. Direct bacillary destruction of the skin
   *C. Loss of protective (pain) sensation leading to repeated unnoticed trauma
    D. Immune-complex deposition in dermal vessels
   key=C ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Disability and Trophic Ulcers in Leprosy
   concept: Trophic (neuropathic/plantar) ulcers in leprosy arise from anaesthesia — loss of protective pain and pressure sensation (posterior tibial nerve damage) means repeated trauma over weight-bearing points (metatarsal heads, heel) goes unnoticed, producing painless, deep, punched-out ulcers. They are a marker of established nerve damage and disability, not active infection or vasculitis. Management centres on protective/customised footwear, meticulous wound care, off-loading and preventing further trauma, not on antibacterial escalation alone.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_011 [M] The WHO multidrug therapy (MDT) regimen classically used for adult PAUCIBACILLARY (PB) leprosy consists of:
   *A. Rifampicin 600 mg once monthly (supervised) plus dapsone 100 mg daily, for 6 months
    B. Rifampicin 600 mg once monthly plus dapsone 100 mg daily plus clofazimine, for 12 months
    C. Dapsone 100 mg daily alone for 6 months
    D. Rifampicin 600 mg daily plus dapsone 100 mg daily for 6 months
   key=A ref=WHO Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment and Prevention of Leprosy (2018); NLEP guidelines
   concept: The classic WHO adult PB MDT is rifampicin 600 mg once monthly (supervised) plus dapsone 100 mg daily, given for 6 months. Rifampicin is given monthly (not daily) to limit toxicity and cost while retaining potent bactericidal effect. IMPORTANT change-prone program fact: WHO's 2018 guideline recommends a UNIFORM three-drug regimen (adding clofazimine) for BOTH PB and MB, so many current NLEP programmes now use rifampicin+dapsone+clofazimine for PB too — verify against the current WHO/NLEP edition before quoting.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_012 [M] The standard WHO multidrug therapy (MDT) for adult MULTIBACILLARY (MB) leprosy is:
    A. Rifampicin monthly plus dapsone daily for 6 months
    B. Rifampicin daily plus dapsone daily plus clofazimine daily for 12 months
    C. Dapsone plus clofazimine daily for 24 months
   *D. Rifampicin 600 mg monthly (supervised) + clofazimine 300 mg monthly (supervised) + clofazimine 50 mg daily + dapsone 100 mg daily, for 12 months
   key=D ref=WHO Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment and Prevention of Leprosy (2018); NLEP guidelines
   concept: The WHO adult MB MDT is: rifampicin 600 mg once monthly (supervised) + clofazimine 300 mg once monthly (supervised), together with clofazimine 50 mg daily + dapsone 100 mg daily, given for 12 months. The monthly rifampicin and monthly clofazimine are the supervised 'pulse' doses. The older MB regimen ran for 24 months but was shortened to 12 months. Note duration/regimen details are periodically revised — verify against the current WHO/NLEP edition. Option A is the PB regimen; rifampicin is monthly, not daily (excluding B).
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_013 [H] A patient has a SINGLE skin lesion of leprosy with no peripheral nerve trunk involvement. Which single-dose regimen has been used by WHO for this presentation?
    A. Rifampicin + dapsone + clofazimine as a single combined dose
   *B. Rifampicin 600 mg + ofloxacin 400 mg + minocycline 100 mg (ROM) as a single dose
    C. Dapsone 100 mg as a single dose
    D. Rifampicin 600 mg as a single dose alone
   key=B ref=WHO Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment and Prevention of Leprosy (2018)
   concept: For single-lesion paucibacillary leprosy (one patch, no nerve trunk involvement), WHO previously endorsed a single supervised dose of ROM: Rifampicin 600 mg + Ofloxacin 400 mg + Minocycline 100 mg. Change-prone caveat: current WHO (2018) guidance favours standard 6-month PB MDT even for single lesions in many programmes, so single-dose ROM is now of limited/declining use — verify against the current edition. Ofloxacin and minocycline (with clarithromycin) also serve as second-line agents for rifampicin resistance or intolerance.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_014 [M] A borderline leprosy patient on MDT develops acute inflammation of existing skin lesions together with a NEW motor deficit from acute neuritis (a type 1 reversal reaction). The most appropriate first-line treatment is:
    A. Stop MDT and start thalidomide
    B. High-dose clofazimine alone
   *C. Systemic corticosteroids (e.g., prednisolone) while continuing MDT
    D. NSAIDs alone with observation
   key=C ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Lepra Reactions (Type 1 / Reversal Reaction)
   concept: Type 1 (reversal) reactions are delayed cell-mediated (type IV) immunological upgrades occurring in borderline leprosy, causing acute inflammation of existing lesions and — critically — acute neuritis that can cause permanent nerve damage. Because deficits may become irreversible, first-line treatment is systemic corticosteroids (e.g., prednisolone, tapered over several weeks to months) to reduce nerve inflammation, WHILE antileprosy MDT is continued uninterrupted. Thalidomide (option A) is reserved for type 2/ENL reactions, not type 1; NSAIDs/clofazimine alone are inadequate for neuritis.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_015 [M] Erythema nodosum leprosum (type 2 lepra reaction) is BEST characterised as:
   *A. An immune-complex-mediated (type III hypersensitivity) reaction seen in lepromatous/borderline-lepromatous leprosy, with crops of tender erythematous nodules, fever and systemic features
    B. A type IV cell-mediated reaction occurring only in tuberculoid leprosy
    C. An IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity reaction
    D. Simple bacterial abscess formation from secondary infection
   key=A ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Lepra Reactions (Type 2 / Erythema Nodosum Leprosum)
   concept: Erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL, type 2 reaction) is an immune-complex-mediated (type III hypersensitivity) phenomenon occurring in high-bacillary-load forms (LL and BL), driven by antigen release and circulating immune complexes with a neutrophilic dermal infiltrate. It presents with recurrent crops of tender, erythematous subcutaneous nodules plus systemic features — fever, malaise, arthralgia, iridocyclitis, orchitis, lymphadenopathy and neuritis. Type 1 reactions, by contrast, are type IV cell-mediated events seen in borderline disease, distinguishing them from ENL.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_016 [H] Thalidomide is highly effective for severe, recurrent erythema nodosum leprosum, but its use is ABSOLUTELY contraindicated in which situation?
    A. Elderly male patients
    B. Patients already receiving clofazimine
    C. Patients with coexisting diabetes mellitus
   *D. Women of child-bearing potential / pregnancy, owing to severe teratogenicity
   key=D ref=WHO Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment and Prevention of Leprosy — Management of ENL
   concept: Thalidomide is very effective for severe, recurrent or steroid-dependent ENL (type 2 reaction), acting largely through TNF-α suppression. It is ABSOLUTELY contraindicated in pregnancy and in women of child-bearing potential without stringent contraception because it is a potent teratogen causing phocomelia and other limb-reduction defects (the historic thalidomide disaster). Other adverse effects include peripheral neuropathy, drowsiness and constipation. Safer alternatives for ENL include systemic corticosteroids and high-dose clofazimine, especially in women who may conceive.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_017 [M] The most important dose-related adverse effect of dapsone, especially in a G6PD-deficient patient, is:
    A. Reddish-black skin hyperpigmentation
   *B. Haemolytic anaemia (with methaemoglobinaemia)
    C. Orange discoloration of urine and sweat
    D. Symmetrical peripheral sensory neuropathy
   key=B ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Antileprosy Drugs and Adverse Effects (Dapsone)
   concept: Dapsone characteristically produces dose-related oxidative haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia, which are markedly worse in G6PD-deficient individuals — so G6PD status should be checked before starting where feasible. Other dapsone effects include dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome (a DRESS-like reaction weeks after starting) and agranulocytosis. Reddish-black skin pigmentation (option A) is caused by clofazimine; orange discoloration of body fluids (option C) is caused by rifampicin; significant peripheral neuropathy (option D) is not a typical dapsone effect.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_018 [H] About five weeks after starting MDT, a leprosy patient develops fever, a widespread morbilliform rash, facial oedema, generalised lymphadenopathy, hepatitis and marked eosinophilia. The MOST likely cause is:
    A. Type 1 (reversal) lepra reaction
    B. Erythema nodosum leprosum (type 2 reaction)
   *C. Dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome
    D. Rifampicin-induced flu-like syndrome
   key=C ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Dapsone Hypersensitivity (Dapsone Syndrome)
   concept: Dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome (a DRESS-type reaction) typically appears 4-6 weeks after starting dapsone and presents with fever, a morbilliform or exfoliative rash, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, hepatitis and eosinophilia (sometimes a mononucleosis-like picture). Management is IMMEDIATE dapsone withdrawal plus systemic corticosteroids; untreated it can be fatal. It is distinguished from lepra reactions, which involve inflammation of skin lesions/nerves rather than a diffuse drug-rash morphology, and from rifampicin's transient flu-like syndrome seen with intermittent dosing.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_019 [E] A well-known and largely reversible adverse effect of clofazimine used in MDT is:
   *A. Reddish-brown to bluish-black hyperpigmentation of the skin and lesions
    B. Haemolytic anaemia in G6PD deficiency
    C. Nephrotoxicity
    D. Irreversible ototoxicity
   key=A ref=Neena Khanna — Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD, Antileprosy Drugs (Clofazimine)
   concept: Clofazimine characteristically causes a dose-dependent reddish-brown to bluish-black discoloration of the skin (most marked over lesions and sun-exposed areas), along with dryness/ichthyosis, and reddish discoloration of urine, sweat and conjunctivae; this pigmentation is cosmetically distressing but reversible over months to years after stopping and is a common cause of poor compliance. High doses can also cause GI/enteropathy effects. Haemolysis (option B) is a dapsone effect; nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity are not typical MDT adverse effects.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_020 [H] In the WHO disability grading for leprosy, GRADE 2 disability of the hand or foot refers to:
    A. No anaesthesia and no visible deformity
    B. Anaesthesia present but no visible deformity or damage
    C. Loss of eyebrows (madarosis) only
   *D. Visible deformity or damage of the hand/foot (e.g., ulcer, claw hand, foot drop, resorption)
   key=D ref=WHO Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment and Prevention of Leprosy — Disability Grading
   concept: WHO disability grading for hands and feet: Grade 0 = no anaesthesia and no visible deformity/damage; Grade 1 = loss of protective sensation (anaesthesia) present but NO visible deformity or damage; Grade 2 = VISIBLE deformity or damage — e.g., trophic ulcer, claw hand, foot drop, or resorption/shortening of digits. For the eye: Grade 1 = eye problems present but vision not severely affected; Grade 2 = severe visual impairment, lagophthalmos, or corneal changes. The Grade 2 disability rate is a key programme monitoring indicator.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_021 [E] A child presents with a hypopigmented patch. Which single feature would MOST strongly favour leprosy over pityriasis alba or vitiligo?
    A. Fine surface scaling within the patch
   *B. Definite loss of sensation within the patch
    C. Complete, chalky-white depigmentation
    D. Location on the cheeks
   key=B ref=Neena Khanna — Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD, Differential Diagnosis of Hypopigmented Macules
   concept: The key discriminator of a leprosy patch is DEFINITE loss of sensation (touch, temperature and/or pain) within it, often accompanied by dryness (loss of sweating), loss of hair, and sometimes a palpably thickened feeding nerve. Vitiligo is chalky-white DEPIGMENTATION with entirely intact sensation; pityriasis alba shows scaly HYPOpigmentation with intact sensation, typically on children's cheeks. Other sensation-intact mimics include pityriasis versicolor, post-inflammatory hypopigmentation and naevus depigmentosus — none of which is anaesthetic.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_022 [H] A patient with a history of prolonged dapsone monotherapy develops multiple shiny, well-defined, dome-shaped cutaneous and subcutaneous nodules on apparently normal skin; smears show a very high bacillary load with spindle-shaped histiocytes. The diagnosis is:
    A. Erythema nodosum leprosum
    B. Borderline tuberculoid leprosy
   *C. Histoid leprosy
    D. Lucio phenomenon
   key=C ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Histoid Leprosy
   concept: Histoid leprosy is a distinct multibacillary variant, classically associated with dapsone monotherapy/resistance or irregular treatment, though it can arise de novo. It presents with characteristic shiny, firm, well-circumscribed, dome-shaped cutaneous and subcutaneous nodules and plaques on apparently NORMAL-looking skin. Histology shows interlacing spindle-shaped ('histoid') histiocytes packed with abundant acid-fast bacilli (very high bacillary index). It is not a reaction (unlike ENL or Lucio phenomenon) and is heavily bacillated, unlike the paucibacillary borderline tuberculoid form.
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_023 [H] A patient with untreated diffuse, non-nodular lepromatous (Lucio) leprosy develops painful, jagged/angulated, stellate purpuric skin lesions that necrose and ulcerate. This severe reaction is termed:
   *A. Lucio phenomenon
    B. Type 1 (reversal) reaction
    C. Erythema nodosum leprosum
    D. Histoid transformation
   key=A ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Lucio Leprosy and the Lucio Phenomenon
   concept: The Lucio phenomenon is a severe necrotizing reaction — essentially a cutaneous vasculitis/thrombotic vaso-occlusion with extensive endothelial invasion by bacilli — occurring in untreated diffuse, non-nodular lepromatous ('Lucio') leprosy, most classically reported from Mexico and Central America. It produces painful, sharply angulated/jagged, stellate purpuric plaques that undergo necrosis into irregular ulcers healing with atrophic scars; secondary sepsis can be fatal. It differs from ENL (crops of tender nodules) and from type 1 reactions (inflammation of existing lesions with neuritis).
Q_GEN_DERM_LEPROSY_024 [M] A leprosy patient is unable to fully close one eye (lagophthalmos), placing the cornea at risk of exposure keratitis. This is MOST directly due to involvement of the:
    A. Sensory branch of the trigeminal (V) nerve
    B. Oculomotor (III) nerve
    C. Optic (II) nerve
   *D. Zygomatic/temporal branches of the facial (VII) nerve supplying orbicularis oculi
   key=D ref=IADVL Textbook of Dermatology — Ocular Complications and Facial Nerve Involvement in Leprosy
   concept: Lagophthalmos (inability to fully close the eye) in leprosy results from paralysis of the orbicularis oculi due to involvement of the zygomatic and temporal branches of the facial (VII) nerve. The eye cannot be closed or blinked properly, leading to exposure keratitis, corneal ulceration and potentially blindness — hence eye protection, lubrication and lid-care are vital. Coexisting corneal anaesthesia from trigeminal (V) involvement compounds the risk. Lagophthalmos with visual threat constitutes a WHO grade-2 (eye) disability; the optic and oculomotor nerves are not the mechanism.
Q_S_024 [E] According to the WHO operational classification (based on the number of skin lesions), a patient with 3 hypopigmented anaesthetic skin patches and a negative slit-skin smear is classified and treated as:
    A. Multibacillary leprosy - 12 months of MDT
   *B. Paucibacillary leprosy - 6 months of MDT

    D. Lepromatous leprosy - lifelong dapsone
   key=B ref=Neena Khanna, Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD; WHO leprosy guidelines (verified 2026-07-04)
   concept: WHO operational classification: paucibacillary leprosy = 1-5 skin lesions (smear-negative), treated with 6 months of rifampicin + dapsone; multibacillary leprosy = more than 5 lesions (or a positive skin smear), treated with 12 months of rifampicin + dapsone + clofazimine.
Q_S_025 [M] A patient with borderline leprosy on multidrug therapy develops, over a few days, acute painful swelling and redness of existing skin lesions together with sudden nerve pain, tenderness and a new nerve deficit. This is best described as:
    A. Type 2 reaction (erythema nodosum leprosum)
   *B. Type 1 (reversal) reaction
    C. Lucio phenomenon
    D. Dapsone hypersensitivity syndrome
   key=B ref=Neena Khanna, Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD
   concept: A type 1 (reversal) reaction is a type IV cell-mediated reaction seen in borderline leprosy: existing lesions become acutely inflamed and nerves become tender with sudden deficit (neuritis) - a medical emergency treated with corticosteroids. Type 2 reaction (ENL) is a type III immune-complex phenomenon with crops of tender nodules and systemic upset, seen in lepromatous/borderline-lepromatous disease.

=== Dermatology / Pemphigus vs pemphigoid; STDs
Q_S_028 [H] A 50-year-old presents with flaccid blisters and painful oral erosions; lateral pressure on normal-looking skin shears off the epidermis (Nikolsky's sign positive). Which set of findings correctly fits pemphigus vulgaris (rather than bullous pemphigoid)?
    A. Subepidermal blister, Nikolsky negative, IgG against BP180/BP230
   *B. Intraepidermal (suprabasal) blister, Nikolsky positive, IgG against desmoglein 3
    C. Subepidermal blister, linear IgA at the basement membrane
    D. Intraepidermal blister, Nikolsky negative, no autoantibodies
   key=B ref=Neena Khanna, Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD
   concept: Pemphigus vulgaris: intraepidermal (suprabasal) acantholytic blister, flaccid bullae, Nikolsky positive, autoantibodies against desmoglein 3 (with a 'fish-net' intercellular IgG pattern on direct immunofluorescence). Bullous pemphigoid: tense subepidermal blisters, Nikolsky negative, antibodies against hemidesmosomal BP180/BP230 with linear IgG/C3 along the basement membrane.
Q_S_029 [E] A young man presents about 3 weeks after unprotected intercourse with a single, painless, indurated genital ulcer that has a clean base, together with non-tender inguinal lymphadenopathy. The most likely diagnosis is:
    A. Chancroid (Haemophilus ducreyi)
   *B. Primary syphilis (Treponema pallidum) - a hard chancre
    C. Genital herpes (HSV)
    D. Lymphogranuloma venereum
   key=B ref=Neena Khanna, Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD
   concept: Primary syphilis presents as a solitary, painless, indurated ulcer with a clean base (the hard chancre) plus non-tender ('shotty') lymphadenopathy, roughly 3 weeks after exposure. Chancroid is painful with a ragged, undermined edge and tender suppurative nodes; herpes causes grouped painful vesicles and ulcers.

=== Dermatology / Psoriasis, lichen planus
Q_GEN_DERM_PAPULOSQ_001 [E] Which of the following is the single most common clinical type of psoriasis, presenting as symmetrical, well-demarcated erythematous plaques with adherent silvery scale over the elbows, knees, scalp and lumbosacral region?
    A. Guttate psoriasis
    B. Generalized pustular psoriasis
   *C. Chronic plaque psoriasis (psoriasis vulgaris)
    D. Erythrodermic psoriasis
   key=C ref=Neena Khanna, Illustrated Synopsis of Dermatology & STD, 6th ed. — Papulosquamous Disorders (Psoriasis)
   concept: Chronic plaque psoriasis (psoriasis vulgaris) accounts for roughly 80-90% of cases and is the classic presentation: symmetrical, well-demarcated erythematous plaques with adherent silvery-white scale over extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp and lumbosacral area. Guttate, generalized pustular and erythrodermic forms are far less common and are distinguished by their morphology and acuity, making chronic plaque the single most common type.
