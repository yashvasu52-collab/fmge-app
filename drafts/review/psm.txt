
=== PSM (Community Medicine) / Biostatistics: tests & distributions
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_001 [E] In hospital records, patients are grouped by ABO blood group (A, B, AB, O). What scale of measurement does this variable represent?
    A. Ordinal, because the categories can be meaningfully ranked from low to high
    B. Ratio, because it has a true zero and permits meaningful ratios
   *C. Nominal, because the categories are merely labels with no inherent order
    D. Interval, because equal differences between categories are meaningful
   key=C ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Biostatistics: types of data
   concept: Blood group is a qualitative categorical variable whose categories (A, B, AB, O) are names/labels with no natural order or magnitude, so it is nominal. Ordinal data require a meaningful rank order (e.g., disease stage); interval data have ordered categories with equal, meaningful gaps but no true zero (e.g., temperature in Celsius); ratio data add a true zero permitting ratios (e.g., weight). None of these applies to blood group, hence nominal.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_002 [M] Cancer is commonly recorded using TNM stages I, II, III and IV. Which type of data does the stage variable represent?
    A. Nominal data, since the stages are unordered categories
   *B. Ordinal data, since the categories have a natural rank order but unequal or undefined intervals
    C. Continuous ratio data, since stage IV is exactly twice stage II
    D. Discrete interval data with equal spacing between the stages
   key=B ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — types of data and scales of measurement
   concept: TNM stage has a meaningful order (I < II < III < IV reflecting increasing anatomical extent), which makes it ordinal; but the 'distance' between consecutive stages is neither equal nor quantifiable, so it is not interval or ratio, and stage IV is not '2x stage II'. Being ordered, it is not nominal. Ordinal data are best summarized by the median and analysed with non-parametric tests rather than means and t-tests.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_003 [E] Household monthly incomes in a community are highly right-skewed by a few very wealthy families. Which measure of central tendency best represents the 'typical' income?
   *A. Median, because it is not distorted by extreme high values
    B. Mean, because it uses every observation and is therefore most accurate here
    C. Mode, because income is a continuous variable
    D. Range, because it captures the spread of incomes
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — measures of central tendency
   concept: In a skewed distribution the mean is pulled toward the long tail (here inflated by a few very high incomes), so it overstates the 'typical' value. The median (the 50th percentile) is resistant to extreme values and best reflects the centre of skewed data, which is why income is usually reported as a median. Mode is unreliable for continuous income, and range is a measure of dispersion, not central tendency.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_004 [H] Serum cholesterol has mean 200 mg/dL with SD 30 mg/dL; body weight in the same group has mean 50 kg with SD 5 kg. Which variable shows greater relative variability?
    A. Body weight, because its absolute SD is proportionately closer to its mean value
    B. They are equal, because the coefficient of variation ignores the mean
    C. Cannot be compared because the units (mg/dL vs kg) differ
   *D. Cholesterol, because its coefficient of variation (15%) exceeds that of weight (10%)
   key=D ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — coefficient of variation
   concept: To compare spread across variables with different units or means, use the coefficient of variation, CV = (SD/mean) x 100, which is unitless. Cholesterol CV = 30/200 x 100 = 15%; weight CV = 5/50 x 100 = 10%; therefore cholesterol is relatively more variable. Absolute SDs (30 vs 5) cannot be compared across different units, which is precisely why the CV is needed; the CV explicitly uses the mean, so it is not 'ignored'.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_005 [E] Assertion (A): In a normal distribution the mean, median and mode are equal. Reason (R): A normal distribution is perfectly symmetrical (bell-shaped) about its mean.
   *A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true, but R is false
    D. A is false, but R is true
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — the normal distribution and its properties
   concept: Both statements are true and the reason correctly explains the assertion. A normal distribution is perfectly symmetrical about its mean; this symmetry forces the mean, median and mode to fall at the same central point, so mean = median = mode. In a skewed distribution the symmetry is lost and these three measures separate (the mean is dragged toward the tail). Hence symmetry is the direct cause of their coincidence, so R explains A.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_006 [M] Birth weights in a population are normally distributed with mean 3.0 kg and SD 0.4 kg. Approximately 68% of newborns weigh between which limits?
    A. 2.2 kg and 3.8 kg
   *B. 2.6 kg and 3.4 kg
    C. 1.8 kg and 4.2 kg
    D. 2.9 kg and 3.1 kg
   key=B ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — normal distribution and the empirical rule
   concept: By the empirical rule, about 68% of values in a normal distribution lie within plus or minus 1 SD of the mean. Here mean +/- 1 SD = 3.0 +/- 0.4 = 2.6 to 3.4 kg. The interval 2.2-3.8 kg is mean +/- 2 SD (about 95%), and 1.8-4.2 kg is mean +/- 3 SD (about 99.7%); 2.9-3.1 kg is far narrower than 1 SD and would capture far less than 68% of newborns.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_007 [M] In a population, serum cholesterol is normally distributed with mean 200 mg/dL and SD 25 mg/dL. A patient's value is 250 mg/dL. What is the standard normal (Z) score, and what does it indicate?
   *A. Z = +2.0; the value lies 2 SD above the mean
    B. Z = +2.5; the value lies 2.5 SD above the mean
    C. Z = +50; the value is 50 units above the mean
    D. Z = +0.5; the value is half an SD above the mean
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — standard normal deviate (Z score)
   concept: Z = (x - mean)/SD = (250 - 200)/25 = 50/25 = +2.0, meaning the observation lies exactly 2 standard deviations above the mean. Z = 2.5 would require SD = 20; Z = 50 forgets to divide by the SD; Z = 0.5 divides incorrectly. A Z of +2 corresponds to roughly the 97.7th percentile, so only about 2.3% of the population would have a higher cholesterol than this patient.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_008 [H] A student scores 70 in Anatomy (class mean 60, SD 5) and 80 in Physiology (class mean 70, SD 10). Relative to classmates, in which subject did the student perform better?
    A. Physiology, because the raw score of 80 is higher than 70
    B. Equal, because the student was 10 marks above the mean in both subjects
    C. Physiology, because its larger SD indicates a tougher paper
   *D. Anatomy, because its Z-score (+2.0) exceeds that of Physiology (+1.0)
   key=D ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — Z scores and comparison across distributions
   concept: Standardizing removes scale differences: Anatomy Z = (70 - 60)/5 = +2.0; Physiology Z = (80 - 70)/10 = +1.0. Although the raw Physiology mark (80) is higher and the absolute deviation is 10 in both subjects, the student is 2 SD above the mean in Anatomy versus only 1 SD in Physiology, so the relative standing is better in Anatomy. Comparing raw marks or absolute deviations across different distributions is misleading; Z scores make them comparable.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_009 [M] A 2-year-old boy's weight is plotted on a growth chart and falls exactly on the 25th percentile. What does this mean?
    A. The child weighs 25% of the expected weight for his age
    B. 25% of children of the same age and sex weigh more than this boy
   *C. 75% of children of the same age and sex weigh more than this boy
    D. The child's weight is 25 units below the population mean
   key=C ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — percentiles and growth monitoring
   concept: A percentile gives the position of a value within a distribution: the 25th percentile means 25% of the reference population lies at or below this weight, and therefore 75% lie above it. It is not '25% of expected weight' (that confuses a percentile with a percentage of a target), and it does not state a fixed number of units from the mean. On growth charts, tracking steadily along one percentile is generally more reassuring than the absolute percentile itself.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_010 [M] Assertion (A): The standard error of the mean (SEM) measures the scatter of individual observations within a sample. Reason (R): The SEM is calculated as the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true, but R is false
   *D. A is false, but R is true
   key=D ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — standard deviation vs standard error of the mean
   concept: The assertion is false but the reason is true. The SEM does not describe the scatter of individual observations — that is the role of the standard deviation (SD). The SEM (= SD divided by the square root of n) instead describes the precision of the sample mean as an estimate of the population mean, and it shrinks as sample size rises. The formula SEM = SD/sqrt(n) is itself correct, so R is true while A misattributes the meaning of the SEM.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_011 [H] A sample of 100 adults has a mean fasting glucose of 95 mg/dL with a standard deviation of 20 mg/dL. What is the standard error of the mean?
   *A. 2 mg/dL
    B. 20 mg/dL
    C. 0.2 mg/dL
    D. 10 mg/dL
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — standard error of the mean
   concept: SEM = SD / sqrt(n) = 20 / sqrt(100) = 20/10 = 2 mg/dL. The value 20 is the SD (the spread of individuals, not of the mean); 0.2 wrongly divides by n instead of sqrt(n); 10 arbitrarily halves the SD. Because the SEM shrinks with the square root of n, quadrupling the sample size to 400 would halve the SEM to 1 mg/dL, tightening the confidence interval around the estimated mean.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_012 [H] In a large sample the mean diastolic blood pressure is 80 mmHg with a standard error of the mean of 2.5 mmHg. What is the approximate 95% confidence interval for the true mean?
    A. 77.5 to 82.5 mmHg
    B. 70.0 to 90.0 mmHg
   *C. 75.1 to 84.9 mmHg
    D. 80 plus or minus 2.5, i.e., the SEM itself is the interval
   key=C ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — confidence interval for a mean
   concept: A 95% CI for a mean = sample mean +/- 1.96 x SEM = 80 +/- 1.96 x 2.5 = 80 +/- 4.9 = 75.1 to 84.9 mmHg. Option A uses only 1 SEM (about a 68% interval, the wrong multiplier); option B uses roughly 4 SEM, far too wide; option D confuses the SEM with the interval itself. Interpretation: we are 95% confident the true population mean diastolic BP lies within 75.1-84.9 mmHg.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_013 [E] In hypothesis testing, what does the null hypothesis (H0) typically state?
    A. That the new treatment is superior to the standard treatment
   *B. That there is no difference or no association between the groups being compared
    C. That the study has adequate power to detect a difference
    D. That the observed result reflects a real effect rather than chance
   key=B ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — tests of significance, null hypothesis
   concept: The null hypothesis (H0) is the hypothesis of no difference, no effect, or no association — for example, that two treatments are equally effective. The alternative hypothesis (H1) states that a difference or association does exist (options A and D describe H1). Statistical tests assess the evidence against H0; power is a property of the study design, not a hypothesis. We either reject or fail to reject H0, and we never 'prove' it true.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_014 [M] A trial concludes that a drug works when in reality it has no effect. This error, and the probability of committing it, are termed:
    A. Type II error; probability denoted by beta
    B. Random error; probability given by the p-value
    C. Systematic error; probability equal to 1 minus alpha
   *D. Type I error; probability denoted by alpha
   key=D ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — Type I and Type II errors
   concept: Rejecting a true null hypothesis (concluding an effect exists when it does not) is a Type I, or false-positive, error, whose probability is alpha — conventionally set at 0.05. A Type II error (beta) is the opposite: failing to detect a real effect (false negative). Alpha is the significance level chosen in advance; the p-value is the observed probability compared against alpha, not the definition of the error. Type I/II errors are distinct from systematic (bias) error.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_015 [H] Assertion (A): Increasing the sample size increases the statistical power of a study. Reason (R): A larger sample size lowers alpha, the Type I error rate, below the chosen significance level.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
   *C. A is true, but R is false
    D. A is false, but R is true
   key=C ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — power of a study, sample size
   concept: The assertion is true but the reason is false. Increasing the sample size does raise statistical power, but it does so by reducing the standard error and hence the Type II error rate (beta), not by lowering alpha. Alpha (the significance level, e.g., 0.05) is fixed a priori by the investigator and is unchanged by sample size. Power = 1 - beta; larger n, a larger effect size and lower variability all increase power, so A is true while R is false.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_016 [M] In a study comparing two means the p-value is 0.03 with alpha set at 0.05. Which interpretation is correct?
    A. There is a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true
    B. The result is not statistically significant, so we accept the null hypothesis
   *C. If the null hypothesis were true, a result at least as extreme would occur 3% of the time; since 0.03 < 0.05 we reject the null
    D. There is a 97% probability that the alternative hypothesis is true
   key=C ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — p-value and level of significance
   concept: The p-value is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as that observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. It is NOT the probability that H0 or H1 is true — options A and D commit this common misinterpretation. Since 0.03 is less than alpha 0.05, the result is statistically significant and we reject H0 (option B states the opposite). A small p-value shows the data are unlikely under H0 but says nothing about the size of the effect.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_017 [M] Systolic blood pressure is measured in the same 30 patients before and after 8 weeks of a new drug; the values are approximately normally distributed. Which test best compares the before and after means?
    A. Unpaired (independent-samples) t-test
   *B. Paired t-test
    C. Chi-square test of independence
    D. One-way ANOVA
   key=B ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — paired vs unpaired t-test
   concept: The two measurements come from the SAME individuals (before vs after), so the observations are paired/related; with approximately normal, continuous data the correct parametric test is the paired t-test, which analyses the mean of the within-person differences. An unpaired t-test assumes two independent groups. Chi-square is for categorical data, and one-way ANOVA compares three or more independent groups. For non-normal paired data the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is the non-parametric alternative.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_018 [H] Researchers compare serum bilirubin between two independent groups of patients. The data are markedly right-skewed and the samples are small. Which test is most appropriate?
    A. Paired t-test on the raw values
    B. One-way ANOVA
    C. Pearson correlation coefficient
   *D. Mann-Whitney U (Wilcoxon rank-sum) test
   key=D ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — non-parametric tests, Mann-Whitney U
   concept: With two INDEPENDENT groups, continuous but markedly skewed (non-normal) data, and small samples, the parametric t-test's normality assumption fails, so the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (equivalently the Wilcoxon rank-sum test), which compares distributions using ranks, is appropriate. A paired t-test requires matched observations (not the case here); ANOVA compares three or more groups and assumes normality; Pearson correlation measures association between two variables, not a between-group difference.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_019 [H] A 2x2 table relates exposure to outcome, but the total sample is only 18 and several expected cell counts are below 5. Which test of association is most appropriate?
   *A. Fisher's exact test
    B. Chi-square test without any correction
    C. Unpaired t-test
    D. Pearson correlation coefficient
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — chi-square test and Fisher's exact test
   concept: The chi-square test is unreliable when expected cell counts are small (a common rule: more than 20% of cells with expected count under 5, or any expected count under 1). With such small numbers in a 2x2 table, Fisher's exact test, which computes the exact probability, is preferred. An uncorrected chi-square would be invalid here (Yates' correction is only a partial fix). A t-test compares means of continuous data and Pearson correlation measures linear association between two continuous variables — neither suits a 2x2 categorical association.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_020 [M] An investigator wants to quantify the linear relationship between height and weight, both continuous and approximately normally distributed, in 200 adults. Which statistic is most appropriate, and what is its possible range?
    A. Chi-square statistic, ranging from 0 to infinity
    B. Relative risk, ranging from 0 to infinity
   *C. Pearson correlation coefficient (r), ranging from -1 to +1
    D. Spearman rank correlation, appropriate here only because the data are normal
   key=C ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — correlation coefficient (Pearson)
   concept: For the linear association between two continuous, approximately normal variables, the Pearson correlation coefficient r is appropriate; r ranges from -1 (perfect negative) through 0 (no linear relationship) to +1 (perfect positive). Spearman's rho is the rank-based choice for ordinal or non-normal/monotonic data — it is NOT preferred simply because the data are normal (option D is self-contradictory). Chi-square (categorical data) and relative risk (a risk ratio) are not measures of linear correlation.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_021 [M] A researcher wishes to assess the association between two ordinal variables — pain severity graded 1-4 and a 1-5 patient-satisfaction rating. Which correlation coefficient is most appropriate?
    A. Pearson correlation coefficient
   *B. Spearman rank correlation coefficient
    C. Odds ratio
    D. Coefficient of variation
   key=B ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Spearman rank correlation
   concept: For ordinal (ranked) data — or for continuous data that are non-normal or related in a monotonic but non-linear way — the non-parametric Spearman rank correlation coefficient (rho) is appropriate, as it operates on ranks rather than raw values. Pearson's r assumes continuous, normally distributed, linearly related variables, so it is unsuitable for graded scales. The odds ratio measures association between binary variables, and the coefficient of variation is a measure of relative dispersion, not correlation.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_022 [H] In a regression of blood pressure on age, the Pearson correlation coefficient is r = 0.6. What proportion of the variation in blood pressure is explained by age?
    A. 60%, because r directly gives the explained variation
    B. 80%, because it equals 1 minus r
    C. 40%, because unexplained variation equals r
   *D. 36%, because the coefficient of determination r-squared = 0.36
   key=D ref=Mahajan's Methods in Biostatistics — coefficient of determination (r-squared) and regression
   concept: The coefficient of determination is r-squared, the fraction of the variance in the outcome explained by the predictor: r-squared = 0.6 x 0.6 = 0.36, i.e., 36% of the variation in blood pressure is explained by age (the remaining 64% is due to other factors and error). r itself (0.6) measures the strength and direction of the linear association but overstates 'explained variation'; the '1 minus r' and 'r as unexplained variation' options misuse the relationship. Squaring makes r-squared <= |r| when |r| < 1.
Q_GEN_PSM_BIOSTAT_023 [E] Which statement correctly distinguishes incidence from prevalence?
   *A. Incidence measures NEW cases arising over a period; prevalence measures ALL existing (old plus new) cases at a point or over a period
    B. Incidence measures existing cases, while prevalence measures only new cases
    C. Incidence and prevalence are always numerically identical for chronic diseases
    D. Prevalence is a rate with time in its denominator, whereas incidence is a static proportion with no time element
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — incidence and prevalence
   concept: Incidence quantifies the number of NEW cases developing in a defined population over a specified time period (a measure of risk or rate of occurrence). Prevalence counts ALL cases existing (old plus new) at a given point (point prevalence) or over an interval (period prevalence), reflecting disease burden. Prevalence is approximately incidence x average duration, so for a long-duration chronic disease prevalence exceeds incidence — they are not identical. Option D reverses the time relationship: it is incidence that inherently carries a time dimension.

=== PSM (Community Medicine) / Demography, family planning, MCH indicators
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_001 [M] Assertion (A): India is currently placed in the "late expanding" stage of the demographic cycle. Reason (R): In the late expanding stage, both the birth rate and the death rate remain persistently high, producing a stable population.
    A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
   *C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=C ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning
   concept: Park describes five stages of the demographic cycle: high stationary (high birth + high death), early expanding (death falls, birth stays high), late expanding (birth begins to fall, death low — India is here), low stationary (both low), and declining (death exceeds birth). The Reason wrongly describes the FIRST (high stationary) stage; in late expanding the death rate is already low while the birth rate is declining, so A is true but R is false. India's stage placement is change-prone as fertility approaches replacement — verify current TFR against latest NFHS/SRS.
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_002 [E] A population age-sex pyramid with a very broad base that tapers steeply towards the apex is MOST characteristic of which type of population?
    A. A constrictive (contracting) population with a low birth rate
   *B. An expansive population with a high birth rate and a young age structure
    C. A stationary population where births roughly equal deaths
    D. An ageing population with a high proportion of the elderly
   key=B ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning
   concept: The shape of a population pyramid encodes fertility and mortality history. A broad-based, steeply tapering (triangular) pyramid signals an expansive population: a high birth rate feeds large young cohorts at the base while higher mortality trims the older bars — typical of developing/high-fertility settings. A constrictive pyramid narrows at the base (declining births), and a stationary pyramid has near-vertical sides with a rounded top. Recognising the young base is key to understanding the demographic dividend, in which a bulge of working-age adults can boost the economy if productively employed.
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_003 [E] The population census in India, which enumerates the entire population, is conducted at what interval?
    A. Every 5 years (quinquennial)
   *B. Every 10 years (decennial)
    C. Every 7 years
    D. Every year (annual)
   key=B ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning
   concept: The census is a complete, direct, individual and periodic enumeration of the whole population; in India it has been carried out decennially (every 10 years) without interruption since 1881, most recently in 2011. It is distinct from the vital-statistics system: the Civil Registration System (CRS) records births and deaths continuously, while the Sample Registration System (SRS) estimates vital rates on a sample basis between censuses. The scheduled census has been change-prone recently (the 2021 round was delayed) — verify the latest completed census year before quoting figures.
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_004 [M] What is the PRIMARY function of the Sample Registration System (SRS) in India?
   *A. To provide reliable estimates of birth rate, death rate and infant mortality rate on a sample basis using a dual-record system
    B. To legally register every birth and death occurring in the country as a compulsory civil requirement
    C. To carry out the complete decennial enumeration of the entire population
    D. To register and follow up all eligible couples for family planning services
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning
   concept: The SRS is a large-scale, continuous demographic survey that yields dependable estimates of fertility and mortality indicators (CBR, CDR, IMR, MMR, TFR) at national and state level between censuses. It works on a DUAL-RECORD system: a resident part-time enumerator does continuous recording of vital events, and an independent half-yearly retrospective survey cross-checks these; discrepancies are field-verified. This differs from the Civil Registration System (compulsory legal registration of each event under the RBD Act) and from the census (complete enumeration). Reported SRS figures update annually — flag them as verify-latest.
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_005 [M] Total Fertility Rate (TFR) is BEST defined as:
    A. The number of live births per 1000 mid-year population in a given year
    B. The number of live births per 1000 women aged 15–49 years in a given year
    C. The average number of daughters a woman would bear during her reproductive life
   *D. The average number of children a woman would bear if she passed through her reproductive years (15–49) experiencing the current age-specific fertility rates
   key=D ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning
   concept: TFR is a synthetic cohort measure: it sums the age-specific fertility rates across the reproductive span and is read as the average number of children a woman would have if current fertility patterns persisted. It is superior to the crude birth rate (option A) because it removes the effect of population age-sex structure, and unlike the general fertility rate (option B, births per 1000 women 15–49) it captures completed fertility. Option C describes the gross reproduction rate (daughters only). A TFR of about 2.1 is the replacement level; India's current TFR is change-prone near replacement — verify against the latest NFHS/SRS.
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_006 [H] Which statement correctly distinguishes the Gross Reproduction Rate (GRR) from the Net Reproduction Rate (NRR)?
    A. GRR counts total children (both sexes); NRR counts only sons surviving to adulthood
    B. GRR accounts for female mortality before the end of the reproductive period, whereas NRR ignores it
   *C. GRR is the average number of daughters a woman would bear at current fertility, IGNORING mortality; NRR is the same but ADJUSTED for the mortality of females before they complete the reproductive period
    D. GRR and NRR are identical measures and the terms are interchangeable
   key=C ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning
   concept: Both are female-replacement measures counting DAUGHTERS. GRR is the average number of daughters a woman would have if she survived through her whole reproductive period at current age-specific fertility — it assumes NO mortality. NRR corrects this by factoring in the probability that a female child survives to and through the childbearing ages, so NRR is always ≤ GRR. NRR is the more realistic index of generational replacement; NRR = 1 means each generation of mothers is exactly replaced by an equal generation of daughters. Option B reverses the relationship, and option A confuses the sex counted (daughters, not sons).
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_007 [E] The Crude Birth Rate (CBR) is calculated as:
    A. Total live births in a year ÷ number of married women aged 15–45, × 1000
   *B. Number of live births during a year ÷ estimated mid-year population, × 1000
    C. Number of live births ÷ number of women aged 15–49, × 1000
    D. Number of live births ÷ total pregnancies in the year, × 100
   key=B ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning
   concept: CBR = (number of live births in a year / estimated mid-year population) × 1000. It is 'crude' because the denominator is the whole population, including men, children and the elderly who cannot give birth, so it is heavily influenced by age-sex composition and is a poor tool for comparing fertility across dissimilar populations. Option C describes the general fertility rate (denominator = women 15–49). For like-for-like comparison, age-specific fertility rates, GFR or TFR are preferred. India's CBR is a change-prone SRS figure — verify the latest release rather than quoting a fixed number.
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_008 [M] Assertion (A): The crude death rate is not a satisfactory measure for comparing mortality between two different countries. Reason (R): The crude death rate is strongly influenced by the age and sex composition of the population.
   *A. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    B. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    C. A is true but R is false
    D. A is false but R is true
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning
   concept: The crude death rate (deaths per 1000 mid-year population) mixes together populations with very different age structures: a country with many elderly people will show a high CDR even with excellent health services, while a youthful population may show a deceptively low CDR. Because age-sex composition drives the crude rate, direct comparison is misleading — hence standardization (direct or indirect) or age-specific/cause-specific rates are used for valid comparison. Here A is true and R is not only true but is the precise reason A holds, so both are true and R correctly explains A.
Q_GEN_PSM_DEMOG_FP_009 [H] In the DIRECT method of age-standardization of death rates, what is done?
   *A. The age-specific death rates of each study population are applied to a chosen standard population's age structure to yield comparable standardized rates
    B. The age-specific death rates of a standard population are applied to the age structure of each study population
    C. The crude death rates of both populations are simply averaged together
    D. Only the deaths in the 15–49 year age group are compared between the two populations
   key=A ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Demography and Family Planning

Q_GEN_PSM_COMMDIS_002 [E] In infectious-disease control, 'sterilization' is BEST defined as:

Q_GEN_PSM_COMMDIS_004 [E] As per current WHO status, which human infectious disease is the ONLY one to have been certified globally eradicated?

Q_GEN_PSM_COMMDIS_006 [M] The time interval between the onset of symptoms in a primary case and the onset of symptoms in a secondary case infected by that primary case is termed the:
    A. Incubation period
   *B. Serial interval
    C. Latent period
    D. Generation time
   key=B ref=Park's Textbook of Preventive and Social Medicine — Dynamics of Disease Transmission
   concept: The SERIAL INTERVAL is the time between symptom onset in a primary case and symptom onset in the secondary case(s) it infects; because it is directly observable, it is widely used to estimate transmission dynamics and to time quarantine. Q_GEN_PSM_COMMDIS_014 [M] A hospital reviews four documented HIV exposures. Which single exposure carries the HIGHEST per-exposure probability of HIV transmission?

   *B. Incidence
    C. Secondary attack rate

   key=B ref=Park's Textbook of PSM 27e, Epidemiology
