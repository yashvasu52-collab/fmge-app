@ Pathology ~ Endocrine pathology: thyroid, adrenal, pituitary, MEN ~ PAT_ENP ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. The Endocrine System
E ~ Which nuclear features characterise papillary thyroid carcinoma on histology? ~ Optically clear ground-glass nuclei with grooves and intranuclear pseudoinclusions, with psammoma bodies ~ Hyperchromatic nuclei with amyloid stroma ~ Small round nuclei with follicles and capsular invasion ~ Pleomorphic giant cells ~ A ~ The diagnosis rests on these nuclear features rather than on papillae alone, and the tumour spreads by lymphatics yet carries an excellent prognosis.
M ~ Which combination defines multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A, and which gene is responsible? ~ Medullary thyroid carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma and parathyroid hyperplasia, caused by a germline RET mutation ~ Parathyroid, pituitary and pancreatic tumours from a MEN1 mutation ~ Medullary thyroid carcinoma with mucosal neuromas and a marfanoid habitus ~ Phaeochromocytoma with haemangioblastoma from a VHL mutation ~ A ~ Identifying a RET mutation permits prophylactic thyroidectomy in childhood, and phaeochromocytoma must always be excluded before any surgery to avoid an intraoperative hypertensive crisis.
M ~ Which adrenal lesion causes hypertension with hypokalaemia and a suppressed renin, and what is its typical appearance? ~ An aldosterone-producing cortical adenoma, small and golden-yellow on section ~ A phaeochromocytoma, brown and haemorrhagic ~ Adrenocortical carcinoma, large and necrotic ~ Adrenal metastasis ~ A ~ Bilateral idiopathic hyperplasia is now recognised as commoner than the classic Conn adenoma, and the distinction matters because hyperplasia is treated medically rather than surgically.
M ~ Which histological feature identifies a phaeochromocytoma, and what determines its malignancy? ~ Nests of chromaffin cells in a zellballen pattern, with malignancy defined only by metastasis rather than by histology ~ Amyloid stroma, with malignancy defined by mitoses ~ Papillae, with malignancy defined by capsular invasion ~ Clear cells, with malignancy defined by nuclear grade ~ A ~ Because no histological feature reliably predicts behaviour, all such tumours are followed long term, and about a quarter are associated with a germline mutation.
H ~ Which pituitary pathology causes hypopituitarism with an enlarged sella and a raised prolactin that is only modestly elevated? ~ A non-functioning macroadenoma compressing the stalk, producing the stalk section effect ~ A prolactinoma secreting autonomously ~ Sheehan syndrome ~ Lymphocytic hypophysitis ~ A ~ A prolactin of a few thousand indicates a true prolactinoma whereas a level of 50 to 100 with a large mass indicates compression, and the distinction determines whether a dopamine agonist or surgery is appropriate.
H ~ Which thyroid pathology is associated with a raised serum calcitonin and amyloid deposition in the stroma? ~ Medullary carcinoma, arising from neural crest-derived parafollicular C cells ~ Papillary carcinoma ~ Follicular carcinoma ~ Anaplastic carcinoma ~ A ~ The amyloid is derived from procalcitonin, the tumour does not take up radioiodine because it is not of follicular origin, and carcinoembryonic antigen with calcitonin is used for follow-up.
@ Pathology ~ GIT: PUD, IBD, colorectal carcinoma, malabsorption ~ PAT_GIT ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. The Gastrointestinal Tract
E ~ Which sequence describes the commonest molecular pathway to sporadic colorectal carcinoma? ~ The chromosomal instability pathway with APC loss, then KRAS activation and TP53 loss ~ The microsatellite instability pathway with mismatch repair loss ~ The serrated pathway with BRAF mutation ~ Direct TP53 loss alone ~ A ~ Familial adenomatous polyposis is the germline model of this pathway, whereas Lynch syndrome follows the mismatch repair route and tends to produce right-sided, poorly differentiated, mucinous tumours.
M ~ Which pathological features distinguish Crohn disease from ulcerative colitis? ~ Transmural inflammation with skip lesions, non-caseating granulomas, fissures and fistulae ~ Continuous mucosal inflammation from the rectum with crypt abscesses ~ Pseudopolyps with backwash ileitis ~ Toxic megacolon with mucosal ulceration ~ A ~ Because Crohn disease affects the full thickness it produces strictures and fistulae, whereas ulcerative colitis remains mucosal so it produces bleeding, pseudopolyps and a higher risk of dysplasia in long-standing disease.
M ~ Which histological finding in a duodenal biopsy indicates coeliac disease? ~ Villous atrophy with crypt hyperplasia and increased intraepithelial lymphocytes ~ Granulomas in the lamina propria ~ Periodic acid-Schiff-positive macrophages ~ Signet ring cells ~ A ~ Periodic acid-Schiff-positive macrophages indicate Whipple disease, and the changes of coeliac disease are graded by the Marsh classification and reverse on a gluten-free diet.
M ~ Which gastric pathology carries the greatest risk of gastric adenocarcinoma? ~ Chronic atrophic gastritis with intestinal metaplasia, usually from Helicobacter pylori ~ Acute erosive gastritis ~ Zollinger-Ellison syndrome ~ Duodenal ulcer disease ~ A ~ Duodenal ulcer disease with high acid output is actually associated with a reduced gastric cancer risk, whereas the atrophic corpus-predominant pattern with hypochlorhydria is the high-risk phenotype.
H ~ Which polyp type carries essentially no malignant potential yet may be numerous in a syndrome associated with cancer elsewhere? ~ Hamartomatous polyps of Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, where the cancer risk arises from other organs and from associated adenomas ~ Tubular adenoma ~ Villous adenoma ~ Sessile serrated lesion ~ A ~ Understanding which polyps are precursors and which are markers determines surveillance strategy, and juvenile polyposis and Cowden syndrome pose comparable interpretive problems.
H ~ Why does a right-sided colonic carcinoma present differently from a left-sided one? ~ The wider caecal lumen allows exophytic growth with occult bleeding and anaemia, whereas the narrower left colon produces annular obstruction and altered bowel habit ~ Right-sided tumours obstruct earlier ~ Left-sided tumours bleed more occultly ~ Both present identically ~ A ~ This anatomical difference, together with distinct molecular pathways, explains why an unexplained iron deficiency anaemia in an older adult demands colonic investigation.
@ Pathology ~ Genetic & chromosomal disorders ~ PAT_GEN ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Genetic Disorders
E ~ Which mechanism explains the variable severity of a mitochondrial disorder within one family? ~ Heteroplasmy, with the proportion of mutant mitochondrial genomes varying between cells and tissues ~ Genomic imprinting ~ Anticipation ~ Locus heterogeneity ~ A ~ Mitochondrial DNA is maternally inherited so affected mothers transmit to all children, but the threshold effect of mutant load explains why phenotype varies so widely.
M ~ Which phenomenon explains why Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes result from the same chromosomal deletion? ~ Genomic imprinting, with the phenotype depending on whether the deleted 15q11-13 region is of paternal or maternal origin ~ Anticipation ~ Mosaicism ~ Uniparental disomy alone ~ A ~ Paternal deletion or maternal uniparental disomy gives Prader-Willi, and maternal deletion or paternal disomy gives Angelman, which is why methylation testing rather than karyotype alone is used.
M ~ Which molecular mechanism accounts for the increasing severity of myotonic dystrophy in successive generations? ~ Anticipation from expansion of a CTG trinucleotide repeat during transmission ~ Imprinting ~ Mosaicism ~ Gene conversion ~ A ~ The same phenomenon occurs in Huntington disease and fragile X syndrome, and the repeat length correlates with age of onset and severity.
M ~ Which finding on a karyotype indicates a Robertsonian translocation as the cause of Down syndrome, and why does it matter? ~ Fusion of the long arms of chromosome 21 with another acrocentric chromosome, because a parent may be a balanced carrier with a high recurrence risk ~ Trisomy 21 from meiotic non-disjunction ~ Mosaicism for trisomy 21 ~ An isochromosome ~ A ~ About 4 percent of cases are translocation-related, and identifying a carrier parent transforms the recurrence risk from about 1 percent to as high as 15 percent, which changes counselling entirely.
H ~ Which mechanism explains why a female heterozygous for an X-linked disorder may be symptomatic? ~ Skewed X inactivation, so the mutant allele is active in a disproportionate share of cells ~ Genomic imprinting ~ Anticipation ~ Complete penetrance ~ A ~ Lyonisation is random so most carriers are mosaic and asymptomatic, but skewing explains symptomatic carriers of Duchenne dystrophy, ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency and Fabry disease.
H ~ Why does a mutation in a gene of a lysosomal enzyme produce a progressive storage disease rather than an acute presentation? ~ Undegraded substrate accumulates gradually within lysosomes until cellular function is compromised ~ The enzyme is required only in adulthood ~ Substrate is toxic immediately ~ Compensation is impossible from birth ~ A ~ The gradual accumulation explains the progressive course with a symptom-free interval and the correlation between residual enzyme activity and age of onset, and it is the rationale for enzyme replacement.
@ Pathology ~ Granulomatous disease & TB pathology ~ PAT_GRN ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Inflammation and Infectious Disease
E ~ Which cell is the essential component of a granuloma, and what cytokine drives its formation? ~ The epithelioid macrophage, driven by interferon-gamma from Th1 lymphocytes ~ The neutrophil, driven by interleukin-8 ~ The plasma cell, driven by interleukin-6 ~ The eosinophil, driven by interleukin-5 ~ A ~ Tumour necrosis factor maintains granuloma integrity, which is why its blockade reactivates latent tuberculosis and why screening precedes such therapy.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes the granulomas of sarcoidosis from those of tuberculosis? ~ Non-caseating granulomas with no organisms in sarcoidosis, versus caseating granulomas with acid-fast bacilli in tuberculosis ~ Presence of giant cells in sarcoidosis only ~ Absence of lymphocytes in tuberculosis ~ Necrosis in sarcoidosis only ~ A ~ Because sarcoidosis is a diagnosis of exclusion, special stains and culture must be negative, and asteroid and Schaumann bodies are supportive but not specific.
M ~ Which pathological sequence describes primary pulmonary tuberculosis? ~ A subpleural parenchymal focus with hilar node involvement forming the Ghon complex, which usually heals by fibrosis and calcification ~ Apical cavitation with bronchial spread ~ Miliary dissemination in all cases ~ Pleural effusion without parenchymal disease ~ A ~ Post-primary disease instead affects the apical and posterior segments where oxygen tension is highest, with cavitation from liquefaction of caseous material.
M ~ Which structure is characteristic of caseous necrosis on histology? ~ Amorphous granular eosinophilic debris with loss of cellular outline, enclosed by a granulomatous rim ~ Preserved cellular architecture with coagulated protein ~ Liquefied tissue with neutrophils ~ Bright eosinophilic vessel wall change ~ A ~ The combination of coagulative and liquefactive change reflects the lipid-rich mycobacterial wall, and the cheesy gross appearance gives the name.
H ~ Why does miliary tuberculosis produce nodules of uniform size throughout both lungs? ~ Simultaneous haematogenous seeding disseminates organisms to all regions at once, so all lesions are of the same age ~ Lymphatic spread produces uniform nodules ~ Bronchial spread is uniform ~ Nodules coalesce over time ~ A ~ The uniformity distinguishes it radiologically from bronchogenic spread, where lesions differ in age and size, and it reflects the moment of vascular breach.
H ~ Why can a granulomatous reaction occur without an infectious agent, and what is the mechanism? ~ Persistent poorly degradable material or a hypersensitivity reaction sustains macrophage activation, as with foreign bodies, berylliosis and Crohn disease ~ Granulomas always indicate infection ~ Macrophages require bacterial antigen ~ Only mycobacteria can cause granulomas ~ A ~ Foreign body granulomas differ in lacking the T cell-mediated component and in showing the material within giant cells under polarised light, which is why polarisation should always be attempted.
@ Pathology ~ Haemodynamics: oedema, thrombosis, embolism, infarction, shock ~ PAT_HDN ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Hemodynamic Disorders
E ~ Which components of Virchow's triad predispose to venous thrombosis? ~ Endothelial injury, stasis or turbulent flow, and hypercoagulability ~ Hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and smoking ~ Anaemia, leucocytosis and thrombocytosis ~ Acidosis, hypoxia and hypercapnia ~ A ~ Venous thrombi arise mainly from stasis and hypercoagulability whereas arterial thrombi arise mainly from endothelial injury, which is why anticoagulants suit one and antiplatelets the other.
M ~ Which feature distinguishes an antemortem thrombus from a postmortem clot at autopsy? ~ Lines of Zahn with attachment to the vessel wall in an antemortem thrombus ~ A gelatinous chicken-fat appearance ~ Absence of attachment ~ Uniform dark red colour throughout ~ A ~ The alternating pale platelet and fibrin layers with darker red cell layers indicate flowing blood at the time of formation, which is the pathological basis for distinguishing the two.
M ~ Which organ shows a haemorrhagic rather than a pale infarct, and why? ~ The lung, because of its dual blood supply and loose tissue permitting blood to seep into the infarcted area ~ The kidney, because it is highly vascular ~ The spleen, because of arterial supply ~ The heart, because of collaterals ~ A ~ The intestine also produces haemorrhagic infarcts for the same reason, whereas solid organs with a single arterial supply and dense tissue produce pale wedge-shaped infarcts.
M ~ Which type of embolism should be suspected in a patient who becomes confused with petechiae and hypoxia 24 to 72 hours after a long bone fracture? ~ Fat embolism syndrome ~ Pulmonary thromboembolism ~ Air embolism ~ Amniotic fluid embolism ~ A ~ The latency, the triad of respiratory, neurological and cutaneous features and the association with marrow-containing fractures distinguish it from thromboembolism, which usually occurs later and without petechiae.
H ~ Which type of shock is characterised by a high cardiac output with low systemic vascular resistance and warm peripheries? ~ Distributive shock, as in sepsis or anaphylaxis ~ Cardiogenic shock ~ Hypovolaemic shock ~ Obstructive shock ~ A ~ The other three types share a low output with high resistance and cold peripheries, so recognising the warm phase of septic shock avoids the error of withholding fluid and vasopressor.
H ~ Why does chronic left heart failure produce haemosiderin-laden macrophages in the sputum? ~ Raised pulmonary capillary pressure causes microhaemorrhage, and alveolar macrophages phagocytose the red cells, becoming heart failure cells ~ Iron is secreted by pneumocytes ~ It reflects pulmonary infarction ~ It indicates infection ~ A ~ The same process produces brown induration of the lung with fibrosis, and Prussian blue staining identifies the iron pigment within the macrophages.
@ Pathology ~ Hypersensitivity, autoimmunity, amyloidosis ~ PAT_HAA ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Diseases of the Immune System
E ~ Which type of hypersensitivity underlies the tuberculin skin reaction and contact dermatitis? ~ Type IV, delayed cell-mediated hypersensitivity ~ Type I, IgE mediated ~ Type II, antibody mediated cytotoxicity ~ Type III, immune complex mediated ~ A ~ Because it depends on sensitised T cells recruiting macrophages rather than on antibody, it peaks at 48 to 72 hours and cannot be transferred with serum.
M ~ Which mechanism of hypersensitivity explains Graves disease, and how does it differ from myasthenia gravis? ~ Type II with a stimulating antibody to the TSH receptor, whereas myasthenia has a blocking and destructive antibody to the acetylcholine receptor ~ Type III in both ~ Type IV in both ~ Type I in Graves disease ~ A ~ Both are antibody-mediated diseases of receptors, and grouping them by mechanism rather than by organ explains why plasma exchange helps both and why neonatal transient disease can occur in each.
M ~ Which pattern of amyloid deposition follows chronic inflammatory disease such as rheumatoid arthritis or tuberculosis? ~ AA amyloid derived from serum amyloid A, an acute phase protein ~ AL amyloid derived from immunoglobulin light chains ~ Beta-2 microglobulin amyloid ~ Transthyretin amyloid ~ A ~ Treating the underlying inflammation lowers serum amyloid A and can halt progression, which is why the distinction from AL amyloidosis of plasma cell dyscrasia matters therapeutically.
M ~ Which organ involvement in amyloidosis most often determines prognosis, and how is it detected? ~ Cardiac involvement, detected by echocardiography showing a thickened non-dilated ventricle with a restrictive pattern and raised natriuretic peptides ~ Renal involvement, detected by proteinuria ~ Hepatic involvement, detected by hepatomegaly ~ Neuropathy, detected by nerve conduction ~ A ~ Because cardiac amyloid carries the worst survival and is potentially treatable in transthyretin disease, its recognition drives both prognosis and access to specific therapy.
H ~ Which immunological mechanism explains why a single autoimmune disease can progressively involve more antigens over time? ~ Epitope spreading, in which tissue damage exposes new self antigens that recruit further immune responses ~ Molecular mimicry alone ~ Anergy ~ Clonal deletion ~ A ~ Combined with loss of regulatory T cell control and bystander activation, it explains why autoimmune disease tends to broaden and why early intervention may prevent that expansion.
H ~ Why does systemic lupus erythematosus produce such diverse organ involvement? ~ Circulating immune complexes deposit in many vascular beds, activating complement wherever they lodge ~ A single organ-specific antibody is responsible ~ It is a type IV reaction ~ It is caused by direct viral cytotoxicity ~ A ~ Complement consumption gives the low C3 and C4 of active disease, and immune complex deposition explains the glomerulonephritis, serositis, rash and arthritis together.
@ Pathology ~ Immunodeficiency, transplant rejection & HLA ~ PAT_IMM ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Diseases of the Immune System
E ~ Which primary immunodeficiency presents after 6 months of age with recurrent encapsulated bacterial infection and absent B cells? ~ X-linked agammaglobulinaemia from a BTK mutation ~ Severe combined immunodeficiency ~ Chronic granulomatous disease ~ DiGeorge syndrome ~ A ~ The delay reflects the protection of transplacental maternal IgG, and treatment is lifelong immunoglobulin replacement with prompt antibiotic therapy.
M ~ Which immunodeficiency is characterised by recurrent infection with catalase-positive organisms and an abnormal dihydrorhodamine test? ~ Chronic granulomatous disease from NADPH oxidase deficiency ~ Leukocyte adhesion deficiency ~ Chediak-Higashi syndrome ~ Myeloperoxidase deficiency ~ A ~ Phagocytes ingest but cannot kill, so granulomas form, and prophylaxis with cotrimoxazole, itraconazole and interferon-gamma reduces infection rates.
M ~ Which type of transplant rejection is mediated by pre-formed antibody and occurs within minutes of anastomosis? ~ Hyperacute rejection, with thrombosis and immediate graft loss ~ Acute cellular rejection ~ Chronic rejection ~ Graft versus host disease ~ A ~ Crossmatching to detect pre-formed donor-specific antibody is what prevents it, which is why ABO and HLA compatibility testing precede transplantation.
M ~ Which histological finding indicates acute cellular rejection of a renal allograft? ~ A mononuclear interstitial infiltrate with tubulitis and sometimes endarteritis ~ Interstitial fibrosis with tubular atrophy ~ Fibrinoid necrosis with neutrophil margination ~ Glomerular immune complex deposition ~ A ~ Chronic rejection instead shows fibrosis with vascular intimal thickening and responds poorly to increased immunosuppression, so the Banff classification distinguishes them.
H ~ Which HLA class presents antigen to CD8 T cells, and why is that relevant to viral infection? ~ Class I, expressed on all nucleated cells and presenting endogenously derived peptide to cytotoxic T cells ~ Class II, expressed on antigen presenting cells ~ Class III, encoding complement proteins ~ Both classes equally ~ A ~ Viruses replicating in any cell therefore present peptide on class I, which allows cytotoxic killing, and viral downregulation of class I is a common escape mechanism countered by natural killer cells.
H ~ Why does graft versus host disease occur after allogeneic bone marrow transplantation and not after solid organ transplantation? ~ Donor T cells in the graft recognise recipient tissue as foreign, and the recipient is immunosuppressed and cannot eliminate them ~ Recipient T cells attack the graft ~ Antibody mediates the reaction ~ It results from ABO incompatibility ~ A ~ Skin, gut and liver are the classic targets, and T cell depletion of the graft reduces the disease at the cost of a higher relapse rate because the graft versus leukaemia effect is also lost.
@ Pathology ~ Leukemias (acute/chronic) & basic classification ~ PAT_LAC ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Diseases of White Blood Cells
E ~ Which blast percentage in blood or marrow defines acute leukaemia in current classification? ~ Twenty percent or more blasts, or the presence of a defining genetic abnormality regardless of blast count ~ Five percent ~ Ten percent ~ Fifty percent ~ A ~ Certain recurrent abnormalities such as the t(15;17) or the core binding factor translocations are now diagnostic of acute myeloid leukaemia at any blast percentage, because their biology and treatment are defined by the genetics.
E ~ Which stain distinguishes myeloid from lymphoid blasts on cytochemistry? ~ Myeloperoxidase positivity indicates myeloid lineage while periodic acid-Schiff block positivity suggests lymphoid ~ Prussian blue ~ Congo red ~ Ziehl-Neelsen ~ A ~ Flow cytometric immunophenotyping has largely replaced cytochemistry, but the principle of assigning lineage before treatment remains, because the therapy differs completely.
M ~ Which chromosomal abnormality in acute myeloid leukaemia is associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation and responds to a differentiating agent? ~ The t(15;17) PML-RARA fusion of acute promyelocytic leukaemia, treated with all-trans retinoic acid ~ The t(8;21) fusion ~ Inversion 16 ~ The t(9;22) fusion ~ A ~ All-trans retinoic acid with arsenic trioxide has converted the most lethal subtype into the most curable, and treatment begins on clinical suspicion because early haemorrhage kills.
M ~ Which chronic leukaemia shows a markedly raised white cell count with the full spectrum of granulocytic maturation, a low leucocyte alkaline phosphatase and basophilia? ~ Chronic myeloid leukaemia with the BCR-ABL1 fusion ~ Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia ~ Hairy cell leukaemia ~ Myelofibrosis ~ A ~ The distinction from a leukaemoid reaction rests on the fusion gene, the basophilia and the low alkaline phosphatase score, and tyrosine kinase inhibition has transformed survival.
M ~ Which feature indicates transformation of chronic myeloid leukaemia to blast crisis? ~ Twenty percent or more blasts in blood or marrow with extramedullary disease and additional cytogenetic changes ~ Rising platelet count alone ~ Splenomegaly ~ Basophilia of 5 percent ~ A ~ Blast crisis may be myeloid or lymphoid, and its emergence despite tyrosine kinase inhibition usually reflects kinase domain mutations or clonal evolution.
M ~ Which chronic lymphoid neoplasm shows cells with cytoplasmic projections, a dry marrow tap and splenomegaly without lymphadenopathy? ~ Hairy cell leukaemia, with a BRAF V600E mutation and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase positivity ~ Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia ~ Mantle cell lymphoma ~ Prolymphocytic leukaemia ~ A ~ Marrow fibrosis explains the dry tap, and purine analogues such as cladribine produce prolonged remission, with BRAF inhibitors available for refractory disease.
H ~ Which complication of chronic lymphocytic leukaemia presents with rapidly enlarging nodes, fever and a rising lactate dehydrogenase? ~ Richter transformation to diffuse large B cell lymphoma ~ Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia ~ Hypogammaglobulinaemia ~ Marrow failure ~ A ~ Prognosis after transformation is poor, and it must be distinguished by biopsy from simple progression because the treatment approach differs entirely.
H ~ Why does a leukaemoid reaction need to be distinguished from chronic myeloid leukaemia, and how is this done? ~ Both raise the neutrophil count, but the leukaemoid reaction shows toxic granulation with a high alkaline phosphatase score and no BCR-ABL1 fusion ~ Only the blast count differs ~ Splenomegaly is diagnostic ~ Basophilia occurs in both equally ~ A ~ The reaction is a response to infection, inflammation or malignancy elsewhere and resolves with the cause, so molecular testing prevents both overtreatment and missed diagnosis.
