@ Medicine ~ Pancreatitis, malabsorption & acute liver failure ~ MED_PAN ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 345 (Acute and Chronic Pancreatitis)
E ~ A patient with acute pancreatitis has a serum calcium of 6.8 mg/dL on day 3. The mechanism is: ~ Excess parathyroid hormone ~ Saponification of calcium by free fatty acids released by lipase ~ Renal tubular calcium wasting ~ Dilution by intravenous fluids ~ B ~ Peripancreatic fat necrosis liberates free fatty acids that chelate calcium as insoluble soaps, and hypocalcaemia is one of the Ranson prognostic markers of severity.
E ~ Chronic pancreatitis with steatorrhoea is best treated with: ~ Oral pancreatic enzyme replacement with acid suppression ~ Long-term antibiotics ~ A high-fat diet ~ Cholecystectomy ~ A ~ Exocrine insufficiency appears once about 90 percent of acinar function is lost; enteric-coated lipase preparations replace it, and reducing gastric acid prevents inactivation of the enzymes.
E ~ Chain-of-lakes appearance of the pancreatic duct on imaging indicates: ~ Acute interstitial pancreatitis ~ Pancreatic carcinoma ~ Chronic pancreatitis with alternating strictures and dilatation ~ Annular pancreas ~ C ~ Repeated inflammation scars the duct irregularly, and the beaded appearance with intraductal calculi is characteristic of chronic calcific pancreatitis, common in tropical settings.
M ~ A patient with severe acute pancreatitis develops fever and rising inflammatory markers in the third week, and CT shows gas within a peripancreatic collection. The next step is: ~ Immediate open necrosectomy ~ Continue antibiotics alone indefinitely ~ Image-guided drainage with a step-up approach to minimally invasive necrosectomy ~ Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography ~ C ~ Infected necrosis is managed by the step-up strategy of percutaneous or endoscopic drainage first, escalating to minimally invasive necrosectomy only if needed, which halves mortality compared with primary open surgery.
M ~ A young adult with chronic diarrhoea has a raised faecal fat, low serum folate and a positive hydrogen breath test after glucose. The likely diagnosis is: ~ Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth ~ Coeliac disease ~ Chronic pancreatitis ~ Lactose intolerance ~ A ~ Stasis from strictures, diverticula or blind loops lets colonic flora colonise the small bowel, where they deconjugate bile salts causing steatorrhoea and consume B12 while synthesising folate, so folate is often normal or high and B12 low.
M ~ Which laboratory feature distinguishes acute liver failure from decompensated chronic liver disease? ~ Presence of ascites ~ Hepatic encephalopathy with an INR above 1.5 arising within 26 weeks in a previously normal liver ~ Raised serum bilirubin ~ Low serum albumin ~ B ~ Acute liver failure is defined by coagulopathy and encephalopathy developing within 26 weeks in someone without pre-existing liver disease; albumin has a three-week half-life so it falls late, making it a marker of chronicity.
M ~ Serum B12 deficiency with a normal or raised folate, in a patient with chronic steatorrhoea and a surgical history of gastrectomy, is best explained by: ~ Loss of intrinsic factor with bacterial overgrowth in the afferent loop ~ Dietary deficiency alone ~ Increased hepatic storage of B12 ~ Excess pancreatic enzyme secretion ~ A ~ Both loss of parietal cells and consumption of B12 by proliferating luminal bacteria contribute, while the same bacteria synthesise folate, giving the characteristic dissociated pattern.
H ~ In acute liver failure, which finding is the strongest indication for referral for emergency liver transplantation in paracetamol overdose? ~ Peak alanine aminotransferase above 5000 U/L ~ Jaundice alone ~ Arterial pH below 7.3 after adequate fluid resuscitation ~ Nausea and vomiting ~ C ~ The King's College criteria list persistent acidosis, or the combination of INR above 6.5 with creatinine above 300 micromol/L and grade 3 to 4 encephalopathy; transaminase height reflects necrosis already past, not prognosis.
H ~ A patient with acute liver failure develops a rising intracranial pressure. Which measure is contraindicated? ~ Head elevation to 30 degrees ~ Hypertonic saline to maintain sodium 145 to 150 mmol/L ~ Routine lactulose given by nasogastric tube in large volumes ~ Avoiding hypercapnia ~ C ~ Large-volume lactulose causes gaseous distension that complicates any subsequent transplant surgery and risks aspiration, so osmotherapy and control of ammonia by other means are preferred in fulminant failure.
@ Medicine ~ Peripheral neuropathy, GBS & myasthenia gravis ~ MED_PN ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 446 (Peripheral Neuropathy)
E ~ A patient develops sudden painless foot drop with weak dorsiflexion and eversion but a preserved ankle jerk and normal inversion. The lesion is at the: ~ L5 nerve root ~ Common peroneal nerve at the neck of the fibula ~ Sciatic nerve in the buttock ~ Tibial nerve at the ankle ~ B ~ Inversion is by tibialis posterior, supplied by the tibial nerve, so its preservation localises the lesion distal to the sciatic bifurcation; an L5 radiculopathy would also weaken inversion and cause back pain.
E ~ Painful asymmetrical involvement of two or more separate named nerves in a diabetic or vasculitic patient is termed: ~ Polyneuropathy ~ Mononeuritis multiplex ~ Radiculopathy ~ Plexopathy ~ B ~ Ischaemic infarction of individual nerve trunks from vasculitis of the vasa nervorum produces stepwise, painful, asymmetrical deficits, and diabetes, polyarteritis nodosa and leprosy are the classic causes.
E ~ Which pattern of neuropathy is characteristic of a predominantly demyelinating process on nerve conduction studies? ~ Reduced amplitude with normal velocity ~ Marked slowing of conduction velocity with conduction block and temporal dispersion ~ Normal studies with abnormal needle electromyography ~ Isolated prolongation of the F wave only ~ B ~ Demyelination slows saltatory conduction and desynchronises the volley, whereas axonal loss reduces the number of conducting fibres and so lowers amplitude while velocity stays near normal.
M ~ A patient with Guillain-Barre syndrome has a forced vital capacity falling to 15 mL/kg with a weak cough. The appropriate action is: ~ Observe with hourly monitoring ~ Start non-invasive ventilation only ~ Elective intubation and mechanical ventilation ~ Give a further course of immunoglobulin ~ C ~ A vital capacity below 20 mL/kg, maximal inspiratory pressure weaker than 30 cmH2O or bulbar weakness predicts respiratory failure, and elective intubation is far safer than emergency intubation after collapse.
M ~ Autonomic involvement in Guillain-Barre syndrome is important because it causes: ~ Only mild postural dizziness ~ Labile blood pressure, arrhythmias and ileus that contribute to mortality ~ Permanent bladder paralysis in all patients ~ Isolated pupillary abnormalities ~ B ~ Dysautonomia is a leading cause of death in Guillain-Barre syndrome, so continuous cardiac monitoring is needed and suctioning may provoke severe bradycardia or asystole.
M ~ A patient with carpal tunnel syndrome has nocturnal paraesthesia. Which examination finding is most specific? ~ Weakness of finger abduction ~ Wasting of the first dorsal interosseous ~ Weakness of thumb abduction with thenar wasting ~ Loss of sensation over the little finger ~ C ~ Abductor pollicis brevis is supplied by the recurrent median motor branch beyond the tunnel, so its weakness confirms median compression, while the ulnar-innervated interossei and little finger are spared.
M ~ Which antibody, found in about half of acetylcholine-receptor-antibody-negative generalised myasthenia, defines a distinct clinical subgroup with prominent bulbar involvement? ~ Anti-MuSK antibody ~ Anti-GQ1b antibody ~ Anti-titin antibody ~ Anti-GM1 antibody ~ A ~ Muscle-specific kinase antibodies give a phenotype with marked bulbar, facial and neck weakness, a poor response to anticholinesterases and a good response to rituximab, and thymoma is rare in this group.
H ~ Ophthalmoplegia, areflexia and ataxia after a diarrhoeal illness, with antibodies to GQ1b ganglioside, indicates: ~ Miller Fisher variant of Guillain-Barre syndrome ~ Botulism ~ Wernicke encephalopathy ~ Brainstem stroke ~ A ~ The GQ1b epitope is densely expressed in the oculomotor nerves, and the triad follows Campylobacter jejuni infection; treatment is immunoglobulin as for classical Guillain-Barre syndrome.
H ~ In a patient on pyridostigmine who deteriorates, which finding suggests cholinergic rather than myasthenic crisis? ~ Ptosis worsening through the day ~ Miosis with excessive salivation, bronchorrhoea and abdominal cramps ~ Improvement after rest ~ Nasal regurgitation of fluids ~ B ~ Excess acetylcholine produces muscarinic overactivity alongside depolarising blockade; recognising it matters because the treatment is to withhold the anticholinesterase rather than increase it.
@ Medicine ~ Pituitary, parathyroid & calcium disorders ~ MED_PIT ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 373 (Disorders of the Anterior Pituitary)
E ~ The first pituitary hormone to be lost in progressive compression by a macroadenoma is usually: ~ Growth hormone ~ Adrenocorticotropic hormone ~ Thyroid-stimulating hormone ~ Prolactin ~ A ~ The classical sequence of loss is growth hormone, then gonadotropins, then TSH and finally ACTH, while prolactin often rises because stalk compression removes dopaminergic inhibition.
E ~ A modestly raised prolactin of 60 ng/mL in a patient with a large sellar mass that is not a prolactinoma is explained by: ~ Autonomous prolactin secretion by the tumour ~ The stalk section effect interrupting dopamine delivery to lactotrophs ~ Coexisting primary hypothyroidism ~ Laboratory artefact ~ B ~ True prolactinomas of that size would give levels in the thousands; a disproportionately mild elevation with a big mass indicates compression of the hypophyseal stalk, which is treated surgically rather than with a dopamine agonist.
E ~ Which biochemical picture indicates pseudohypoparathyroidism? ~ Low calcium, high phosphate, high parathyroid hormone ~ Low calcium, low phosphate, high parathyroid hormone ~ High calcium, low phosphate, high parathyroid hormone ~ Low calcium, high phosphate, low parathyroid hormone ~ A ~ End-organ resistance to parathyroid hormone means the gland secretes appropriately but the kidney and bone do not respond, so hormone levels are high despite hypocalcaemia; Albright hereditary osteodystrophy with short fourth metacarpals is the associated phenotype.
M ~ A patient with hypercalcaemia has a raised parathyroid hormone, a high urinary calcium excretion and a family history of pancreatitis and pituitary tumours. The likely syndrome is: ~ Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia ~ Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 ~ Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A ~ Sarcoidosis ~ B ~ The MEN1 triad of parathyroid hyperplasia, pancreatic islet tumours and pituitary adenoma follows loss of the menin tumour suppressor, and parathyroid disease is usually the first manifestation.
M ~ Hypercalcaemia with a raised parathyroid hormone but a urinary calcium to creatinine clearance ratio below 0.01 suggests: ~ Primary hyperparathyroidism ~ Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia ~ Malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia ~ Vitamin D intoxication ~ B ~ An inactivating calcium-sensing receptor mutation resets the set point and also increases renal calcium reabsorption, so the hypocalciuria distinguishes this benign condition from primary hyperparathyroidism and spares the patient a needless parathyroidectomy.
M ~ Which visual field change is the earliest in a slowly enlarging pituitary adenoma? ~ Superior bitemporal quadrantanopia ~ Inferior bitemporal quadrantanopia ~ Central scotoma ~ Homonymous hemianopia ~ A ~ The tumour rises from below and first compresses the inferonasal fibres that cross low in the chiasm and serve the upper temporal fields, so superior quadrants are lost before the full bitemporal defect appears.
M ~ Postoperatively after pituitary surgery a patient passes 5 litres of dilute urine daily with a rising serum sodium. The immediate treatment is: ~ Fluid restriction ~ Desmopressin with careful fluid replacement ~ Hypertonic saline ~ Intravenous hydrocortisone alone ~ B ~ Surgical injury to the posterior pituitary or stalk causes central diabetes insipidus, which may follow a triphasic course, so vasopressin analogue replacement with attention to free water balance is required.
H ~ A patient with hypercalcaemia of malignancy without bone metastases most often has a tumour secreting: ~ Parathyroid hormone itself ~ Parathyroid hormone-related peptide ~ Calcitonin ~ 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D ~ B ~ Squamous carcinomas of lung, head and neck and renal cell carcinoma secrete PTHrP, which reproduces the actions of parathyroid hormone at its receptor while assays for intact parathyroid hormone read low.
H ~ Persistent hypocalcaemia with hypomagnesaemia that fails to correct with calcium infusion requires: ~ Higher doses of intravenous calcium ~ Correction of the magnesium deficit ~ Intravenous phosphate ~ Vitamin D loading alone ~ B ~ Magnesium is needed both for parathyroid hormone secretion and for its action at target tissues, so hypomagnesaemia causes a functional hypoparathyroidism that only magnesium replacement will reverse.
@ Medicine ~ Pleural effusion, pneumothorax & interstitial lung disease ~ MED_PLE ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 292 (Disorders of the Pleura)
E ~ A pleural effusion with adenosine deaminase activity above 40 U/L and a lymphocyte predominance most suggests: ~ Malignancy ~ Tuberculous pleural effusion ~ Rheumatoid effusion ~ Transudative heart failure ~ B ~ Adenosine deaminase is released by activated lymphocytes and, with a pleural fluid lymphocyte to neutrophil ratio above 0.75, gives high sensitivity for tuberculous pleuritis where smears are usually negative.
E ~ In a spontaneous primary pneumothorax of 1 cm rim in an asymptomatic young man, the appropriate management is: ~ Observation with oxygen and review ~ Immediate intercostal drain ~ Needle aspiration followed by pleurodesis ~ Emergency thoracotomy ~ A ~ Small primary pneumothoraces in a stable patient reabsorb at about 2 percent of hemithorax volume per day, and supplemental oxygen accelerates nitrogen washout, so intervention is reserved for breathlessness or a large rim.
E ~ Recurrent secondary spontaneous pneumothorax in a patient with severe emphysema is best prevented by: ~ Repeated needle aspiration ~ Chemical or surgical pleurodesis ~ Long-term oral corticosteroids ~ Antibiotic prophylaxis ~ B ~ Obliterating the pleural space prevents recurrence, which in secondary pneumothorax carries far higher morbidity because the underlying lung reserve is already poor.
M ~ Pleural fluid glucose below 30 mg/dL with a very low pH and a high rheumatoid factor titre suggests: ~ Empyema ~ Rheumatoid pleuritis ~ Malignant effusion ~ Chylothorax ~ B ~ Rheumatoid effusions share the very low glucose and pH of empyema but are sterile; the combination is one of the lowest pleural glucose values encountered and is a useful clue in a patient with erosive arthritis.
M ~ A patient with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis is started on treatment. Which agent slows the decline in forced vital capacity? ~ High-dose prednisolone ~ Pirfenidone or nintedanib ~ Azathioprine with N-acetylcysteine ~ Inhaled corticosteroid ~ B ~ The PANTHER trial showed that prednisolone with azathioprine and N-acetylcysteine increased mortality in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, whereas the antifibrotics pirfenidone and nintedanib slow functional decline.
M ~ Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy with erythema nodosum, fever and arthralgia in a young woman describes: ~ Lofgren syndrome, an acute presentation of sarcoidosis ~ Lymphoma ~ Primary tuberculosis ~ Hypersensitivity pneumonitis ~ A ~ This acute form carries an excellent prognosis with spontaneous remission in most patients, so observation with symptomatic treatment is usually sufficient rather than corticosteroids.
M ~ A farmer develops fever, cough and breathlessness 6 hours after cleaning a mouldy hay barn, with recurrent similar episodes and mid-inspiratory squeaks. The likely diagnosis is: ~ Occupational asthma ~ Hypersensitivity pneumonitis ~ Silicosis ~ Bronchiectasis ~ B ~ Antigen exposure provokes a lymphocytic alveolitis with poorly formed granulomas; the timing hours after exposure, the absence of wheeze and the upper-zone changes on high-resolution CT distinguish it from asthma, and antigen avoidance is the key treatment.
H ~ Pleural fluid with a triglyceride level above 110 mg/dL and chylomicrons on electrophoresis indicates: ~ Pseudochylothorax ~ Chylothorax ~ Empyema ~ Haemothorax ~ B ~ True chylothorax reflects thoracic duct disruption by trauma or lymphoma, whereas pseudochylothorax in a chronic effusion is cholesterol-rich with crystals and a normal triglyceride level.
H ~ Which finding on high-resolution CT most favours non-specific interstitial pneumonia over usual interstitial pneumonia? ~ Basal honeycombing with traction bronchiectasis ~ Uniform ground-glass opacity with subpleural sparing ~ Upper-zone nodular fibrosis ~ Apical bullae ~ B ~ Homogeneous ground-glass change with relative sparing of the immediate subpleural zone marks the cellular and fibrotic non-specific pattern, which responds far better to immunosuppression and carries a better prognosis than usual interstitial pneumonia.
@ Medicine ~ Pneumonia, bronchiectasis & lung abscess ~ MED_PNA ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 121 (Pneumonia)
E ~ Which vaccine schedule is recommended for an adult with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease to prevent pneumococcal disease? ~ No vaccination is indicated ~ Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, with annual influenza vaccination ~ Live attenuated pneumococcal vaccine ~ BCG revaccination ~ B ~ Both pneumococcal and annual influenza immunisation reduce exacerbations and hospitalisation in chronic lung disease, and pneumococcal vaccines are all inactivated or polysaccharide-based, never live.
E ~ Sputum with a foul, putrid smell in a patient with a cavitating lower-lobe lesion suggests infection with: ~ Anaerobic organisms ~ Streptococcus pneumoniae ~ Mycoplasma pneumoniae ~ Legionella pneumophila ~ A ~ Putrid sputum is essentially diagnostic of anaerobic infection following aspiration, and treatment requires an agent with anaerobic cover such as clindamycin or a beta-lactam with beta-lactamase inhibitor.
E ~ Which finding on examination indicates consolidation rather than a pleural effusion? ~ Stony dull percussion note with absent breath sounds ~ Dull percussion with bronchial breathing and increased vocal resonance ~ Trachea deviated away from the affected side ~ Reduced chest expansion with a hyperresonant note ~ B ~ Consolidated lung transmits sound better than air-filled lung, so vocal resonance and bronchial breathing increase, whereas fluid between lung and chest wall abolishes them.
M ~ A patient with community-acquired pneumonia has failed to improve after 72 hours of appropriate antibiotics. The most useful next step is: ~ Change antibiotics empirically ~ Repeat imaging to look for empyema, abscess or an obstructing lesion ~ Add systemic corticosteroids ~ Discharge with oral therapy ~ B ~ Non-resolving pneumonia most often reflects a complication or an alternative diagnosis such as bronchial obstruction, organising pneumonia or tuberculosis, so re-imaging directs the search rather than blind antibiotic escalation.
M ~ Which organism is most likely in a patient with bronchiectasis and chronic purulent sputum colonisation? ~ Pseudomonas aeruginosa ~ Streptococcus pyogenes ~ Neisseria meningitidis ~ Bacteroides fragilis ~ A ~ Chronic Pseudomonas colonisation marks more advanced disease with faster lung function decline, and its presence changes both antibiotic choice during exacerbations and the case for long-term azithromycin.
M ~ Long-term low-dose azithromycin in bronchiectasis works chiefly by: ~ Eradicating all airway bacteria ~ Immunomodulation with reduced neutrophilic inflammation and mucus secretion ~ Bronchodilatation ~ Reversing established airway dilatation ~ B ~ Macrolides reduce exacerbation frequency through anti-inflammatory and antibiofilm effects rather than sterilisation; non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection must be excluded first to avoid inducing macrolide resistance.
M ~ Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis in an asthmatic is suggested by: ~ Central bronchiectasis with a high total IgE and Aspergillus-specific IgE ~ Peripheral bronchiectasis with a normal IgE ~ Cavitation with an air-crescent sign ~ Miliary nodules ~ A ~ A hypersensitivity response to Aspergillus colonising the airways produces proximal bronchiectasis, mucus plugging, eosinophilia and very high IgE, and treatment is corticosteroids with itraconazole rather than antibacterials.
H ~ In severe community-acquired pneumonia requiring intensive care, adjunctive corticosteroids are most clearly beneficial in patients with: ~ Any pneumonia regardless of severity ~ Severe pneumonia with a marked inflammatory response, where they reduce treatment failure ~ Suspected influenza pneumonia ~ Aspergillus pneumonia ~ B ~ Trials including CAPE COD showed benefit from hydrocortisone in severe community-acquired pneumonia, whereas steroids worsen outcomes in influenza and invasive fungal disease.
H ~ A young adult with recurrent sinopulmonary infection, bronchiectasis and infertility has a normal sweat chloride. The likely diagnosis is: ~ Cystic fibrosis ~ Primary ciliary dyskinesia ~ Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency ~ Common variable immunodeficiency ~ B ~ Defective ciliary ultrastructure impairs mucociliary clearance and sperm motility; about half have situs inversus, and nasal nitric oxide is characteristically low, distinguishing it from cystic fibrosis.
@ Medicine ~ Poisoning & envenomation: emergency management ~ MED_POI ~ Harrison 21e, Ch. 449 (Poisoning and Drug Overdose)
E ~ The antidote for benzodiazepine overdose, used with caution because it may precipitate seizures, is: ~ Naloxone ~ Flumazenil ~ Physostigmine ~ Pralidoxime ~ B ~ Flumazenil competitively blocks the benzodiazepine site on the GABA-A receptor, but in a mixed overdose or a chronically dependent patient it can unmask proconvulsant effects, so airway support is often preferred.
E ~ Which feature distinguishes an anticholinergic toxidrome from a sympathomimetic one? ~ Mydriasis ~ Dry, flushed skin with urinary retention and absent bowel sounds ~ Tachycardia ~ Agitation ~ B ~ Both cause mydriasis, tachycardia and agitation, but sympathomimetic poisoning produces sweating and normal bowel sounds, whereas antimuscarinic blockade dries secretions and paralyses smooth muscle.
E ~ In aluminium phosphide poisoning, the principal mechanism of death is: ~ Phosphine-induced inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase causing refractory shock ~ Direct corrosive oesophageal injury ~ Haemolysis ~ Renal tubular necrosis ~ A ~ Contact with gastric acid liberates phosphine gas, which blocks mitochondrial respiration and produces profound myocardial depression with severe metabolic acidosis, and there is no specific antidote.
M ~ A patient with a Russell viper bite has continuing bleeding despite antivenom, with a prolonged whole blood clotting time. The next step is: ~ Repeat the antivenom dose ~ Give fresh frozen plasma alone ~ Give heparin ~ Perform fasciotomy ~ A ~ Persisting incoagulable blood after 6 hours indicates continuing circulating venom, so a further dose of polyvalent antivenom is given; clotting factors are consumed uselessly until the venom is neutralised.
M ~ Which antidote is used for methaemoglobinaemia and must be avoided in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency? ~ Methylene blue ~ Sodium thiosulphate ~ Hydroxocobalamin ~ Deferoxamine ~ A ~ Methylene blue works through NADPH-dependent methaemoglobin reductase, which requires the pentose phosphate pathway, so in G6PD deficiency it is ineffective and can precipitate haemolysis; ascorbic acid or exchange transfusion is used instead.
M ~ A patient with an intentional overdose has a serum osmolality of 340 mOsm/kg, a measured osmolar gap of 30 and no acidosis at presentation. The likely agent is: ~ Salicylate ~ An alcohol such as ethylene glycol or methanol before metabolism ~ Paracetamol ~ Digoxin ~ B ~ The parent alcohols are small osmotically active molecules, so early on the osmolar gap is wide while the anion gap is normal; as they are metabolised to acids the osmolar gap closes and the anion gap opens.
M ~ Which measure most effectively enhances elimination in severe salicylate poisoning with an altered mental state? ~ Multiple-dose activated charcoal alone ~ Haemodialysis ~ Forced acid diuresis ~ Gastric lavage ~ B ~ Salicylate has a low molecular weight and limited protein binding at toxic levels, so dialysis removes it efficiently and simultaneously corrects the acidosis; altered mental state, renal failure or a very high level are the accepted indications.
H ~ In digoxin toxicity, which electrolyte abnormality most strongly indicates the need for digoxin-specific antibody fragments? ~ Hyponatraemia ~ Hyperkalaemia ~ Hypocalcaemia ~ Hypophosphataemia ~ B ~ Inhibition of the sodium-potassium ATPase moves potassium out of cells, and a potassium above 5.5 mmol/L in acute poisoning predicts lethal arrhythmia; calcium must be avoided because it worsens digoxin-induced contracture.
H ~ Delayed presentation after a paracetamol overdose beyond 24 hours with a raised INR and transaminases should be managed by: ~ Withholding acetylcysteine as it is now useless ~ Starting intravenous acetylcysteine regardless of the time since ingestion ~ Giving activated charcoal ~ Giving oral methionine only ~ B ~ Acetylcysteine still improves survival in established paracetamol-induced liver injury through antioxidant and microcirculatory effects independent of glutathione repletion, so late presentation is not a reason to withhold it.
