@ Anatomy ~ Lower limb: femoral/sciatic nerve, foot drop ~ AN_LL ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Lower Limb
M ~ Which nerve is at risk during an intramuscular injection given in the lower medial quadrant of the buttock, and what deficit results? ~ The sciatic nerve, causing foot drop with loss of sensation over the sole and lateral leg ~ The femoral nerve, causing loss of knee extension ~ The obturator nerve, causing loss of adduction ~ The pudendal nerve, causing incontinence ~ A ~ Injections are given in the upper outer quadrant precisely to avoid the nerve, and the resulting palsy affects the common peroneal component most because its fascicles lie laterally and are more vulnerable.
M ~ A patient cannot evert the foot but dorsiflexion is preserved, with sensory loss over the lower lateral leg and dorsum. Which nerve is injured? ~ The superficial peroneal nerve ~ The deep peroneal nerve ~ The tibial nerve ~ The sural nerve ~ A ~ The deep division supplies the dorsiflexors and a small web-space patch of skin, so isolated loss of eversion with preserved dorsiflexion localises the lesion to the superficial division rather than to the common trunk.
E ~ Which artery is the chief supply to the head of the femur in an adult, and why does a fracture of the femoral neck threaten it? ~ The medial circumflex femoral artery through its retinacular branches, which run along the neck and are torn by a displaced intracapsular fracture ~ The obturator artery through the ligamentum teres, which is the main supply in adults ~ The lateral circumflex femoral artery ~ The first perforating branch of the profunda femoris ~ A ~ The artery of the ligamentum teres contributes significantly only in childhood, which is why avascular necrosis follows a displaced intracapsular fracture in an adult and internal fixation is often replaced by arthroplasty.
M ~ Which structures pass through the adductor canal, and what is its surgical importance? ~ The femoral artery and vein with the nerve to vastus medialis and the saphenous nerve, making it the site of femoral artery exposure in the thigh ~ The sciatic nerve and popliteal vessels ~ The obturator nerve and vessels ~ The great saphenous vein alone ~ A ~ The canal ends at the adductor hiatus where the vessels become the popliteal artery and vein, and blockade of the saphenous nerve here provides analgesia for knee surgery while sparing quadriceps power.
H ~ A patient walks with the trunk lurching towards the affected side and a positive Trendelenburg sign after hip surgery. Which nerve and muscles are affected? ~ The superior gluteal nerve supplying gluteus medius and minimus, the abductors that stabilise the pelvis in single-leg stance ~ The inferior gluteal nerve supplying gluteus maximus ~ The femoral nerve supplying quadriceps ~ The obturator nerve supplying adductors ~ A ~ The lurch shifts the centre of gravity over the affected hip to reduce the abductor moment required, which is why the compensation itself is diagnostic of abductor weakness rather than of pain.
@ Anatomy ~ Abdomen: peritoneum, liver & biliary, portal system ~ AN_AB ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Abdomen
M ~ Which peritoneal recess is the commonest site of a subphrenic collection after upper abdominal surgery, and why? ~ The right subphrenic and subhepatic spaces, because peritoneal fluid is drawn upward along the right paracolic gutter by the negative pressure of respiration ~ The left paracolic gutter, because it is deeper ~ The lesser sac, because it is closed ~ The pelvic cavity, which never collects fluid ~ A ~ The phrenicocolic ligament limits upward spread on the left, so a right-sided collection with referred shoulder pain and a pleural effusion is the classic postoperative presentation.
M ~ Which segments of the liver are removed in a right hepatectomy, and what defines the plane of resection? ~ Segments 5 to 8, with the plane defined by the middle hepatic vein and the line of Cantlie rather than by the falciform ligament ~ Segments 2 to 4, with the plane at the falciform ligament ~ Segments 1 to 4 ~ Segments 4 to 8, with the plane at the right hepatic vein ~ A ~ The Couinaud scheme divides the liver by the portal and hepatic venous anatomy, and this functional rather than surface anatomy is what makes segmental and lobar resection possible.
H ~ Which anatomical variant of the cystic artery most often causes bleeding during laparoscopic cholecystectomy? ~ A cystic artery arising from the right hepatic artery but crossing anterior to the bile duct or arising outside Calot triangle ~ A cystic artery arising from the portal vein ~ Absence of the cystic artery ~ A cystic artery arising from the splenic artery ~ A ~ A right hepatic artery running a tortuous course close to the cystic duct, the caterpillar hump, is the other classic hazard, which is why the critical view of safety is established before any structure is divided.
@ Anatomy ~ Brachial plexus & nerve injuries (radial/median/ulnar) ~ AN_BP ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Upper Limb
M ~ A patient has clawing of the ring and little fingers with a positive Froment sign and normal sensation over the dorsum of the hand. Where is the lesion? ~ The ulnar nerve at the wrist, distal to the origin of the dorsal cutaneous branch ~ The ulnar nerve at the elbow ~ The median nerve at the wrist ~ The lower brachial plexus ~ A ~ The dorsal cutaneous branch leaves about 5 cm proximal to the wrist, so preserved dorsal sensation with motor loss localises the injury distally, and the thumb adductor weakness compensated by flexor pollicis longus is what the Froment sign demonstrates.
H ~ A patient has weakness of shoulder abduction and elbow flexion with a small pupil and drooping eyelid on the same side after a motorcycle crash. What does the pupillary sign indicate? ~ Avulsion of the T1 root proximal to the sympathetic outflow, indicating a preganglionic injury with a poor prognosis for recovery ~ Injury to the axillary nerve ~ A postganglionic rupture with a good prognosis ~ Injury to the long thoracic nerve ~ A ~ An associated Horner syndrome, elevated hemidiaphragm or winged scapula all point to root avulsion rather than a distal rupture, which changes management from expectant to early nerve transfer surgery.
@ Anatomy ~ Cerebrum: blood supply, internal capsule, basal ganglia, cerebellum ~ AN_CER ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Head and Neck
M ~ Occlusion of which artery causes weakness of the contralateral leg greater than the arm with urinary incontinence? ~ The anterior cerebral artery, supplying the medial surface including the paracentral lobule ~ The middle cerebral artery ~ The posterior cerebral artery ~ The basilar artery ~ A ~ The leg area lies on the medial surface within the territory of this vessel, and bilateral involvement from an anterior communicating artery aneurysm can produce paraplegia with abulia and a grasp reflex.
M ~ Occlusion of the posterior cerebral artery produces which visual defect, and why is central vision often spared? ~ Contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing, because the occipital pole receives collateral supply from the middle cerebral artery ~ Bitemporal hemianopia, because the chiasma is supplied by this vessel ~ Monocular blindness ~ Upper quadrantanopia only ~ A ~ The same vessel supplies the thalamus and midbrain, so an infarct may be accompanied by hemisensory loss, a third nerve palsy or memory disturbance from medial temporal involvement.
H ~ A lesion of which cerebellar structure causes truncal ataxia with a wide-based gait and little limb incoordination? ~ The vermis and flocculonodular lobe, which is the midline vestibulocerebellum and spinocerebellum ~ The lateral hemisphere, which causes truncal ataxia ~ The dentate nucleus ~ The superior cerebellar peduncle ~ A ~ Medulloblastoma in a child arises in this midline region, whereas a lateral hemispheric lesion instead causes ipsilateral limb dysmetria, intention tremor and dysdiadochokinesia.
@ Anatomy ~ General embryology & histology: fertilisation, placenta, epithelia ~ AN_EMB ~ Langman Medical Embryology 14e, Ch. Gametogenesis and First Weeks
M ~ Which structure gives rise to the amnion, and what is the consequence of oligohydramnios in the second trimester? ~ The epiblast, and severe oligohydramnios causes Potter sequence with pulmonary hypoplasia, limb deformity and a flattened facies ~ The trophoblast, and oligohydramnios causes hydrops ~ The hypoblast, and oligohydramnios has no effect ~ The extraembryonic mesoderm, and oligohydramnios causes polyhydramnios ~ A ~ Amniotic fluid is largely fetal urine after the first trimester, so bilateral renal agenesis or obstruction produces the sequence and the lethal element is the failure of lung development.
E ~ Which type of epithelium lines the urinary bladder, and what is its functional advantage? ~ Transitional epithelium or urothelium, which allows distension without loss of the barrier ~ Simple columnar epithelium, which allows absorption ~ Keratinised stratified squamous epithelium, which resists abrasion ~ Simple cuboidal epithelium, which allows secretion ~ A ~ The umbrella cells with their specialised plaques and tight junctions maintain the barrier against urine as the surface area changes, and it is this epithelium that gives rise to the commonest bladder carcinoma.
M ~ Which germ layer gives rise to the adrenal medulla, and what does this explain clinically? ~ Neural crest ectoderm, which explains why phaeochromocytoma arises with other neural crest tumours in the multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes ~ Mesoderm, the same as the cortex ~ Endoderm, the same as the thyroid ~ Surface ectoderm ~ A ~ The cortex derives from intermediate mesoderm instead, so a single gland has two embryologically distinct halves, and extra-adrenal chromaffin tissue along the sympathetic chain explains paragangliomas outside the gland.
@ Anatomy ~ Head & neck: triangles, thyroid, parotid, TMJ ~ AN_HN ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Head and Neck
M ~ Which nerve is at risk during ligation of the inferior thyroid artery, and what is the resulting deficit? ~ The recurrent laryngeal nerve, causing hoarseness with a paramedian vocal cord ~ The external laryngeal nerve, causing loss of high notes ~ The hypoglossal nerve, causing tongue deviation ~ The glossopharyngeal nerve, causing loss of gag ~ A ~ The artery is therefore ligated well away from the gland close to its origin, whereas the superior thyroid artery is ligated close to the gland to protect the external laryngeal nerve, so the two vessels follow opposite rules.
M ~ Which structure lies immediately deep to the submandibular gland and is at risk during its excision? ~ The lingual nerve, the hypoglossal nerve and the facial artery ~ The internal carotid artery ~ The glossopharyngeal nerve ~ The vagus nerve in the carotid sheath ~ A ~ The marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve is also at risk in the skin incision, which is why the incision is placed two finger-breadths below the mandible.
H ~ Which anatomical feature of the temporomandibular joint permits the wide opening of the mouth, and which movement causes anterior dislocation? ~ Translation of the condyle forward onto the articular eminence during the second phase of opening, so excessive opening can carry it anterior to the eminence ~ A purely hinge movement at the condyle ~ Rotation of the disc alone ~ Posterior translation of the condyle ~ A ~ Reduction requires downward and backward pressure on the molars to lift the condyle back over the eminence, and recurrent dislocation is associated with joint laxity and a shallow eminence.
@ Anatomy ~ Pelvis & perineum: pelvic floor, urogenital triangle ~ AN_PP ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Pelvis and Perineum
M ~ Which nerve block is used for the second stage of labour and forceps delivery, and where is the landmark? ~ Pudendal nerve block at the ischial spine, approached transvaginally ~ Obturator nerve block at the obturator foramen ~ Paracervical block at the uterosacral ligament ~ Ilioinguinal nerve block at the anterior superior iliac spine ~ A ~ The nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen and re-enters through the lesser, hooking around the spine, and it supplies the perineal skin, external anal sphincter and the anal canal below the pectinate line.
M ~ Which fascial arrangement determines the pattern of urine extravasation after rupture of the membranous urethra? ~ Rupture above the perineal membrane allows urine to extravasate into the extraperitoneal pelvic tissue around the bladder rather than into the superficial perineal pouch ~ Urine always tracks into the scrotum and anterior abdominal wall ~ Urine enters the peritoneal cavity ~ Urine tracks into the ischioanal fossa ~ A ~ Bulbar urethral rupture below the membrane instead fills the superficial pouch and spreads into the scrotum, penis and anterior abdominal wall, so the site of injury is inferred from where the swelling appears.
H ~ Which structure must be preserved to maintain continence during a low anterior resection for rectal carcinoma? ~ The puborectalis component of the levator ani with the intact anal sphincter complex and its pudendal innervation ~ The coccygeus muscle ~ The obturator internus ~ The piriformis ~ A ~ The anorectal angle maintained by this sling is as important as the sphincter itself, which is why damage to it or to the nerve supply produces incontinence even when the sphincter ring appears intact.
@ Anatomy ~ Skull base, cavernous sinus & meninges ~ AN_SK ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Head and Neck
M ~ Which structures pass through the jugular foramen, and what is the effect of a tumour there? ~ Cranial nerves IX, X and XI with the internal jugular vein, so a tumour causes loss of gag, hoarseness and weakness of trapezius and sternocleidomastoid ~ Cranial nerves III, IV and VI ~ The internal carotid artery and cranial nerve VII ~ Cranial nerve XII alone ~ A ~ This combination is Vernet syndrome, and involvement of the hypoglossal nerve in the nearby canal or of the sympathetic chain extends it to the other named jugular foramen syndromes.
M ~ Rupture of which vessel causes an extradural haematoma after a temporal skull fracture, and why does the patient deteriorate after a lucid interval? ~ The middle meningeal artery, because arterial bleeding gradually strips the dura from the skull until the expanding mass causes herniation ~ A bridging vein, because venous bleeding is slow ~ The cavernous sinus ~ The superior sagittal sinus ~ A ~ Because the dura is firmly adherent at the sutures the collection is limited and lens-shaped on imaging, and the lucid interval is the window in which surgical evacuation is life-saving.
H ~ Which anatomical relationship explains why an infection of the upper lip or nose can cause cavernous sinus thrombosis? ~ The facial vein communicates with the sinus through the superior ophthalmic vein and the deep facial vein, and these veins are valveless so flow can reverse ~ Infection spreads along the trigeminal nerve ~ Infection reaches it through the middle meningeal artery ~ Lymphatic drainage passes into the sinus ~ A ~ This valveless communication is the anatomical basis of the danger area of the face, and thrombosis presents with proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia and reduced corneal sensation.
@ Anatomy ~ Thorax: lungs, pleura, diaphragm ~ AN_TH ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Thorax
E ~ At which site is an intercostal drain inserted for a pneumothorax, and which structures define the safe triangle? ~ The fifth intercostal space in the midaxillary line, within the triangle bounded by the lateral border of pectoralis major, the lateral border of latissimus dorsi and a line above the level of the nipple ~ The second space in the midclavicular line, below the nipple ~ The eighth space posteriorly ~ The subcostal margin anteriorly ~ A ~ Placing the drain within this triangle avoids the long thoracic nerve, the muscle bulk of the back and the diaphragm, and the tube is passed just above a rib to avoid the neurovascular bundle.
M ~ Which pleural recess is the most dependent part of the pleural cavity, and why does this matter for drainage? ~ The costodiaphragmatic recess, which is where a small effusion collects and where a needle is directed ~ The costomediastinal recess ~ The cupola of the pleura ~ The oblique fissure ~ A ~ The pleura extends about two ribs lower than the lung in this recess, so a needle placed there enters pleura without traversing lung, which is what makes thoracocentesis safe at that level.
H ~ Which anatomical feature of the diaphragm explains why a congenital diaphragmatic hernia is usually left-sided and posterolateral? ~ Failure of closure of the pleuroperitoneal canal at the foramen of Bochdalek, which closes later on the left and is protected on the right by the liver ~ Weakness at the foramen of Morgagni, which is posterolateral ~ A defect in the central tendon ~ Absence of the crura ~ A ~ Herniated bowel compresses the developing lung so pulmonary hypoplasia and pulmonary hypertension rather than the hernia itself determine survival, and a Morgagni hernia is instead anterior, retrosternal and usually right-sided.
@ Anatomy ~ Upper limb: axilla, cubital fossa, hand spaces ~ AN_UL ~ Snell Clinical Anatomy by Regions 10e, Ch. The Upper Limb
M ~ Which nerve is at risk during axillary lymph node clearance, and what deficit results? ~ The long thoracic nerve, whose injury denervates serratus anterior and produces a winged scapula ~ The axillary nerve, producing deltoid paralysis ~ The musculocutaneous nerve, producing loss of elbow flexion ~ The radial nerve, producing wrist drop ~ A ~ The thoracodorsal nerve to latissimus dorsi is the other structure at risk, and both run on the walls of the axilla which is why they are identified and preserved during clearance.
M ~ Which tendon sheath arrangement explains why an infection of the little finger can spread to the forearm? ~ The digital sheath of the little finger is usually continuous with the ulnar bursa, which extends proximal to the flexor retinaculum into the space of Parona ~ Each digital sheath is entirely separate ~ The sheath communicates with the mid-palmar space directly ~ The sheath ends at the metacarpophalangeal joint ~ A ~ The thumb sheath similarly continues as the radial bursa, so infection in either of these two digits can reach the forearm whereas infection of the index, middle or ring finger is usually confined to the digit.
H ~ Which structure is compressed in the quadrangular space, and what is the resulting clinical picture? ~ The axillary nerve with the posterior circumflex humeral artery, causing deltoid weakness and sensory loss over the lateral shoulder ~ The radial nerve with the profunda brachii ~ The suprascapular nerve ~ The musculocutaneous nerve ~ A ~ The space is bounded by the humerus, the long head of triceps and the two teres muscles, and the same structures are injured by a surgical neck fracture or by inferior dislocation of the shoulder.
