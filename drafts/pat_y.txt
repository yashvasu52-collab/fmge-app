@ Pathology ~ Lung: COPD, pneumonia, carcinoma lung, occupational lung disease ~ PAT_LNG ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. The Lung
E ~ Which lung carcinoma is most strongly associated with smoking and characteristically arises centrally with cavitation and hypercalcaemia? ~ Squamous cell carcinoma, secreting parathyroid hormone-related peptide ~ Adenocarcinoma ~ Small cell carcinoma ~ Large cell carcinoma ~ A ~ Adenocarcinoma is now the commonest overall and the commonest in non-smokers and in women, arising peripherally and carrying targetable EGFR and ALK alterations.
E ~ Which occupational exposure causes upper zone nodular fibrosis with eggshell calcification of hilar nodes and a greatly increased risk of tuberculosis? ~ Silica ~ Asbestos ~ Coal dust ~ Cotton dust ~ A ~ Silica impairs macrophage function, which explains the tuberculosis association, whereas asbestos causes lower zone fibrosis with pleural plaques and mesothelioma.
E ~ Which pathological definition distinguishes emphysema from chronic bronchitis? ~ Emphysema is permanent alveolar enlargement with wall destruction, while chronic bronchitis is defined clinically by productive cough for 3 months in 2 consecutive years ~ Both are defined clinically ~ Both are defined anatomically ~ Emphysema is defined by cough ~ A ~ Because one definition is anatomical and the other clinical, the two frequently coexist, and the Reid index of gland to wall thickness is the histological correlate of bronchitis.
M ~ Which paraneoplastic syndrome is characteristic of small cell carcinoma of the lung? ~ Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion and ectopic ACTH production ~ Hypercalcaemia from parathyroid hormone-related peptide ~ Hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy ~ Polycythaemia ~ A ~ Its neuroendocrine origin explains the hormonal syndromes and the Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome, and it is almost always disseminated at presentation so it is treated with chemotherapy rather than surgery.
M ~ Which type of emphysema is associated with alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, and which with smoking? ~ Panacinar emphysema affecting the lower lobes in deficiency, and centriacinar emphysema affecting the upper lobes in smoking ~ Centriacinar in deficiency and panacinar in smoking ~ Paraseptal in both ~ Irregular emphysema in both ~ A ~ Unopposed neutrophil elastase in deficiency destroys the whole acinus and the basal distribution reflects greater perfusion, whereas smoke deposition is greatest in the upper zone respiratory bronchioles.
H ~ Why does asbestos exposure multiply the risk of bronchogenic carcinoma in a smoker far more than either exposure alone? ~ The two carcinogens act synergistically on the same bronchial epithelium, so the combined risk is multiplicative rather than additive ~ Asbestos blocks mucociliary clearance only ~ Smoking causes mesothelioma ~ The risks are independent ~ A ~ Mesothelioma risk by contrast is not increased by smoking, which is why the two asbestos-related malignancies behave differently in relation to tobacco.
@ Pathology ~ Male & female genital tract pathology ~ PAT_GEN2 ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. The Male and Female Genital Tracts
E ~ Which testicular germ cell tumour is most common, radiosensitive and associated with a raised placental alkaline phosphatase? ~ Seminoma ~ Yolk sac tumour ~ Choriocarcinoma ~ Teratoma ~ A ~ Alpha-fetoprotein elevation excludes pure seminoma and indicates a non-seminomatous element, which changes both prognosis and management.
E ~ Which human papillomavirus types account for most cervical carcinoma, and which viral proteins are responsible? ~ Types 16 and 18, through E6 degrading p53 and E7 inactivating RB ~ Types 6 and 11, through E1 and E2 ~ Types 16 and 18, through L1 capsid protein ~ Types 31 and 33, through E4 ~ A ~ Types 6 and 11 cause condylomata rather than cancer, and vaccine design targets the L1 capsid protein because it generates neutralising antibody.
E ~ Which prostatic zone gives rise to most carcinomas, and which to benign hyperplasia? ~ The peripheral zone for carcinoma and the transitional zone for hyperplasia ~ The transitional zone for carcinoma ~ The central zone for both ~ The anterior fibromuscular stroma for carcinoma ~ A ~ This anatomy explains why carcinoma is palpable on digital rectal examination while hyperplasia obstructs the urethra, and why they can coexist independently.
M ~ Which grading system is used for prostatic adenocarcinoma, and what does it assess? ~ The Gleason system, based on the architectural pattern of glands rather than nuclear features ~ The Fuhrman system, based on nuclear grade ~ The Bloom-Richardson system ~ The Nottingham system ~ A ~ Because it is architectural, it is assessed at low power, and the modern grade group system translates Gleason scores into five prognostic groups to improve communication.
M ~ Which ovarian tumour contains mature tissue from more than one germ layer and is the commonest ovarian neoplasm in young women? ~ Mature cystic teratoma, or dermoid cyst ~ Serous cystadenoma ~ Dysgerminoma ~ Granulosa cell tumour ~ A ~ Malignant transformation is rare and usually squamous, and the specific risk of torsion together with the rare complication of gliomatosis peritonei and of anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis are worth knowing.
H ~ Why does testicular carcinoma in situ warrant treatment even though it is confined to the seminiferous tubules? ~ It progresses to invasive germ cell tumour in the great majority within a few years ~ It causes infertility only ~ It resolves spontaneously ~ It is not a precursor lesion ~ A ~ It is often found in the contralateral testis of a man with a germ cell tumour and in biopsy of an atrophic or undescended testis, so its identification alters surveillance and treatment of the remaining gonad.
@ Pathology ~ Myelodysplasia, marrow failure & transfusion medicine ~ PAT_MDS ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Diseases of White Blood Cells and Transfusion Medicine
E ~ Which finding in the marrow defines a myelodysplastic syndrome? ~ Dysplastic maturation of one or more lineages with ineffective haemopoiesis and cytopenias, with fewer than 20 percent blasts ~ Hypocellular marrow with no dysplasia ~ More than 20 percent blasts ~ Marrow fibrosis alone ~ A ~ The paradox of a cellular marrow with peripheral cytopenias reflects intramedullary apoptosis, and the risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukaemia is graded by blast percentage and cytogenetics.
E ~ Which transfusion reaction presents with fever, loin pain, haemoglobinuria and hypotension within minutes of starting a unit? ~ Acute haemolytic reaction from ABO incompatibility ~ Febrile non-haemolytic reaction ~ Allergic urticarial reaction ~ Transfusion-related acute lung injury ~ A ~ It is almost always a clerical error, so the transfusion is stopped immediately, the unit and patient identity rechecked, and support given for disseminated intravascular coagulation and renal failure.
E ~ Which blood product is indicated to replace fibrinogen specifically? ~ Cryoprecipitate ~ Fresh frozen plasma ~ Packed red cells ~ Platelet concentrate ~ A ~ Cryoprecipitate is concentrated in fibrinogen, factor VIII, von Willebrand factor and factor XIII, so it corrects hypofibrinogenaemia in far less volume than plasma.
M ~ Which finding indicates aplastic anaemia rather than myelodysplasia in a patient with pancytopenia? ~ A hypocellular marrow replaced by fat with no dysplasia and no excess blasts ~ A hypercellular marrow with dysplasia ~ Ring sideroblasts ~ Marrow fibrosis with teardrop cells ~ A ~ The distinction matters because immunosuppression and transplantation treat aplasia whereas hypomethylating agents and transplantation are used in myelodysplasia, and hypoplastic myelodysplasia can blur the boundary.
M ~ Which transfusion complication presents with acute hypoxaemia and bilateral infiltrates within 6 hours, without volume overload? ~ Transfusion-related acute lung injury, mediated by donor antibody against recipient leucocytes ~ Transfusion-associated circulatory overload ~ Bacterial contamination ~ Acute haemolytic reaction ~ A ~ Distinguishing it from circulatory overload matters because the treatment is supportive rather than diuretic, and it is a leading cause of transfusion-related death.
H ~ Why is irradiated blood used for a patient receiving intrauterine transfusion or for a stem cell transplant recipient? ~ Irradiation inactivates donor lymphocytes and prevents transfusion-associated graft versus host disease ~ It removes leucocytes to prevent febrile reactions ~ It sterilises the unit ~ It prevents cytomegalovirus transmission ~ A ~ Leucodepletion reduces febrile reactions, HLA alloimmunisation and cytomegalovirus transmission but does not prevent graft versus host disease, which is why the two processes are not interchangeable.
@ Pathology ~ Plasma cell dyscrasias & myeloproliferative neoplasms ~ PAT_PLZ ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. Diseases of White Blood Cells
E ~ Which criteria define symptomatic multiple myeloma requiring treatment? ~ Clonal plasma cells with hypercalcaemia, renal impairment, anaemia or bone lesions, or a defined biomarker of malignancy ~ Any monoclonal protein in serum ~ Marrow plasma cells above 5 percent ~ Bone pain alone ~ A ~ The addition of biomarkers such as 60 percent marrow plasma cells, a light chain ratio above 100 or more than one focal lesion on MRI allows treatment before end-organ damage occurs.
E ~ Which myeloproliferative neoplasm is defined by the JAK2 V617F mutation with a raised haematocrit and a low erythropoietin? ~ Polycythaemia vera ~ Essential thrombocythaemia ~ Primary myelofibrosis ~ Chronic myeloid leukaemia ~ A ~ A low erythropoietin distinguishes it from secondary polycythaemia, and treatment with venesection and low-dose aspirin, with hydroxyurea in high-risk patients, prevents thrombosis.
E ~ Which finding on blood film suggests primary myelofibrosis? ~ Teardrop poikilocytes with a leucoerythroblastic picture and massive splenomegaly ~ Rouleaux formation ~ Smudge cells ~ Auer rods ~ A ~ Marrow fibrosis with extramedullary haemopoiesis produces the film changes, and a dry tap on aspiration with trephine biopsy showing fibrosis confirms it.
M ~ Which renal lesion is characteristic of multiple myeloma, and by what mechanism? ~ Light chain cast nephropathy, with tubular obstruction and direct tubular toxicity ~ Membranous nephropathy from immune complexes ~ Minimal change disease ~ Crescentic glomerulonephritis ~ A ~ Because free light chains are filtered and toxic to tubules, urine dipstick for albumin is often negative while total protein is high, so light chain quantification is needed rather than dipstick alone.
M ~ Which plasma cell disorder presents with a monoclonal IgM, hyperviscosity and lymphadenopathy but without lytic bone lesions? ~ Waldenstrom macroglobulinaemia, a lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma ~ Multiple myeloma ~ Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance ~ Amyloidosis ~ A ~ The large pentameric IgM raises plasma viscosity causing visual disturbance and neurological symptoms, treated urgently by plasmapheresis, and lytic lesions are characteristically absent.
H ~ Why does a patient with essential thrombocythaemia bleed as well as thrombose despite a very high platelet count? ~ The platelets are qualitatively abnormal and extreme counts can cause an acquired von Willebrand syndrome by adsorbing the factor ~ Platelet count is irrelevant to function ~ Fibrinogen is consumed ~ Vitamin K is deficient ~ A ~ This is why aspirin must be used cautiously at very high counts and why cytoreduction rather than antiplatelet therapy alone is used to reduce both risks.
@ Pathology ~ Renal: tubulointerstitial, cystic disease, renal tumours ~ PAT_REN ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. The Kidney
E ~ Which inherited cystic kidney disease is autosomal dominant, presents in adulthood and is associated with berry aneurysms? ~ Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease from PKD1 or PKD2 mutation ~ Autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease ~ Medullary sponge kidney ~ Simple renal cysts ~ A ~ Extrarenal features include hepatic cysts, mitral valve prolapse, colonic diverticula and intracranial aneurysm, and tolvaptan slows the decline in function in rapidly progressive disease.
E ~ Which renal tumour arises from the proximal tubular epithelium, shows clear cells and is associated with VHL inactivation? ~ Clear cell renal cell carcinoma ~ Papillary renal cell carcinoma ~ Chromophobe carcinoma ~ Oncocytoma ~ A ~ The classic triad of haematuria, flank pain and a mass is now uncommon because most are found incidentally, and paraneoplastic polycythaemia, hypercalcaemia and Stauffer syndrome may occur.
E ~ Which renal tumour of childhood shows a triphasic blastemal, epithelial and stromal pattern? ~ Wilms tumour ~ Neuroblastoma ~ Clear cell sarcoma ~ Mesoblastic nephroma ~ A ~ It is associated with WT1 mutations in the WAGR and Denys-Drash syndromes and with WT2 in Beckwith-Wiedemann, and biopsy is avoided to prevent upstaging.
M ~ Which pattern of renal injury follows chronic analgesic abuse, and what is its hallmark? ~ Chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis with papillary necrosis ~ Membranous nephropathy ~ Crescentic glomerulonephritis ~ Amyloidosis ~ A ~ Diabetes, sickle cell disease and obstruction with infection are the other classic causes of papillary necrosis, and the sloughed papilla may cause colic and be seen in the urine.
M ~ Which finding distinguishes acute tubular necrosis from prerenal azotaemia on urine analysis? ~ Muddy brown granular casts with a fractional excretion of sodium above 2 percent and a low urine osmolality ~ Bland urine with a fractional excretion below 1 percent ~ Red cell casts ~ White cell casts with eosinophils ~ A ~ Because injured tubules can neither reabsorb sodium nor concentrate urine, these indices separate the two, though diuretics and chronic kidney disease confound them.
H ~ Why does reflux nephropathy cause hypertension and progressive renal failure years after the childhood infections have ceased? ~ Coarse cortical scarring over deformed calyces reduces nephron mass, and hyperfiltration in the remainder drives progressive glomerulosclerosis ~ Infection persists lifelong ~ Reflux continues into adulthood in all cases ~ Immune complexes deposit continuously ~ A ~ This is the same final common pathway seen in any cause of reduced nephron number, which is why blood pressure control and renin-angiotensin blockade slow progression.
@ Pathology ~ Skin pathology & melanoma ~ PAT_SKN ~ Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease 10e, Ch. The Skin
E ~ Which histological change defines the blister of pemphigus vulgaris? ~ Suprabasal intraepidermal acantholysis with a row of tombstones basal layer ~ Subepidermal blister with linear IgG ~ Intraepidermal spongiosis ~ Subcorneal pustule ~ A ~ Antibodies to desmoglein 3 break cell-cell adhesion so the blister is flaccid and Nikolsky positive, whereas bullous pemphigoid gives tense subepidermal blisters with hemidesmosomal antibodies.
E ~ Which feature on histology of a melanocytic lesion most strongly indicates melanoma? ~ Asymmetry with poor circumscription, upward migration of atypical melanocytes and mitoses in the dermal component ~ Presence of any junctional nests ~ Melanin pigment ~ Solar elastosis ~ A ~ Breslow thickness measured from the granular layer to the deepest tumour cell remains the single most important prognostic factor, with ulceration and mitotic rate adding independently.
E ~ Which skin malignancy shows peripheral palisading of basaloid cells with retraction artefact and almost never metastasises? ~ Basal cell carcinoma ~ Squamous cell carcinoma ~ Melanoma ~ Merkel cell carcinoma ~ A ~ Its local destructiveness rather than metastatic potential determines management, and the sun-exposed head and neck with a rolled pearly edge and telangiectasia is the typical presentation.
M ~ Which precursor lesion progresses to invasive squamous cell carcinoma of the skin, and what defines the transition? ~ Actinic keratosis progressing through Bowen disease, with invasion through the basement membrane defining carcinoma ~ Seborrhoeic keratosis ~ Dermatofibroma ~ Melanocytic naevus ~ A ~ Because the whole sun-damaged field carries risk, field treatment with topical agents or photodynamic therapy is used alongside excision of individual lesions.
M ~ Which melanoma subtype has the best prognosis stage for stage because of its long horizontal growth phase? ~ Lentigo maligna melanoma ~ Nodular melanoma ~ Acral lentiginous melanoma ~ Desmoplastic melanoma ~ A ~ Nodular melanoma lacks a radial growth phase so it invades early and presents at greater thickness, which explains its worse outcome despite a shorter history.
H ~ Why is a mutation in BRAF clinically important in melanoma? ~ About half of cutaneous melanomas carry a V600E mutation that is targetable with BRAF and MEK inhibitors ~ It confers a better prognosis without treatment ~ It predicts response to radiotherapy ~ It excludes immunotherapy ~ A ~ Because checkpoint inhibition is also highly effective, molecular testing at diagnosis of advanced disease determines whether targeted therapy or immunotherapy is used first.
